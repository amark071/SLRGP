# Neural Architecture Search

### Evolutionary and Population-Based NAS

Evolutionary and population-based methods represent one of the earliest and most enduring paradigms in neural architecture search, drawing on bio-inspired optimization to navigate complex, non-differentiable search spaces. Rather than relying on gradient signals, these approaches maintain a population of candidate architectures that are iteratively selected, mutated, and recombined, allowing the search to escape local optima that might trap gradient-based methods.

A prominent strand of this literature explores swarm intelligence as an alternative to classical genetic algorithms. [arxiv_2211.10250] argue that while genetic algorithms have been extensively applied to NAS, other bio-inspired strategies remain underexplored, and propose HiveNAS, which employs Artificial Bee Colony optimization to automate the discovery of network topologies. The authors position their work against the broader observation that traditional neural network development demands substantial expert knowledge and trial-and-error, framing population-based NAS as a means of democratizing architecture design. Complementing this perspective, [arxiv_2403.05123] introduce ECToNAS, an evolutionary cross-topology search framework that performs architecture optimization without requiring any pre-trained meta-controllers. A distinguishing feature of ECToNAS is its capacity for cross-topology optimization, enabling the algorithm to select among fundamentally different network families rather than confining search to a single topology class. By fusing training and topology optimization into a single hybrid loop, ECToNAS is presented as a cost-efficient alternative to controller-dependent evolutionary methods.

A recurring challenge for evolutionary NAS is computational cost, since evaluating each candidate architecture typically requires training to convergence. [arxiv_2003.12857] address this bottleneck through NPENAS, which integrates a neural predictor into the evolutionary loop to estimate architecture performance without exhaustive training. By guiding mutation and selection with surrogate predictions, NPENAS reduces the number of full evaluations needed, offering a practical middle ground between the thoroughness of population-based search and the efficiency demands of large-scale experiments. The authors contrast this predictor-guided strategy with Bayesian optimization, noting that both suffer from scalability issues when applied naively, but that neural predictors can be more tightly coupled with the evolutionary operators.

Hybrid approaches further extend the evolutionary paradigm by combining it with differentiable search components. [arxiv_2204.09320] present SpiderNet, which merges differentiable and evolutionary architecture search and employs train-free metrics to evaluate candidates, thereby circumventing the training cost that burdens conventional evolutionary NAS. Taken together, these works illustrate a clear trajectory in the field: from pure population-based search toward increasingly hybrid frameworks that incorporate predictors, differentiable relaxations, and training-free proxies in order to retain the exploratory advantages of evolutionary methods while substantially reducing their computational overhead.

### Differentiable and Gradient-Based NAS

Differentiable Neural Architecture Search (DNAS) emerged as a response to the fundamental inefficiency of earlier NAS paradigms. Prior approaches grounded in reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms operated over discrete architecture spaces, making the search process computationally prohibitive. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2304.05405], DNAS addressed this bottleneck by reformulating architecture search as a continuous optimization problem, enabling gradient-based updates that are faster by several orders of magnitude than their predecessors. This shift was catalysed largely by the popularity of DARTS, which the survey identifies as one of the first major DNAS methods and the primary driver of the field's rapid growth.

The theoretical motivation for continuous relaxation is articulated clearly in [arxiv_1808.07233], which proposes encoding discrete architecture choices into a continuous, differentiable embedding space. By doing so, the architecture design problem becomes amenable to standard gradient descent, circumventing the need for costly discrete sampling strategies. This formulation demonstrated that automatic architecture design need not be confined to the combinatorial search regimes that had previously dominated the literature.

Building on the continuous relaxation paradigm, subsequent work explored richer structural representations. [arxiv_2007.16112] extended the NAS problem beyond single-path and multi-path search by modelling the search space as a sparse supernet with a mixture of sparsity constraints. This formulation enables automated mixed-path search, offering a more expressive architecture representation while maintaining differentiability through the use of continuous sparsity penalties.

A critical practical concern within gradient-based NAS is the reliability of architecture selection at the conclusion of the search phase. [arxiv_2108.04392] specifically interrogates this issue, observing that in weight-sharing supernets the architecture parameters optimised during search do not always faithfully predict the performance of the derived standalone architectures. The authors argue that jointly optimising model weights and architecture parameters via gradient-based algorithms can introduce a systematic discrepancy between the supernet ranking and true architecture quality, motivating revised selection criteria.

Efficiency through hierarchical structuring of the search space represents another line of development. [arxiv_1909.00122] introduces HM-NAS, which generalises weight-sharing NAS approaches by replacing hand-designed heuristics for architecture generation with a hierarchical masking mechanism. This approach retains the gradient-based efficiency of DNAS while reducing reliance on manually specified inductive biases, thereby broadening the scope of architectures that can be discovered automatically. Taken together, these works illustrate a trajectory in which the core differentiable relaxation idea has been progressively refined to address representational expressiveness, search reliability, and the reduction of human-imposed constraints.
