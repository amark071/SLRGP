# Neural Architecture Search

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

Neural architecture search (NAS) has emerged as a prominent research direction driven by the recognition that architectural choices are critical determinants of deep learning performance, even as the computational demands of discovering such architectures remain substantial [arxiv_1905.01392]. Early NAS methods operated primarily in discrete search spaces, relying on reinforcement learning (RL) or evolutionary algorithms (EA) to navigate combinatorially large candidate sets, a strategy that proved highly inefficient in practice [arxiv_1808.07233]. To address this inefficiency, continuous relaxation approaches were proposed, most notably the neural architecture optimization framework of Luo et al., which embeds discrete architectures into a continuous latent space amenable to gradient-based optimization [arxiv_1808.07233]. However, differentiable methods that convert architecture search into a hyperparameter optimization problem solved by gradient descent have themselves attracted scrutiny, with concerns raised about their effectiveness and generalizability on non-convex architecture landscapes [arxiv_2109.12425]. In response, Mills et al. introduced L²NAS, which reframes the search as a continuous-action reinforcement learning problem, seeking to retain the tractability of continuous representations while avoiding the pitfalls of pure gradient descent [arxiv_2109.12425].

A complementary line of inquiry concerns the interaction between architectural decisions and other hyperparameter settings. Zela et al. demonstrated that treating architecture search and hyperparameter tuning as sequential, independent processes is suboptimal, because the two dimensions interact in ways that can substantially affect final model quality; they further showed that using very few training epochs during the main NAS phase and many more during post-processing introduces a problematic mismatch [arxiv_1807.06906]. This finding motivates joint search strategies that optimize architecture and hyperparameters simultaneously.

Survey-level analyses have sought to organize the rapidly expanding NAS literature into coherent taxonomies. Kim et al. classify dominant approaches into neuro-evolutionary algorithms, RL-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search, providing a structured account of acceleration strategies across these paradigms [arxiv_2108.08474], while Shao's more recent survey focuses specifically on RL-based NAS, reflecting the sustained importance of that sub-field [arxiv_2409.18163]. Scalability remains a persistent concern; Cassimon et al. evaluate a novel RL-based agent trained to search for good architectures rather than return a single optimum, benchmarking it against strong baselines on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 [arxiv_2410.01431]. Finally, Rong et al. address the data and compute constraints that arise when NAS must generalize across tasks, proposing AT-NAS, which combines gradient-based meta-learning with EA-based search to enable across-task architecture discovery under limited resources [arxiv_2110.05842]. Together, these works illustrate a field progressively moving from expensive discrete search toward more efficient, generalizable, and jointly optimized frameworks.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has attracted sustained research attention as a means of automating the design of deep learning models, and a cluster of recent contributions has sought to address persistent limitations in efficiency, search space definition, and the locality of gradient-based methods. A recurring concern in this literature is the computational cost of evaluating candidate architectures. [arxiv_2502.03553] directly confronts this tension between modular and global search strategies, observing that while restricting search to reusable building blocks reduces training overhead, it sacrifices the expressiveness that a truly global search over entire architectures could provide. Their proposed approach attempts to recover the benefits of global search without incurring prohibitive computational expense, situating itself within a broader trend of making NAS practically deployable.

The definition of the search space itself has received dedicated treatment in [arxiv_2310.17669], which argues that poorly constructed search spaces are a primary bottleneck in NAS pipelines. The authors propose a systematic methodology for search space definition, emphasising that domain knowledge should be encoded structurally rather than left to the optimiser to discover implicitly. This concern with search space quality complements the efficiency-oriented perspective of [arxiv_2502.03553], since even an efficient search algorithm will underperform if the space it explores is ill-defined or excessively large.

Within the dominant paradigm of Differentiable Neural Architecture Search (DNAS), [arxiv_2104.10450] identifies a fundamental weakness: the tendency of DARTS-based methods to converge to locally optimal but globally poor architectures, a phenomenon the authors term performance collapse. They argue that existing remedies address symptoms rather than causes, and propose modifications that reduce the locality of the search by encouraging exploration of a broader region of the architecture space. This critique resonates with the general observation, echoed across the NAS literature, that gradient-based relaxations introduce optimisation biases that are not always aligned with downstream task performance.

Taken together, these three contributions illuminate complementary dimensions of the NAS problem. [arxiv_2310.17669] addresses the upstream challenge of space construction, [arxiv_2104.10450] targets the optimisation dynamics within differentiable frameworks, and [arxiv_2502.03553] tackles the downstream question of how search scope affects both cost and solution quality. Collectively, they reflect a maturing field in which researchers are moving beyond demonstrating that automated architecture discovery is feasible and are instead interrogating the structural assumptions that govern where and how the search is conducted.
