# Neural Architecture Search

### Reinforcement Learning-Based NAS

#### RL-Based NAS: Core Methods and Algorithms

Reinforcement learning-based neural architecture search (RL-based NAS) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for automating the design of deep neural networks, with a growing body of work focused on refining the core methods and algorithms that underpin this approach. At its foundation, RL-based NAS frames the architecture design problem as a sequential decision-making task, wherein an agent—typically a controller network—receives rewards based on the validation performance of sampled architectures and updates its policy accordingly. This formulation, while theoretically powerful, introduces substantial practical challenges related to computational cost, search space design, and scalability.

One of the most significant limitations of conventional RL-based NAS is its demanding resource requirements. As noted by [arxiv_2105.11694], RL-based NAS generally guarantees better convergence than gradient-based alternatives but suffers from a rollout bottleneck, whereby each sampled architecture must be exhaustively trained on proxy tasks before a reward signal can be computed. To address this, [arxiv_2105.11694] proposes a general pipeline that accelerates convergence by reducing the cost associated with individual rollout evaluations. Complementarily, [arxiv_2110.06751] approaches the same problem from the angle of sample efficiency, arguing that the number of architecture evaluations required by standard RL-based controllers can be substantially reduced through more informed search strategies, thereby making the overall process more tractable.

The structure of the search space itself is another critical algorithmic consideration. Traditional RL-based NAS operates over discrete, high-dimensional spaces that are inherently difficult to optimize. [arxiv_1909.03615] addresses this by proposing a search in a continuous embedding space, enabling smoother optimization landscapes and more effective exploration. This idea of continuity is further developed by [arxiv_2109.12425], which introduces a continuous-action reinforcement learning framework for architecture search. Importantly, [arxiv_2109.12425] critiques differentiable architecture search methods, arguing that gradient descent may be ill-suited to non-convex architecture optimization problems, and positions continuous-action RL as a more robust alternative that retains the flexibility of RL while operating over a smoother action space.

Beyond individual architectural decisions, scalability of the RL agent itself has received increasing attention. [arxiv_2410.01431] evaluates a novel RL-based solution designed not merely to return a single optimal architecture but to learn a generalizable search policy across architecture spaces. Benchmarked against strong baselines on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301, this work highlights the importance of designing agents capable of operating effectively across diverse search settings, pointing toward a broader vision of RL-based NAS as a scalable, reusable methodology rather than a problem-specific tool.

#### Improving RL-NAS Efficiency and Reducing Computational Cost

Early reinforcement learning-based NAS methods demonstrated strong convergence properties but imposed prohibitive computational demands, largely due to the rollout bottleneck in which each sampled architecture must be exhaustively trained on proxy tasks before a reward signal can be returned to the controller [arxiv_2105.11694]. This fundamental inefficiency motivated a sustained research effort aimed at reducing the cost of RL-NAS without sacrificing the quality of discovered architectures.

One prominent direction involves improving the sample efficiency of the RL controller itself. Nagy and Boros [arxiv_2110.06751] directly target this problem, arguing that the number of architecture evaluations required before convergence can be substantially reduced through more informed exploration strategies. Their work situates itself within a broader trend identified by Kim et al. [arxiv_2108.08474], who classify RL-based algorithms as one of three principal NAS paradigms and highlight acceleration as the central open challenge across all of them.

A complementary strategy focuses on the initialization and pretraining of NAS controllers rather than on the search dynamics themselves. Choi et al. [arxiv_2103.08157] propose a locality-based self-supervised pretraining scheme that exploits structural similarities among candidate architectures to warm-start the controller, thereby reducing the number of costly evaluations needed during the main search phase. This approach is notable because it is designed to be agnostic to the specific RL algorithm used, making it broadly applicable across controller-based NAS frameworks.

Liu et al. [arxiv_2105.11694] address the rollout bottleneck more directly by introducing uncertainty-aware mechanisms that allow the controller to skip or truncate evaluations for architectures whose performance can be predicted with sufficient confidence, accelerating convergence without requiring a full proxy-task training run for every candidate. This uncertainty-driven pruning of the evaluation budget represents a different trade-off from pretraining approaches, prioritising runtime reduction over improved initialisation.

More recent work has examined scalability as a distinct dimension of efficiency. Cassimon et al. [arxiv_2410.01431] evaluate a novel RL-based NAS agent on both NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 benchmarks, comparing it against strong baselines and demonstrating that the agent can learn to search effectively across increasingly complex search spaces, suggesting that scalability and computational tractability need not be mutually exclusive goals. Meanwhile, Mills et al. [arxiv_2109.12425] reframe the search problem using continuous-action RL, arguing that gradient-based differentiable methods face non-convexity issues that continuous-action RL can sidestep, thereby offering an efficiency advantage over both classical discrete RL controllers and gradient-based alternatives.

Taken together, these contributions reflect a multi-pronged effort to make RL-NAS computationally viable: through uncertainty-aware rollout reduction [arxiv_2105.11694], improved sample efficiency [arxiv_2110.06751], controller pretraining [arxiv_2103.08157], scalable agent design [arxiv_2410.01431], and continuous-action reformulations [arxiv_2109.12425].

### Evolutionary and Genetic Algorithm-Based NAS

#### Search Efficiency and Acceleration in Evolutionary NAS

The computational cost of evaluating candidate architectures represents one of the most significant bottlenecks in evolutionary neural architecture search (ENAS), and researchers have pursued several complementary strategies to address this challenge.

A foundational acceleration technique involves replacing full training-from-scratch evaluations with a supernet that shares weights across all architectures in the search space. Both [arxiv_2204.00188] and [arxiv_2203.01559] identify the same core problem: vanilla EA-based NAS requires training each candidate architecture independently, which is extremely time-consuming, and both works propose leveraging a supernet to estimate architectural fitness more efficiently through weight sharing. However, both studies also acknowledge a shared limitation of this approach — the estimated fitness is noisy due to the co-adaptation of operations within the supernet, meaning that the inherited weights may not faithfully represent the true standalone performance of any given architecture. This tension between efficiency and estimation fidelity is a recurring theme in the supernet-based acceleration literature, and it motivates the development of complementary or alternative strategies.

A distinct but related line of work addresses acceleration through smarter sampling and weight inheritance during the evolutionary process itself. [arxiv_2003.11613] proposes sampled training combined with node inheritance as mechanisms to reduce the evaluation overhead in ENAS. Rather than relying on a monolithic supernet, this approach focuses on how training samples are selected and how architectural components can inherit parameters from parent networks, thereby avoiding redundant computation across generations. This strategy directly targets the extreme computational cost that [arxiv_2003.11613] identifies as a defining challenge for ENAS, and it complements the supernet paradigm by operating at the level of the evolutionary operators rather than the weight-sharing infrastructure.

A more recent direction replaces direct fitness evaluation altogether with learned performance predictors. [arxiv_2411.14498] frames the problem in terms of the growing complexity of search spaces and the need to balance fine-grained accuracy against coarse-grained computational efficiency. By encoding architectural differences rather than absolute architectural representations, the predictor-based approach described in [arxiv_2411.14498] aims to capture relational information between architectures, which can improve sample efficiency in training the surrogate model. This contrasts with supernet-based methods [arxiv_2204.00188, arxiv_2203.01559], which amortize cost through weight sharing rather than through surrogate modeling. Collectively, these three strategies — supernet weight sharing, sampling and inheritance, and predictor-based surrogates — represent the principal axes along which the field has sought to make evolutionary NAS computationally tractable without sacrificing search quality.

#### Multi-Objective Evolutionary NAS

Multi-objective evolutionary neural architecture search (NAS) has emerged as a prominent research direction in response to the limitations of single-objective approaches, which optimize solely for predictive accuracy while neglecting practical deployment constraints such as computational cost, memory footprint, and inference latency. The foundational motivation for this paradigm shift is well articulated in [arxiv_2307.09099], which surveys how early NAS efforts focused exclusively on accuracy before the field broadened to simultaneously optimize multiple, often conflicting, objectives. This transition reflects the real-world requirement that deployed models balance performance against resource efficiency.

A landmark contribution to this area is NSGA-Net [arxiv_1810.03522], which adapts the well-established NSGA-II multi-objective genetic algorithm to NAS. NSGA-Net explicitly targets three design goals: handling conflicting objectives, balancing exploration and exploitation of the architecture search space, and producing a diverse Pareto-optimal set of architectures. This work established a template that subsequent research has refined and extended. Building on similar principles, [arxiv_2306.02648] proposes a Cartesian genetic programming representation combined with multi-objective evolutionary algorithms, demonstrating that the choice of architecture encoding can substantially influence the diversity and quality of discovered solutions.

Hardware-aware variants of multi-objective evolutionary NAS have attracted considerable attention. The SONATA framework [arxiv_2402.13204] introduces a self-adaptive evolutionary strategy tailored for IoT deployment scenarios, where balancing accuracy against hardware constraints is critical. Similarly, [arxiv_2101.11883] extends multi-objective evolutionary NAS to support approximate multipliers, targeting energy-efficient inference by co-optimizing network error and hardware resource usage simultaneously. The OFA² framework [arxiv_2303.13683] revisits the Once-for-All paradigm through a multi-objective lens, decoupling training from search to enable efficient Pareto-front exploration across devices with heterogeneous resource budgets. Complementing these efforts, [arxiv_2406.00291] addresses scalability by learning to partition the search space, reducing the computational burden of applying multi-objective optimization to large NAS problems.

Diversity and coverage of the Pareto front remain persistent challenges. [arxiv_2208.00204] proposes quality diversity optimization as an alternative to classical multi-objective NAS, arguing that standard Pareto-based methods may insufficiently explore the architecture space. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2407.15600] introduces a pairwise comparison relation-assisted approach with a multi-population mechanism, aiming to improve evaluation efficiency while maintaining solution diversity. Collectively, these works reveal a field actively negotiating the tension between search efficiency, solution diversity, and hardware fidelity, with evolutionary and genetic algorithm-based methods remaining central to advancing the state of the art in multi-objective NAS.
