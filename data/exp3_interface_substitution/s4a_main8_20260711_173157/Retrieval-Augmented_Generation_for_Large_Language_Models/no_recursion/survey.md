# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential architectural innovations in natural language processing, and the volume of survey literature dedicated to it reflects its rapid adoption. At its core, RAG addresses well-documented limitations of large language models (LLMs), including hallucination, static parametric knowledge, and insufficient domain-specific expertise, by coupling generative models with dynamically retrieved external information [arxiv_2407.13193]. The foundational formulation of this paradigm was established by Lewis et al., who demonstrated that pre-trained language models, despite storing substantial factual knowledge in their parameters, lag behind task-specific architectures on knowledge-intensive tasks, and proposed a retrieval-augmented framework that retrieves relevant documents at inference time to condition generation [arxiv_2005.11401]. This seminal contribution set the conceptual template that subsequent surveys have sought to systematize and extend.

Several broad surveys have since mapped the evolving RAG landscape from complementary angles. Gupta et al. trace the evolution of RAG from its foundational concepts through to the current state of the art, examining how retrieval mechanisms and generative components are integrated and identifying future research directions [arxiv_2410.12837]. Similarly, Fan et al. situate RAG within the wider context of AI-generated content, arguing that retrieval's capacity to supply up-to-date external knowledge makes RAG an indispensable complement to generative AI systems [arxiv_2405.06211]. Huang and Huang focus specifically on the text domain, framing RAG as a cost-effective remedy for the generation of plausible but factually incorrect responses, and emphasising the dynamic integration of current information as a key advantage over purely parametric approaches [arxiv_2404.10981].

More specialised surveys have refined this picture by foregrounding particular dimensions of RAG. Cheng et al. adopt a knowledge-oriented perspective, examining how RAG leverages heterogeneous external sources—including documents, databases, and structured data—to improve contextual accuracy [arxiv_2503.10677], a concern that resonates with benchmark work showing that existing RAG evaluation has been overly focused on unstructured text retrieval while neglecting tabular and graph-structured knowledge [arxiv_2505.11180]. The workshop overview by Wang et al. further underscores that, despite RAG's prominence in enhancing LLM-based applications, comprehensive application has exposed persistent problems and limitations that the community is only beginning to address systematically [arxiv_2410.20598].

Taken together, these surveys converge on a shared diagnosis: while RAG substantially mitigates hallucination and knowledge-currency problems, the interaction between retrieval and generation components remains incompletely understood, and evaluation methodologies have not kept pace with architectural complexity. This consensus motivates the more targeted investigations into retrieval quality, component integration, and mechanistic behaviour reviewed in subsequent subsections.

### Retrieval Quality and Document Selection Improvements

Effective retrieval is foundational to the performance of retrieval-augmented generation systems, yet several interconnected challenges — including document relevance scoring, context window constraints, and the semantic gap between retrievers and generators — continue to limit the quality of retrieved content passed to large language models.

A central concern in document selection is the efficient use of the context window. Because context windows are typically limited to several thousand tokens, indiscriminate retrieval risks occupying available space with passages that contribute little informational value to the model's response. To address this, Pickett and colleagues propose evaluating retrieved passages according to their relevant information gain, prioritising content that meaningfully extends what the model already knows rather than simply matching surface-level query terms [arxiv_2407.12101]. This framing shifts the selection criterion from raw similarity to marginal utility, offering a more principled basis for deciding which passages deserve inclusion.

Complementing this concern over selection quality is the challenge of dynamic relevance across multi-step or complex queries. Hei, Liu, and colleagues observe that accessing large language models multiple times per query is inefficient, while retrieving all potentially relevant documents indiscriminately is unreliable [arxiv_2406.07348]. Their DR-RAG framework applies dynamic document relevance scoring to question-answering tasks, adjusting which documents are considered relevant as the query context evolves, thereby improving both efficiency and response accuracy in knowledge-intensive settings.

Even when relevant documents are successfully retrieved, a further structural problem arises from the semantic gap between retrievers and generators. Because retrievers and large language models differ in their training objectives and architectures, language models are often forced to passively accept documents that may be incomprehensible or misaligned with their generative expectations [arxiv_2406.13249]. The R²AG framework proposed by Ye and colleagues addresses this misalignment by incorporating retrieval-derived information directly into the generation process, enabling the language model to better interpret and utilise the documents it receives.

Taken together, these contributions highlight that retrieval quality in RAG systems is not reducible to a single problem. Broader comparative analyses reinforce this point: traditional retrieval models such as BM25 and Dense Passage Retrieval efficiently handle large corpora but often lack semantic depth, whereas generative models offer greater flexibility at the cost of grounding [arxiv_2502.20245]. The papers reviewed here collectively suggest that robust document selection requires advances on multiple fronts simultaneously — relevance scoring, dynamic adaptation, and architectural alignment between retrieval and generation components.
