# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a foundational paradigm for addressing core limitations of large language models, and the literature surveyed in this top rank slab reflects both the origins of the approach and its rapid maturation. The seminal contribution by Lewis et al. [arxiv_2005.11401] established the conceptual and technical basis for RAG by demonstrating that pre-trained language models, despite storing substantial factual knowledge in their parameters, remain limited in their ability to access and precisely manipulate that knowledge on knowledge-intensive tasks. Their framework combined a parametric memory component with a non-parametric retrieval module, yielding state-of-the-art results and providing a template that subsequent work has extensively refined.

Building on this foundation, survey-oriented contributions have sought to systematize the field. Wu et al. [arxiv_2407.13193] offer a broad survey of RAG for natural language processing, cataloguing how the retrieval of external knowledge mitigates hallucination, addresses knowledge staleness, and supplies domain-specific expertise that model parameters alone cannot reliably encode. Similarly, Huang and Huang [arxiv_2404.10981] frame RAG as a cost-effective remedy for the static limitations of LLMs, emphasizing its capacity to integrate up-to-date information dynamically and reduce the generation of plausible but factually incorrect responses. Together, these surveys situate RAG within the wider landscape of deep learning and information retrieval, while the workshop proceedings of Wang et al. [arxiv_2410.20598] signal the community's recognition that, as RAG applications proliferate, new problems and limitations demand systematic attention.

A recurring theme across the higher-ranked papers is the semantic gap between retrievers and generators. Ye et al. [arxiv_2406.13249] identify a structural misalignment arising from differences in training objectives and architectures, which forces language models to passively accept documents that may be incomprehensible or irrelevant to the generation task; their R²AG system incorporates retrieval-side information directly into the generation process to bridge this gap. Complementing this, Hei et al. [arxiv_2406.07348] address the inefficiency of repeated LLM calls and the unreliability of retrieving all relevant documents simultaneously, proposing a dynamic document relevance mechanism within their DR-RAG framework.

At the document-processing level, Hwang et al. [arxiv_2407.03627] argue that retrieved documents frequently contain noise that degrades generation quality, and propose sentence-level re-ranking and reconstruction to refine documents before they reach the generator. This concern with pipeline configuration is echoed empirically by Li et al. [arxiv_2501.07391], whose systematic study of RAG best practices reveals that the influence of individual components and their configurations remains underexplored, underscoring the need for principled evaluation. Collectively, these eight works trace a coherent trajectory from RAG's foundational formulation through survey-level synthesis to targeted architectural and procedural innovations aimed at improving retrieval fidelity, semantic alignment, and generation reliability.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Scholarship at this tier of the RAG literature reflects a productive tension between two broad research agendas: improving how models are trained to exploit retrieved content, and rethinking the architectural assumptions that underpin retrieval itself. A recurring concern is that standard language model training does not equip models to handle retrieved passages reliably. [arxiv_2402.18150] addresses this directly, proposing an unsupervised information-refinement training regime that teaches LLMs to utilise retrieved texts with greater fidelity, arguing that without such targeted training models frequently ignore or are misled by retrieved content. This diagnosis is echoed by [arxiv_2502.10596], which advocates post-training on self-generated demonstrations as a practical means of aligning a model's generation behaviour with the demands of retrieval-conditioned inference, particularly for knowledge-intensive tasks where pre-training knowledge is insufficient or outdated. Together, these two works converge on the view that retrieval alone is insufficient; the generation model must be explicitly trained to integrate external evidence, though they differ in whether supervision is derived from external annotation pipelines or from the model's own outputs.

A complementary perspective is offered by survey-oriented contributions that situate these training challenges within the broader landscape of RAG research. [arxiv_2405.06211] provides a comprehensive mapping of how RAG intersects with the capabilities of large language models across diverse task types, framing retrieval augmentation as a mechanism for supplying reliable and up-to-date knowledge in the era of AI-generated content. This macro-level view contextualises the more targeted interventions proposed elsewhere in the literature, highlighting that the field spans retrieval design, knowledge fusion, and downstream application simultaneously.

What unites much of this work is an implicit assumption that retrieval from an external corpus is the appropriate architectural choice. However, this assumption is not universally shared within the slab. [arxiv_2501.15915] introduces parametric retrieval augmented generation, which internalises knowledge retrieval within model parameters rather than appending documents to the input context, offering an alternative that sidesteps some of the latency and error-propagation problems associated with pipeline-based retrieval. This parametric framing resonates with arguments made elsewhere in the broader literature about the costs of retrieval latency and document-selection errors, though [arxiv_2501.15915] pursues a fundamentally different resolution by relocating knowledge storage rather than refining the retrieval pipeline itself. Taken together, the papers in this cluster reveal that the field is simultaneously maturing its training methodologies for conventional RAG while also questioning whether the retrieval-then-generate pipeline is the only viable paradigm for knowledge-grounded generation.
