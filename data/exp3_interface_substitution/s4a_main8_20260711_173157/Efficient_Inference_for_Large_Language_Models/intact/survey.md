# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated a substantial body of survey literature dedicated to understanding and mitigating the computational burdens associated with their inference. As these models have grown in scale and capability, the gap between their theoretical promise and practical deployability has widened, motivating researchers to systematically catalogue the techniques available for efficient inference.

Among the most comprehensive treatments of this topic, Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] provide a dedicated survey on efficient inference for LLMs, directly addressing the substantial computational and memory requirements that make deployment in resource-constrained scenarios particularly challenging. Their work organizes the landscape of efficiency techniques into a coherent taxonomy, offering a structured view of how the field has responded to the inference bottleneck. Similarly, Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] approach the problem from an algorithmic perspective, surveying model compression and efficient inference methods together, thereby situating inference optimization within the broader context of model compression strategies such as quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation. By treating compression and inference efficiency as interrelated concerns, Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] highlight the degree to which deployment constraints shape algorithmic design choices upstream.

Complementing these focused surveys, Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] offer a broader overview that spans the full pipeline from training to inference, situating efficiency concerns within the wider trajectory of LLM development. Their work notes that the introduction of systems such as ChatGPT has intensified interest in cost-efficient training and deployment, framing low-cost inference not merely as a technical desideratum but as a defining trend for the future of the field. This broader framing is useful for contextualizing the more narrowly scoped surveys, as it connects inference efficiency to the economic and practical realities of large-scale model adoption.

Donisch et al. [arxiv_2408.03130] adopt a somewhat different orientation, focusing on the practical considerations and real-world challenges that accompany inference optimization techniques. Their literature review emphasizes the effects and trade-offs of various resource-reduction methods, acknowledging that techniques which appear promising in isolation may introduce new complications when deployed at scale. This practitioner-oriented perspective complements the more taxonomic approaches of [arxiv_2404.14294] and [arxiv_2402.09748], together suggesting that efficient LLM inference is a multidimensional challenge requiring both algorithmic innovation and careful engineering judgment. Collectively, these surveys establish a rich intellectual foundation for understanding the state of the art in efficient LLM inference.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The compression of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a central research priority, driven by the substantial memory and computational demands that constrain their practical deployment. Three principal techniques — pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation — have been extensively studied, both in isolation and in combination, as means of reducing model size while preserving task performance.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) has attracted particular attention because it requires no retraining of the full model. However, as [arxiv_2408.08554] demonstrates, PTQ deployment faces significant challenges at low bit-widths, including accuracy degradation and hardware incompatibility, motivating the development of arbitrary-bit quantization schemes capable of accelerating inference across diverse hardware targets. Complementing this line of work, [arxiv_2305.14152] addresses the intersection of quantization and fine-tuning, showing that sub-4-bit integer quantization can be combined with parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods to reduce both the memory footprint of pre-trained weights and the optimizer-state overhead incurred during adaptation. Together, these studies illustrate that quantization must be considered not only at the inference stage but also throughout the fine-tuning pipeline.

Pruning offers a structurally different route to compression by identifying and removing redundant weights or attention heads. [arxiv_2409.17372] situates pruning alongside quantization and distillation within a unified perspective, arguing that the effectiveness of all three techniques ultimately reflects the substantial redundancy present in LLM parameterizations. Crucially, however, [arxiv_2409.17372] also observes that most compression methods focus narrowly on weight optimization, leaving complementary dimensions of efficiency — such as architectural search — underexplored.

Knowledge distillation transfers the predictive behaviour of a large teacher model into a smaller student, enabling inference acceleration without necessarily altering numerical precision or sparsity. [arxiv_2211.07715] demonstrates this approach in the context of BERT-family models, combining distillation with quantization and pruning to meet the strict latency constraints of CPU-based production environments, thereby showing that hybrid compression pipelines can be practically effective. The theoretical motivation for distillation is further reinforced by [arxiv_2002.11794], which establishes that training larger models and subsequently compressing them can yield better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs than training small models directly — a finding that reframes compression as an integral component of the training strategy rather than a post-hoc remediation.

Taken together, these works reveal a converging consensus: no single compression technique is universally sufficient, and the most effective approaches tend to combine pruning, quantization, and distillation in ways that are sensitive to both the target hardware and the deployment scenario.
