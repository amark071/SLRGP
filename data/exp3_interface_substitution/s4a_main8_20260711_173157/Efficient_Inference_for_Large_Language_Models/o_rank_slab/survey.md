# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently has generated a rich body of research spanning compression techniques, hardware-aware optimization, and novel decoding strategies. At the broadest level, Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] provide a comprehensive survey of compression and efficient inference methods for LLMs, cataloguing approaches such as quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation from an algorithmic perspective. This taxonomic work establishes the conceptual scaffolding within which more targeted contributions can be situated.

A recurring theme in the literature is the tension between model size and deployment feasibility on resource-constrained hardware. Shen et al. [arxiv_2311.00502] address this directly by proposing an inference framework optimized for CPUs, arguing that the astronomical parameter counts of modern LLMs impose prohibitive demands on memory capacity and bandwidth. Their earlier work on DistilBERT [arxiv_2211.07715] similarly targeted CPU throughput, demonstrating that combined quantization and pruning can satisfy latency constraints that would otherwise prevent transformer models from reaching production. Together, these two contributions trace an evolution from compressing encoder-only models toward tackling the more demanding generative LLM setting, with CPU deployment as a consistent practical motivation.

Weight-level compression, however, is not the only axis of optimization. Shen et al. [arxiv_2409.17372] argue that most compression techniques concentrate narrowly on weight optimization while neglecting architectural search, and propose methods that explore structural redundancy more broadly. This perspective complements quantization-centric approaches by suggesting that efficiency gains are available at multiple levels of model design simultaneously.

Quantization itself continues to attract dedicated attention. Zeng et al. [arxiv_2408.08554] introduce ABQ-LLM, a post-training quantization framework supporting arbitrary bit-widths, addressing two practical obstacles: the accuracy degradation associated with low-bit representations and the difficulty of deploying mixed-precision configurations on real hardware. Post-training quantization is positioned as particularly attractive because it avoids the cost of retraining, a consideration that grows more pressing as model scale increases.

Beyond compression, researchers have explored inference-time algorithmic innovations. Wu and Tu [arxiv_2405.10637] target the key-value cache, which consumes substantial memory in deep transformer models, proposing a layer-condensed caching scheme that reduces this footprint without retraining. Speculative decoding represents another inference-time strategy: Chen et al. [arxiv_2503.15921] use a small speculative model to generate candidate tokens that a larger model then verifies, while Xu et al. [arxiv_2504.08850] combine speculation with early exiting, allowing tokens to bypass later layers when confidence is sufficiently high. These two approaches share the intuition that not every token requires the full computational depth of the model, though they differ in whether redundancy is exploited across model layers or across the draft-verify pipeline. Collectively, this body of work illustrates that efficient LLM inference is a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated advances in compression, memory management, and decoding algorithms.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Research into efficient inference for large language models has produced a rich body of work spanning algorithmic innovations, hardware co-design, and comprehensive taxonomic surveys. Among the most broadly scoped contributions in this area, [arxiv_2404.14294] offers a systematic survey of techniques aimed at reducing the substantial computational and memory requirements that characterize LLM inference, organizing efforts across model compression, hardware-aware optimization, and decoding strategies. This breadth of coverage makes it a natural reference point against which more specialized contributions can be situated.

A recurring theme across the literature is the memory bottleneck introduced by the key-value (KV) cache, whose size grows with context length and imposes severe constraints on throughput and scalability. [arxiv_2508.06297] addresses this problem directly, reviewing KV cache compression strategies as a primary lever for improving inference efficiency, arguing that as context windows continue to expand, cache optimization becomes indispensable rather than merely beneficial. This concern is echoed at the hardware level by [arxiv_2503.18869], which proposes a compression-aware memory controller design that alleviates bandwidth and capacity demands without relying solely on model-level approximations such as pruning or quantization. The complementarity of these two works is notable: where [arxiv_2508.06297] focuses on algorithmic compression of the cache, [arxiv_2503.18869] targets the memory subsystem architecture itself, suggesting that software and hardware solutions must co-evolve.

Decoding efficiency represents another active front. [arxiv_2309.08168] introduces self-speculative decoding, a lossless acceleration scheme that avoids the need for a separate draft model by selectively skipping intermediate layers during a fast drafting stage and then verifying candidate tokens with the full model. This approach is particularly appealing because it preserves output quality while reducing latency, distinguishing it from lossy compression methods reviewed in [arxiv_2404.14294] and [arxiv_2508.06297]. The self-contained nature of self-speculative decoding also contrasts with systems that require auxiliary components or specialized hardware.

Taken together, these works illustrate the multi-dimensional nature of the inference efficiency problem. Survey contributions such as [arxiv_2404.14294] provide the taxonomic scaffolding within which targeted innovations—whether cache compression [arxiv_2508.06297], memory controller redesign [arxiv_2503.18869], or novel decoding paradigms [arxiv_2309.08168]—can be understood and compared. The convergence of algorithmic and architectural approaches across these studies underscores that no single technique is sufficient; rather, practical deployment of large language models demands coordinated progress across the full inference stack.
