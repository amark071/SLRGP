# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models has generated substantial scholarly interest in making their inference pipelines more computationally tractable, prompting several comprehensive surveys that map the landscape of efficiency-oriented techniques. These overviews collectively establish that the central bottleneck in LLM deployment is the tension between model capability and the memory and computational resources required at inference time.

Among the broadest treatments of the subject, Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] trace the full arc from training to inference, situating cost-efficient deployment as the defining trend for future LLM development. Their review contextualises inference optimisation within the wider evolution of model training paradigms, offering a longitudinal perspective that complements more narrowly focused surveys. Similarly broad in scope, Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] approach the problem from an explicitly algorithmic standpoint, examining compression and efficient inference methods together and arguing that the significant memory and computational costs incurred during inference make deployment on resource-constrained devices a first-order research challenge. By treating compression and inference acceleration as intertwined rather than separate concerns, their taxonomy encourages researchers to consider joint optimisation strategies.

Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] offer a survey oriented specifically toward inference efficiency, cataloguing techniques aimed at reducing the computational and memory demands that arise from the scale of modern LLMs. Their work provides a structured taxonomy of inference-time methods, making it a useful reference for situating individual algorithmic contributions within a coherent framework. A distinguishing feature of the survey by Yuan et al. [arxiv_2402.16363] is its integration of roofline model analysis alongside the conventional literature review format. Rather than merely enumerating methods, Yuan et al. argue that prior surveys lack a concise analytical framework capable of explaining why particular techniques yield performance gains, and they propose roofline-based reasoning as a principled lens for understanding compute- versus memory-bound regimes in LLM inference. This methodological contribution sets their work apart from the more descriptive approaches of [arxiv_2401.02038], [arxiv_2402.09748], and [arxiv_2404.14294].

Taken together, these surveys converge on several shared conclusions: that autoregressive decoding is inherently sequential and therefore difficult to parallelise, that model size creates prohibitive memory bandwidth demands, and that no single technique is sufficient to address all efficiency dimensions simultaneously. They also collectively signal that the field is evolving rapidly, with new methods emerging faster than any single overview can fully capture. This dynamism underscores the value of the roofline framework proposed by [arxiv_2402.16363] as a stable analytical foundation, even as specific algorithmic proposals continue to proliferate. The surveys thus serve not only as reference documents but as conceptual scaffolding for the more specialised lines of work—spanning speculative decoding, quantisation, pruning, and system-level optimisation—that subsequent research has pursued.

### KV Cache Optimization and Compression

The key-value (KV) cache, which stores intermediate attention states to avoid redundant computation during autoregressive decoding, has emerged as a central bottleneck in large language model (LLM) inference. As context lengths grow, KV cache memory consumption scales linearly with sequence length, creating severe constraints on throughput and deployability. A recent comprehensive review by [arxiv_2508.06297] surveys the landscape of KV cache compression techniques, noting that the exponential growth in caching demand has motivated a broad range of optimization strategies aimed at enhancing both memory efficiency and inference scalability. This taxonomic work provides a useful framework for situating more targeted algorithmic contributions.

One prominent direction involves reducing the number of layers that maintain full KV caches. [arxiv_2405.10637] propose a Layer-Condensed KV Cache approach in which only a small subset of layers retain their own key-value representations, while the remaining layers reuse cached states from selected upper layers. This design directly addresses the observation that, in deep transformer models, the per-layer memory overhead compounds substantially, and that not all layers contribute equally to representational fidelity. By condensing the cache across layers, the method achieves significant memory savings without requiring changes to the attention mechanism itself.

A complementary strategy focuses on token-level eviction rather than layer-level reduction. [arxiv_2404.15949] introduce CORM, a cache optimization method that prioritizes retaining recent messages in the KV cache. The underlying intuition is that attention patterns in LLMs tend to concentrate on proximate tokens, making older cache entries candidates for eviction. CORM thus reduces memory pressure dynamically during inference by selectively discarding entries deemed less relevant based on recency, offering a lightweight alternative to more computationally expensive importance-scoring schemes.

Sparsity offers yet another axis of optimization. [arxiv_2403.17312] present ALISA, which exploits the inherent sparsity of attention weights to accelerate inference. Rather than compressing the cache through eviction or layer sharing, ALISA identifies and skips computations associated with near-zero attention scores, reducing both memory bandwidth and arithmetic cost. Compared to the recency-based approach of [arxiv_2404.15949], ALISA's sparsity-aware mechanism is more dynamic and input-dependent, though it introduces additional overhead for sparsity detection.

Taken together, these works illustrate that KV cache optimization can be pursued along multiple orthogonal dimensions—layer consolidation [arxiv_2405.10637], token eviction [arxiv_2404.15949], and sparsity exploitation [arxiv_2403.17312]—each with distinct trade-offs in memory savings, computational overhead, and implementation complexity, as the broader survey in [arxiv_2508.06297] underscores.
