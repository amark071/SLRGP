# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated a corresponding body of scholarship dedicated to understanding and mitigating the substantial computational and memory demands that arise during inference. Several surveys and overviews have emerged to synthesize this growing literature, each approaching the problem from a somewhat distinct angle while converging on a shared set of concerns.

Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] provide a broad algorithmic treatment of compression and efficient inference for transformer-based LLMs, motivated by the observation that significant memory and computational costs incurred during inference make deployment on resource-constrained devices particularly challenging. Their survey organizes the field through a taxonomic lens, examining how various algorithmic strategies can reduce these overheads without sacrificing the remarkable capabilities that have made LLMs so widely adopted. Similarly, Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] survey efficient inference techniques with explicit attention to the dual pressures of computational and memory requirements, framing the problem as one of enabling deployment in resource-constrained scenarios. While both surveys share a concern with the practical barriers to LLM deployment, Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] place particular emphasis on cataloguing the range of techniques that the research community has directed toward inference efficiency, offering a comprehensive mapping of the solution space.

Complementing these algorithmic perspectives, Donisch et al. [arxiv_2408.03130] adopt a more practitioner-oriented stance, reviewing inference optimizations with attention to their effects, challenges, and practical considerations. Their work acknowledges that LLMs have become ubiquitous in natural language processing precisely because of their ability to adapt to new tasks without retraining, yet this same scale and complexity introduces unique deployment challenges. This focus on real-world applicability distinguishes their contribution from the more theoretically organized surveys of Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] and Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294].

At a more foundational level, Mei et al. [arxiv_2405.11704] offer a theoretical analysis of the internal structure and operational mechanisms of large-scale language models, examining how transformer architectures and their derivatives constrain computing efficiency when capturing long-term dependencies. Their investigation into efficiency bottlenecks extends into the training phase and evaluates the role of adaptive optimization algorithms, providing a mechanistic grounding that complements the higher-level taxonomies found in the broader surveys. Together, these works collectively establish that efficient LLM inference is a multifaceted challenge requiring coordinated attention to algorithmic design, architectural properties, and deployment practicalities.

### KV Cache Optimization and Management

The key-value (KV) cache has emerged as one of the central bottlenecks in deploying large language models (LLMs) at scale. During autoregressive generation, intermediate attention states must be stored and reused across decoding steps, causing memory consumption to grow linearly with both sequence length and batch size [arxiv_2406.19707]. As context windows continue to expand, this growth becomes exponential in practical terms, fundamentally limiting inference throughput and scalability [arxiv_2508.06297]. Addressing this challenge has motivated a diverse body of research spanning compression strategies, eviction policies, and architectural redesigns of the cache itself.

One prominent line of work focuses on dynamic management of the KV cache at runtime. InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] proposes a framework that selectively retains and offloads KV entries based on their predicted relevance during generation, thereby reducing the active memory footprint without requiring modifications to model weights. This dynamic approach contrasts with static compression schemes that fix a budget prior to inference and cannot adapt to the varying informational demands of different generation steps. Similarly, CORM [arxiv_2404.15949] introduces an optimization strategy centered on the observation that recent messages in the context carry disproportionate relevance for subsequent token prediction. By prioritizing the retention of recent KV entries and evicting older, less-attended states, CORM reduces memory pressure while preserving generation quality. Both InfiniGen and CORM thus share the principle of relevance-driven eviction, though they differ in the signals used to estimate relevance—predictive prefetching in the former versus recency heuristics in the latter.

A structurally distinct approach is taken by the Layer-Condensed KV Cache [arxiv_2405.10637], which targets the architectural source of memory overhead rather than managing an existing cache post hoc. By computing and storing KV representations for only a subset of transformer layers and reusing them across adjacent layers, this method substantially reduces the total number of cache entries that must be maintained, particularly in very deep models. This architectural intervention is complementary to eviction-based methods, as it reduces the baseline cache size before any runtime management is applied.

Collectively, these contributions reflect a broader recognition, synthesized in recent survey work [arxiv_2508.06297], that KV cache optimization is not a single problem but a multi-dimensional design space encompassing compression granularity, eviction criteria, and architectural coupling. Progress in this area is essential for making long-context LLM inference computationally feasible in real-world deployment scenarios.
