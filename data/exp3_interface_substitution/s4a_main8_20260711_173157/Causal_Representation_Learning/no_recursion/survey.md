# Causal Representation Learning

### Identifiability and Theoretical Foundations of Causal Representation Learning

Causal representation learning (CRL) pursues the ambitious goal of recovering latent causal variables and their relational structure from high-dimensional observations, yet its theoretical foundations rest on a set of identifiability conditions whose scope and limitations remain actively debated. A central challenge is that the observed data are typically generated by an unknown mixing function applied to latent causal variables, making recovery of both the variables and the causal graph an ill-posed problem without additional constraints.

One productive line of work leverages multi-distribution or interventional data to achieve identifiability. [arxiv_2306.00542] addresses this by studying nonparametric identifiability from unknown interventions, relaxing prior assumptions that required linearity in the mixing function or the latent causal model, and avoiding the need for explicit counterfactual supervision. Their framework demonstrates that observing data across multiple intervention contexts can be sufficient to identify latent causal structure under considerably weaker conditions than previously assumed. Complementing this, [arxiv_2402.05052] develops a general theoretical treatment of CRL from multiple distributions, formalising conditions under which the latent causal variables and the graph governing them can be recovered when the observed measurements are arbitrary functions of those variables. Together, these contributions establish that distributional heterogeneity—whether from interventions or naturally occurring shifts—is a powerful source of identifiability.

A contrasting perspective is offered by [arxiv_2410.23620], who investigate whether identifiability can be achieved from purely observational data, without assuming access to interventions on individual latent factors. Their work provides guarantees for causal disentanglement in this harder setting, directly challenging the prevailing assumption that intervention data is indispensable, while also clarifying the boundaries of what purely observational evidence can deliver.

The relationship between CRL and classical Independent Component Analysis (ICA) is examined by [arxiv_2305.17225], who introduce Causal Component Analysis (CauCA) as an intermediate problem bridging the two fields. Whereas ICA targets statistically independent sources, CRL must contend with causally dependent latents; CauCA formalises the transition between these regimes and yields identifiability results that illuminate how causal dependence among latent variables complicates recovery relative to the independent case.

Finally, [arxiv_2312.03580] interrogate the role of mechanism invariance—a cornerstone assumption in causal modelling—when the causal variables of interest are latent rather than observed. Their analysis establishes impossibility results showing that invariance principles, while powerful in the observed-variable setting, face fundamental limitations when applied to latent causal representation learning, thereby delineating important boundaries for the entire research programme.

### Weakly Supervised and Interventional Approaches to Causal Representation Learning

Causal representation learning (CRL) seeks to recover latent causal variables and their structural relationships from high-dimensional observations, yet purely observational data is generally insufficient for this task. A foundational contribution by Brehmer et al. demonstrates that learning high-level causal representations from unstructured data such as pixels is impossible from observational data alone, but becomes identifiable under a weakly supervised regime in which paired samples before and after unknown interventions are available [arxiv_2203.16437]. This result established an important benchmark for subsequent work, motivating a broader investigation into what forms of supervision or interventional structure are necessary and sufficient for identifiability.

Building on this foundation, von Kügelgen et al. extend identifiability theory to a nonparametric setting with unknown interventions, relaxing restrictive assumptions such as linearity on the mixing function or the latent causal model that had constrained earlier approaches [arxiv_2306.00542]. Complementing this, Varičí et al. establish both identifiability and achievability results under a general nonparametric latent causal model using two hard uncoupled interventions per node, crucially without requiring knowledge of which pair of intervention environments corresponds to each node [arxiv_2310.15450]. The extension to multi-node interventions is addressed in a separate contribution by the same group, which relaxes the prevalent but often unrealistic assumption that interventions affect only a single node at a time [arxiv_2406.05937]. Together, these works progressively weaken the assumptions required for identifiability while broadening the class of intervention types considered.

A complementary line of research focuses on practical learning algorithms under weak supervision. Shen et al. propose the DEAR framework, which explicitly models causally related latent factors using a disentangled generative approach under appropriate supervised information, demonstrating that methods relying on independence priors fail when underlying factors are causally structured [arxiv_2010.02637]. Similarly, Ahuja et al. leverage weak distributional invariances across multiple domains to learn causal representations, arguing that multi-domain datasets provide a natural setting in which causal structure can be identified without strong interventional labels [arxiv_2310.02854]. Fan et al. further address the independence assumption by incorporating causal flow-based mechanisms into a variational autoencoder framework, enabling disentangled representations that respect causal dependencies among generative factors [arxiv_2304.09010].

The role of implicit learning from both hard and soft interventions is examined by Shirahmad Gale Bagi et al., who argue that soft interventions are more realistic in practice and develop a switchable mechanism framework for implicit causal representation learning in the absence of known graph structures [arxiv_2402.11124]. Meanwhile, Wendong et al. introduce Causal Component Analysis as an intermediate problem bridging independent component analysis and full CRL, providing identifiability guarantees when interventional data on the latent causal graph are available [arxiv_2305.17225].

Despite these theoretical advances, most results have relied on asymptotic arguments. Lee et al. address this gap by providing explicit finite-sample guarantees for causal representation learning with a sublinear number of environments, offering a more practically grounded foundation for the field [arxiv_2603.25796]. Collectively, these contributions reveal a trajectory from early weak-supervision results toward increasingly general, assumption-light, and empirically actionable frameworks for interventional causal representation learning.
