# Causal Representation Learning

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning

Disentangled causal representation learning seeks to uncover latent generative factors that are not merely statistically independent but are organized according to an underlying causal structure. A foundational challenge in this space is that classical disentanglement methods, which impose independence priors on latent variables, are insufficient when the true generative factors are causally related. This limitation is explicitly addressed by [arxiv_2010.02637], which proposes the Disentangled gEnerative cAusal Representation (DEAR) framework, demonstrating that methods relying on independent priors fail to disentangle causally related factors and that appropriate weak supervision is necessary to recover such structure. Complementing this perspective, [arxiv_2112.05746] argues that prior disentanglement work, even when incorporating weak supervision or correlated features, has largely neglected a causal view of the generative process, and advocates for representations whose factors of variation are explicitly grounded in causal semantics. Together, these two contributions mark a shift from purely statistical notions of disentanglement toward causally informed ones.

On the generative modeling side, Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs) have served as a dominant architectural choice for disentangled representation learning, yet their standard formulations inherit the independence assumption. [arxiv_2304.09010] addresses this gap by proposing a causal flow-based VAE that explicitly models interdependencies among generative factors, acknowledging that real-world factors are often causally coupled rather than independent. This work thus extends the VAE paradigm to accommodate causal structure, offering a more faithful inductive bias for complex data. Similarly, [arxiv_2504.11609] situates the problem within the broader context of interpretable deep generative models, arguing that causal representation learning provides a principled route to making implicit learned representations more transparent and interpretable, a concern that is particularly pressing as generative AI systems are deployed across high-stakes domains.

A distinct but related challenge arises when the data are multimodal or structurally heterogeneous. [arxiv_2310.18471] examines causal disentanglement in multimodal settings, noting that many existing causal learning algorithms rely on strong prior information such as linear structural causal models, interventional data, or weak supervision, and that these assumptions become difficult to satisfy when data modalities are diverse. This observation highlights a tension in the field: the theoretical identifiability results that underpin disentangled causal representations often depend on assumptions that are hard to verify or enforce in practice. Across these contributions, a consistent theme emerges — moving from independence-based to causality-based disentanglement requires both richer supervision signals and more expressive generative architectures, and the field is actively developing frameworks that balance theoretical rigor with practical applicability.

### Identifiability Theory for Causal Representations

Identifiability theory constitutes a foundational pillar of causal representation learning (CRL), addressing the core question of when and under what conditions latent causal variables and their relational structure can be uniquely recovered from high-dimensional observations. Early contributions to this area often imposed restrictive assumptions—such as linearity of the mixing function or the latent causal model—that limited their applicability to real-world settings. More recent work has sought to relax these constraints substantially.

A prominent line of inquiry concerns nonparametric identifiability, where neither the mixing function nor the latent causal model is assumed to belong to a parametric family. Von Kügelgen and colleagues demonstrate that identifiability of causal representations is achievable without such restrictions, provided that observations are organized into counterfactual or interventional views that supply weak supervision [arxiv_2306.00542]. This work is notable for operating under unknown interventions, meaning the learner need not know which variables were intervened upon, a practically significant relaxation. Complementing this, Varıcı et al. establish both identifiability and achievability results under a fully general nonparametric latent causal model and a general transformation model, showing that two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph suffice for identification, even when the pairing of intervention environments is unknown [arxiv_2310.15450]. Together, these two contributions converge on the conclusion that nonparametric identifiability is attainable under interventional diversity, though they differ in the precise form of supervision and the constructive algorithms they propose.

Zhang et al. extend the scope further by considering a general multi-distribution setting in which the measured variables are arbitrary functions of latent causal variables, and demonstrate that the latent structure—including both the variables and the causal graph—can be recovered from data collected across multiple environments [arxiv_2402.05052]. This framing is particularly relevant for problems involving distribution shift and emphasizes the role of environmental heterogeneity as an identification lever.

A related but distinct approach is explored through Causal Component Analysis (CauCA), which positions itself as an intermediate problem between classical Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and full CRL [arxiv_2305.17225]. Whereas ICA recovers statistically independent components and CRL targets causally related latent variables along with their graph, CauCA bridges these objectives by incorporating causal graph structure into the component recovery problem. This decomposition clarifies the theoretical demands of CRL relative to ICA and provides a modular framework for understanding which aspects of the identification problem are attributable to statistical dependence versus causal structure. Collectively, these contributions delineate a rapidly maturing identifiability theory that progressively weakens assumptions while strengthening guarantees.
