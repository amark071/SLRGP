# Causal Representation Learning

### Identifiability and Theoretical Foundations of Causal Representations

#### Nonparametric and General Identifiability under Interventions

Establishing identifiability of causal representations under minimal or unknown intervention assumptions has emerged as a central theoretical challenge in the field. A foundational contribution in this direction is provided by [arxiv_2306.00542], which studies causal representation learning without placing restrictive parametric assumptions on either the mixing function or the latent causal model. Rather than requiring knowledge of which variables are intervened upon or relying on counterfactual paired observations, this work demonstrates nonparametric identifiability from unknown interventions, thereby substantially relaxing the supervision requirements that characterized earlier approaches. The result is significant because it shows that the latent causal structure can be recovered even when the nature and targets of interventions are not specified in advance.

Building on the goal of generality, [arxiv_2310.15450] establishes both identifiability and achievability results under a fully general nonparametric latent causal model combined with a general transformation mapping latent variables to observations. A distinctive feature of this framework is its reliance on two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph, where crucially the learner need not know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node. This uncoupled setting is considerably more realistic than prior work that assumed known intervention targets, and the paper's achievability results complement its identifiability theory by demonstrating that the identified structure can in principle be recovered algorithmically.

A closely related contribution is [arxiv_2402.00849], which similarly addresses intervention-based causal representation learning under a general nonparametric latent causal model and an unknown transformation, but explicitly distinguishes between linear and general transformation regimes. By treating both identifiability and achievability within a unified score-based framework, this work connects the theoretical guarantees to a concrete algorithmic strategy, offering a more constructive perspective than [arxiv_2310.15450] while sharing its commitment to generality.

Complementing these intervention-centric analyses, [arxiv_2311.12267] examines identifiability from multiple environments without assuming access to single-node interventions, a setting that prior results had largely taken for granted. This work introduces the notion of intrinsic ambiguity, characterizing the fundamental limits on how precisely causal representations can be recovered when the environment structure is more general. Together, these four contributions delineate a coherent research frontier: progressively weakening assumptions about intervention knowledge and parametric form while carefully characterizing what identifiability remains achievable and what irreducible ambiguity persists.

#### Linear and Parametric Causal Representation Identifiability

Early theoretical work on causal representation learning quickly revealed that recovering latent causal structure from observed data is fundamentally underdetermined without additional constraints. A central strand of research has therefore focused on establishing identifiability guarantees under linear or parametric assumptions, progressively clarifying which combinations of structural restrictions and data regimes are sufficient to pin down both the latent causal model and the observation mapping.

Within the linear setting, [arxiv_2211.16467] provides a foundational analysis of the case in which observed variables are a linear transformation of an underlying linear latent causal model. The authors demonstrate that identifiability of such representations—meaning the unique recovery of both the latent model and the mixing transformation—requires data collected under interventions rather than purely observational data. This result formalises an important negative finding: passive observation alone is insufficient, and active or experimental variation is a necessary ingredient for disentanglement in the linear regime. Building directly on this line of inquiry, [arxiv_2311.02695] examines identifiability when causal representations are linearly mixed and data arise from multi-node interventions. Their work situates itself within a growing corpus of approaches that exploit multi-environment data to circumvent the fundamental underconstraint of causal representation learning, showing that intervening on multiple nodes simultaneously provides the leverage needed to identify the latent causal variables up to the indeterminacies permitted by the model class.

Beyond purely linear models, a complementary body of work investigates parametric causal representations in which identifiability is achieved by exploiting variation in model parameters across environments. [arxiv_2208.14153] addresses the identification of weight-variant latent causal models, carefully characterising three intrinsic indeterminacies that arise when instantaneous causal relations among latent variables are permitted, and proposing conditions under which these indeterminacies can be resolved. Extending this parametric perspective, [arxiv_2310.15580] studies latent polynomial causal models and establishes identifiability by leveraging changes in causal influences across multiple environments. Whereas [arxiv_2208.14153] focuses on weight variation as the identifying signal, [arxiv_2310.15580] frames the problem through the lens of distributional change more broadly, demonstrating that polynomial functional forms provide sufficient structure to guarantee reliable recovery of the latent causal model. Together, these four contributions delineate a coherent theoretical landscape in which the degree of structural constraint—linearity of the mixing, linearity or polynomiality of the causal mechanisms, and the richness of interventional or multi-environment data—jointly determines the strength of identifiability guarantees attainable for causal representations.

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning

Disentangled causal representation learning has emerged as a significant research direction that seeks to move beyond classical independence-based disentanglement toward frameworks that explicitly account for causal structure among latent variables. Early disentanglement methods typically enforced independence priors over latent factors, but this assumption proves insufficient when the underlying generative factors are themselves causally related. As [arxiv_2010.02637] demonstrates, such independent-prior methods fundamentally fail to disentangle causal factors, motivating the development of the Disentangled Generative Causal Representation (DEAR) framework, which incorporates supervised information to recover causally structured latent representations. Similarly, [arxiv_2112.05746] critiques the independence assumption prevalent in earlier unsupervised disentanglement models and argues for an explicitly causal view of the generative process, noting that while weak supervision and correlated features have been explored in the literature, these efforts have largely proceeded without grounding in causal generative mechanisms.

Building on such critiques, more principled frameworks have been proposed to formalize what it means for a representation to be causally disentangled. [arxiv_2306.01213] introduces a novel notion of causal disentanglement grounded in the principle of independent causal mechanisms, proposing the ICM-VAE framework to operationalize this definition. This approach is notable for its explicit theoretical grounding, defining disentanglement not merely in terms of statistical independence but in terms of the modularity and autonomy of causal mechanisms—a conceptually richer criterion that has implications for the utility of learned representations in downstream tasks.

The temporal dimension of disentanglement has also received attention. [arxiv_2210.13647] addresses the limitations of existing identifiability results, which tend to rely on restrictive functional form assumptions such as independent sources or linear transitions between latent states. By exploiting temporal structure as a form of side information, this work establishes stronger identifiability guarantees for causally related latent variables in sequential settings, extending the reach of disentangled causal representation learning beyond static observational data.

Taken together, these works reflect a coherent progression in the field: from recognizing the inadequacy of independence-based disentanglement [arxiv_2010.02637, arxiv_2112.05746], to constructing principled causal frameworks [arxiv_2306.01213], to extending identifiability results into temporally structured domains [arxiv_2210.13647]. A shared theme across all four contributions is the necessity of incorporating causal assumptions—whether through supervision, mechanistic independence principles, or temporal dynamics—to achieve representations that are both identifiable and interpretable in a causally meaningful sense.
