# Causal Representation Learning

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

Causal representation learning has emerged as a central challenge at the intersection of causal inference and deep generative modelling, with a growing body of work addressing how latent causal variables and their relational structure can be recovered from high-dimensional observations. A foundational concern in this literature is the disentanglement of latent factors, and several contributions have moved beyond classical independence assumptions to accommodate causally structured priors. [arxiv_2010.02637] introduces the DEAR framework, which explicitly models causal relations among latent variables and demonstrates that methods relying on independent priors fail to disentangle causally related factors, even under weak supervision. Complementing this, [arxiv_2112.05746] similarly critiques independence-based disentanglement and proposes a causally grounded view of the generative process, arguing that a causal perspective is necessary for achieving representations useful in fairness and interpretability applications. Together, these two works establish that causal structure among latent variables is not merely a refinement but a prerequisite for principled disentanglement.

A parallel line of inquiry concerns identifiability—the conditions under which latent causal variables can be uniquely recovered. [arxiv_2306.00542] addresses this in a nonparametric setting, relaxing restrictive assumptions about linearity in the mixing function or the latent causal model, and demonstrates identifiability from unknown interventions without requiring parametric knowledge of which environments correspond to which interventions. This nonparametric generality is extended in [arxiv_2310.15450], which establishes both identifiability and achievability results under a general transformation model using two hard uncoupled interventions per node, further relaxing the requirement that intervention environments be labelled. [arxiv_2402.05052] broadens the scope further still by considering multiple distributions arising from diverse data-generating conditions, providing a general framework for recovering latent causal variables and their graph from observational heterogeneity.

Temporal structure offers another route to identifiability, particularly for sequential data. [arxiv_2210.13647] exploits time-delayed causal influences to establish strong identifiability for causally related latent variables without requiring independent sources, overcoming functional form constraints that limited earlier approaches. [arxiv_2310.18615] extends this temporal paradigm to nonstationary settings, where existing stationary-assumption methods only partially resolve the identification problem; by addressing unknown nonstationarity, it substantially broadens the applicability of temporal disentanglement. Finally, [arxiv_2207.05723] approaches causal representation learning from a Bayesian perspective, introducing a latent variable decoder model for causal discovery from high-dimensional data and studying causal recovery empirically with synthetic benchmarks, thereby bridging generative modelling and structure learning in a probabilistic framework.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Causal representation learning (CRL) has emerged as a rich interdisciplinary field that bridges causal discovery, deep learning, and representation learning, and the literature in this space reflects a diversity of methodological commitments and theoretical concerns. A broad survey perspective is offered by [arxiv_2211.03374], which frames deep causal learning as encompassing representation, discovery, and inference, and catalogues the principal challenges confronting traditional causal methods when applied to high-dimensional, unstructured data, including combinatorial optimization difficulties and the presence of unobserved confounders. This panoramic treatment provides useful context for understanding why more specialized approaches have proliferated.

Among those specialized approaches, disentangled representation learning occupies a central position. [arxiv_2304.09010] critiques the widespread assumption of factor independence in variational autoencoder (VAE)-based methods, arguing that real-world generative factors are frequently interdependent. Their Causal Flow-based VAE addresses this by explicitly modelling causal structure among latent dimensions, thereby extending the representational fidelity of standard VAE frameworks. This concern with inter-factor dependencies is complementary to the work of [arxiv_2306.01213], which approaches disentanglement through the lens of independent causal mechanisms (ICM). Their ICM-VAE framework defines causal disentanglement in terms of mechanism independence rather than statistical independence of factors, offering a principled grounding that connects representation learning to the broader ICM principle from the causal inference literature.

The question of what interventional data is required for learning causal representations is addressed from a different angle by [arxiv_2402.11124], which focuses on implicit causal representation learning in settings where the ground-truth causal graph is unknown. Crucially, this work highlights the distinction between hard and soft interventions, arguing that soft interventions are more realistic in practice and developing a framework based on switchable mechanisms to handle both regimes. This contrasts with the identifiability results surveyed in [arxiv_2311.12267], which examines what can be recovered from observations generated across multiple environments without assuming access to single-node interventions. [arxiv_2311.12267] demonstrates that identifiability remains achievable under more general environmental assumptions, though intrinsic ambiguities must be carefully characterized, thereby relaxing constraints that much prior work had treated as necessary.

Taken together, these contributions illustrate a field navigating the tension between theoretical identifiability guarantees and practical applicability. While [arxiv_2211.03374] maps the landscape of challenges, [arxiv_2304.09010] and [arxiv_2306.01213] propose generative modelling solutions that respect causal structure, and [arxiv_2402.11124] and [arxiv_2311.12267] push the boundaries of what can be identified under weaker or more realistic assumptions about available data and interventions.
