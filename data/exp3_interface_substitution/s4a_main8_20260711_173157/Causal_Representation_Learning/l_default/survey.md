# Causal Representation Learning

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning with Generative Models

Disentangled causal representation learning has emerged as a prominent research direction that seeks to move beyond classical independence assumptions in latent variable models, instead grounding representation learning in explicit causal structures. Early disentanglement methods typically assumed that latent factors are statistically independent, an assumption that proves inadequate in many real-world settings where underlying generative factors are causally related. This limitation has motivated a wave of approaches that incorporate causal reasoning directly into generative model frameworks.

A foundational contribution in this space is the DEAR framework [arxiv_2010.02637], which proposes weakly supervised disentangled generative causal representation learning. Rather than enforcing independence among latent variables, DEAR explicitly models causal relationships between the underlying factors of interest, demonstrating that methods relying on independent priors systematically fail to disentangle causally related variables. Complementing this perspective, [arxiv_2112.05746] argues that prior disentanglement approaches, even those leveraging weak supervision or correlated features, lack a principled causal view of the generative process, and proposes representations that are evaluated through an explicitly causal lens.

On the structural side, [arxiv_2303.00128] extends conventional generative model assumptions by introducing a causal collider structure to describe the data-generating process, addressing scenarios where the standard independent and identically distributed factorization is insufficient, such as datasets subject to sampling selection bias. This structural perspective contrasts with the approach taken by [arxiv_2306.01213], which defines causal disentanglement through the principle of independent causal mechanisms and proposes the ICM-VAE framework, emphasizing that meaningful disentanglement requires not merely separating factors but ensuring that their causal mechanisms operate independently.

Within the variational autoencoder paradigm specifically, [arxiv_2304.09010] proposes a causal flow-based VAE that relaxes the independence assumption among generative factors, accommodating the interdependencies and causal relationships that characterize real-world data. This work highlights that standard VAE-based disentanglement is fundamentally limited when factors exhibit causal structure, a concern echoed across the broader literature. Looking toward interpretability, [arxiv_2504.11609] situates causal representation learning within the context of deep generative models more broadly, arguing that incorporating causal structure is essential for producing representations that are not only disentangled but genuinely interpretable. Taken together, these works collectively establish that integrating causal generative model structures—whether through weak supervision, structural assumptions, or principled independence criteria—is necessary for achieving robust and meaningful disentanglement in complex data domains.

### Temporal and Sequential Causal Representation Learning

#### Identifiability and Disentanglement in Temporal Causal Representation Learning

Establishing identifiability and disentanglement in temporal causal representation learning has emerged as a central theoretical challenge, with a growing body of work progressively relaxing the assumptions under which latent causal variables can be recovered from sequential observations.

Early contributions demonstrated that exploiting temporal structure provides a powerful inductive bias for disentanglement. However, as noted in [arxiv_2210.13647], most existing work in this space remained constrained by restrictive functional form assumptions, such as requiring sources to be statistically independent or imposing linear transition dynamics between time steps. This work sought to move beyond those constraints by establishing identifiability results for causally-related latent variables in stationary temporal settings without relying solely on independence or linearity, leveraging instead the time-delayed causal structure inherent in sequential data.

Building directly on this stationary foundation, subsequent research turned to the more challenging nonstationary setting. [arxiv_2310.18615] observes that while strong identifiability guarantees had been established for stationary environments, nonstationary conditions had only been partially addressed by prior methods, which either required observed auxiliary variables or made other limiting assumptions. Their framework tackles unknown nonstationarity in sequential data with time-delayed latent causal influences, thereby substantially broadening the practical applicability of temporal disentanglement results.

A complementary line of inquiry concerns the generation process linking latent causal variables to high-dimensional observations. [arxiv_2401.14535] highlights that many methods capable of robustly identifying time-delayed latent causal processes nonetheless depend on the assumption that the generation mapping from latent variables to observations is invertible — a condition that is frequently violated in real-world data. By relaxing this invertibility requirement, their approach extends identifiability guarantees to a wider class of generative models, addressing a gap that the stationary and nonstationary frameworks of [arxiv_2210.13647] and [arxiv_2310.18615] had not fully resolved.

A further dimension of complexity arises from the nature of causal interactions themselves. [arxiv_2206.06169] points out that much prior temporal causal representation learning assumes the absence of instantaneous causal relations between variables, focusing exclusively on time-delayed effects. Yet in interactive systems, measurements are frequently influenced by both instantaneous and temporally lagged causal processes. Their work extends identifiability analysis to settings that incorporate both types of effects, demonstrating that causal variables can still be recovered from high-dimensional observations such as images under these more realistic conditions.

Taken together, these contributions trace a coherent trajectory: from stationary settings with strict functional assumptions [arxiv_2210.13647], through nonstationary environments [arxiv_2310.18615] and non-invertible generation processes [arxiv_2401.14535], to systems admitting instantaneous causal interactions [arxiv_2206.06169], collectively advancing the theoretical foundations of identifiable temporal causal representation learning.

#### Causal Discovery from Time Series Data

Causal discovery from time series data has emerged as a central challenge within causal representation learning, attracting approaches ranging from classical constraint-based algorithms to modern deep learning architectures. A comprehensive overview of the field situates temporal data as a uniquely valuable substrate for causal inference, given that chronological ordering provides a natural asymmetry that can be exploited to orient causal relationships [arxiv_2303.10112]. Nevertheless, the problem remains difficult due to latent confounders, high dimensionality, nonstationarity, and the entanglement of temporal with contemporaneous causal relations.

Among constraint-based approaches, one line of work addresses the joint recovery of temporal and contemporaneous causal structures under a stationary structural vector autoregressive assumption, explicitly treating latent confounders through iterative refinement [arxiv_2306.00624]. This contrasts with methods that relax the stationarity assumption entirely: Balsells-Rodas et al. develop a discovery framework for conditionally stationary time series, broadening applicability to a wider class of nonstationary processes that traditional methods cannot handle [arxiv_2110.06257]. The challenge of nonstationarity is thus a recurring theme, motivating methodological extensions beyond classical Granger causality and conditional independence testing.

Deep learning has substantially expanded the toolkit for temporal causal discovery. Neural Granger causality methods have proven effective in moderate-dimensional settings, but their performance degrades with high-dimensional irregular time series; CUTS+ addresses this by redesigning the network architecture to reduce redundancy and accommodate irregular sampling [arxiv_2305.05890]. A complementary direction involves meta-learning: rather than designing hand-crafted assumptions, domain-specific causal discovery learns from data generated under known ground-truth structures, enabling more flexible and accurate inference in domains such as neuroscience and medicine [arxiv_2209.05598]. Pushing further toward end-to-end learning, causal pretraining treats the mapping from multivariate time series directly to causal graphs as a learnable objective, moving away from post-hoc analysis of model components [arxiv_2402.09305].

Transformer-based architectures have attracted particular interest for capturing long-range nonlinear dependencies. CausalFormer leverages attention weights to extract interpretable causal relations from temporal sequences [arxiv_2406.16708], while a related framework explicitly integrates prior knowledge into a multi-layer Transformer forecaster to mitigate spurious correlations and recover time-lagged causal structure [arxiv_2508.15928]. Both approaches reflect a broader shift toward architectures that jointly perform prediction and causal structure identification.

Applications to complex real-world systems further motivate methodological innovation. In climate analysis, for instance, latent atmospheric drivers interact with observed geographically proximate variables, requiring methods that jointly model hidden dynamic processes and direct observed causal influences—a setting where standard causal representation learning focused solely on latent factors proves insufficient [arxiv_2501.12500]. Across all these threads, deep causal learning is increasingly recognized as essential for overcoming the limitations of traditional methods when confronting high-dimensional, unstructured, and confounded temporal data [arxiv_2211.03374].
