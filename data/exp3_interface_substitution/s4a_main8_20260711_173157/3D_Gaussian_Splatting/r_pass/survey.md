# 3D Gaussian Splatting

### Foundational Methods and Surveys

#### Foundational Methods and Core 3DGS Representations

The foundational work in 3D Gaussian Splatting was established by Kerbl et al., who introduced a real-time radiance field rendering framework that addressed longstanding trade-offs between visual quality and computational efficiency [arxiv_2308.04079]. Prior to this contribution, achieving high-fidelity novel-view synthesis of unbounded and complete scenes at 1080p resolution required either costly neural networks or methods that sacrificed rendering quality for speed. The 3DGS framework resolved this tension by representing scenes as collections of explicit 3D Gaussian primitives, enabling both fast rasterization-based rendering and competitive visual quality, thereby setting a new benchmark against which subsequent methods have been measured [arxiv_2308.04079].

Despite its considerable success, the original 3DGS formulation exhibits notable limitations in geometric fidelity. Because 3D Gaussians are volumetric primitives, they tend to produce multi-view inconsistent surface representations, making accurate geometry reconstruction difficult. To address this shortcoming, Huang et al. proposed 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS), which replaces volumetric 3D Gaussians with planar 2D Gaussian discs embedded in three-dimensional space [arxiv_2403.17888]. This reformulation enforces stronger surface alignment and multi-view consistency, yielding geometrically accurate radiance fields while preserving the fast rendering advantages introduced by the foundational 3DGS framework. The contrast between these two approaches highlights a fundamental design tension: volumetric Gaussians offer flexible scene coverage but at the cost of surface precision, whereas flattened primitives recover geometric accuracy by constraining the representational degrees of freedom [arxiv_2308.04079, arxiv_2403.17888].

A complementary line of work targets a different weakness of the original 3DGS representation, namely its difficulty in capturing sharp shape and color discontinuities. Li et al. introduced 3D Half-Gaussian (3D-HGS) kernels as a plug-and-play extension to the standard 3DGS pipeline, specifically designed to model abrupt transitions in appearance and geometry that smooth, symmetric Gaussians inherently struggle to represent [arxiv_2406.02720]. This approach demonstrates that the choice of primitive shape carries significant consequences for reconstruction quality in scenes with fine structural detail.

Further extending the representational scope of Gaussian splatting, Gao et al. proposed 6DGS, which augments the standard spatial Gaussian representation with an angular dimension to form a six-dimensional spatial-angular primitive [arxiv_2410.04974]. This direction-aware formulation is particularly motivated by the challenge of rendering physically-based view-dependent effects, such as specular reflections, which are inadequately captured by purely spatial representations. Collectively, these foundational and extended methods illustrate a progressive refinement of the core Gaussian primitive, moving from the original isotropic volumetric representation toward increasingly specialized primitives tailored to surface accuracy, discontinuity handling, and directional light transport [arxiv_2308.04079, arxiv_2403.17888, arxiv_2406.02720, arxiv_2410.04974].

#### Surveys and Broad Overviews of 3DGS

The rapid rise of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm in novel view synthesis has prompted a growing body of survey literature aimed at consolidating and contextualising its many developments. These overviews collectively situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of scene representation research, most commonly by contrasting it with Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). Where NeRF relies on implicit, coordinate-based neural networks to map spatial positions and viewing directions to colour and density values, 3DGS instead employs millions of learnable 3D Gaussian ellipsoids as an explicit scene representation, paired with a differentiable rasterisation pipeline that enables real-time rendering [arxiv_2401.03890]. This architectural distinction is consistently foregrounded across survey works as the primary reason for 3DGS's substantial gains in rendering speed and its emerging role as a practical alternative to implicit methods [arxiv_2403.11134].

Among the earliest and most comprehensive overviews, Wu et al. survey the acceleration of novel view synthesis that 3DGS enables, tracing how the explicit Gaussian representation supports efficient rendering while maintaining competitive visual fidelity [arxiv_2403.11134]. Chen and Wang similarly characterise 3DGS as a transformative technique within the radiance field landscape, emphasising not only its real-time rendering capability but also the unprecedented editability afforded by its explicit, differentiable representation, which they argue positions it as a potential game-changer for 3D reconstruction [arxiv_2401.03890]. He et al. extend this perspective by highlighting that the explicit and compact nature of 3DGS enables a wide range of downstream applications beyond novel view synthesis, including segmentation, editing, and generation tasks that require geometric and semantic understanding [arxiv_2508.09977]. This breadth of applicability distinguishes their survey from more narrowly scoped overviews and reflects the maturation of the field.

A particularly notable contribution to the survey literature comes from Kerbl, one of the original authors of 3DGS, who provides a reflective overview of the method's impact since its introduction [arxiv_2510.26694]. This work traces how follow-up research has enhanced the efficiency, scalability, and real-world applicability of 3DGS, offering an authoritative perspective on the directions the community has pursued. Compared to the broader thematic surveys, Kerbl's overview is more focused on identifying key research trajectories and open challenges, lending it a prospective as well as retrospective character [arxiv_2510.26694].

Domain-specific surveys complement these broad overviews by examining how 3DGS has been adopted in particular application areas. Zhu et al. survey the use of 3DGS in robotics, noting that its explicit radiance field representation offers advantages over NeRF-based approaches for tasks requiring dense 3D environmental understanding [arxiv_2410.12262]. Similarly, Zhu and Tang survey dynamic scene reconstruction, reviewing both NeRF-based and 3DGS-based methods and emphasising recent progress in real-time rendering and streaming [arxiv_2503.08166]. Together, these domain surveys demonstrate that the influence of 3DGS has extended well beyond its origins in static scene rendering, and that the foundational properties identified in the broad overviews — explicit representation, differentiable rendering, and real-time capability — continue to motivate its adoption across diverse and challenging settings.

### Rendering Quality and Volumetric Accuracy

#### Volumetric Rendering Accuracy and Ray-Based Methods in 3DGS

The relationship between volumetric rendering accuracy and the practical performance of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has emerged as a central theoretical and empirical concern in the field. While Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) are grounded in a principled ray-marching framework for volumetric rendering, 3DGS adopts a rasterization-based pipeline that introduces approximations whose consequences for rendering fidelity remain an active subject of investigation [arxiv_2502.19318]. A foundational treatment of this tension is provided by Matias et al., who trace the theoretical connections between classical volume rendering and the Gaussian splatting formalism, situating 3DGS within the broader landscape of 3D reconstruction and arguing that its rasterization approach, while enabling real-time performance, represents a deliberate departure from volumetrically exact rendering [arxiv_2510.18101].

One of the most consequential approximations in standard 3DGS concerns the handling of alpha compositing, which depends on depth-sorted ordering of Gaussian primitives. Müller et al. identify this reliance on order-dependent alpha blending and coarse density integral approximations as a fundamental limitation, particularly when rendering complex, overlapping semi-transparent objects, and propose a moment-based transmittance formulation to achieve order-independent rendering [arxiv_2512.11800]. This concern is echoed by Kheradmand et al., who demonstrate that sorted rendering introduces artifacts attributable to the built-in approximations of the sorting pipeline, and propose stochastic rasterization as a sorting-free alternative that better respects the underlying volumetric model [arxiv_2503.24366].

In parallel, several works have sought to reintroduce ray-based computation into the 3DGS framework as a means of recovering volumetric accuracy. Blanc et al. propose RayGauss, which replaces the splatting rasterizer with a volumetric ray-casting procedure applied to locally parameterized Gaussian structures, arguing that this hybrid approach recovers the principled integration of NeRF-style methods while retaining the representational advantages of Gaussian primitives [arxiv_2408.03356]. Similarly, Byrski et al. introduce RaySplats, which substitutes rasterization with ray tracing to enable accurate modeling of light transport phenomena such as shadows and reflections that are inherently difficult to capture under rasterization [arxiv_2501.19196]. Gao et al. further extend this direction with 6DGS, which incorporates a six-dimensional spatial-angular Gaussian representation to better handle view-dependent effects under physically-based ray tracing, addressing limitations that persist even in direction-aware extensions of standard 3DGS [arxiv_2410.04974]. Complementing these efforts, Gu et al. develop a 2D Gaussian ray tracer to enable accurate visibility and indirect radiance modeling for inter-reflective scenes, noting that the absence of a capable Gaussian ray tracer had previously forced inverse rendering methods to rely on simplified rendering equations or learnable approximations [arxiv_2412.15867]. Collectively, these contributions suggest that while the approximations inherent to rasterization-based 3DGS are tolerable for many novel-view synthesis tasks, achieving physically accurate volumetric and light-transport fidelity increasingly demands the reintegration of ray-based methods into the Gaussian splatting pipeline.

#### Gaussian Primitive Design and Geometric Representation

The foundational design of Gaussian primitives in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has become a central concern in the literature, as the geometric properties of these primitives directly govern both rendering quality and the fidelity with which a scene's volumetric structure is captured. The original 3DGS framework, introduced by Kerbl et al., represents scenes as collections of anisotropic 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterised by position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent colour [arxiv_2308.04079]. This explicit, primitive-based formulation enables real-time rasterisation at high resolution, distinguishing it sharply from neural implicit representations such as NeRF, which rely on costly volumetric ray-marching [arxiv_2403.11134]. Nevertheless, the rendering pipeline of 3DGS departs from physically principled volumetric integration, and Celarek et al. interrogate whether this departure meaningfully compromises accuracy, finding that the approximated compositing used in 3DGS shares conceptual similarities with NeRF's formulation while introducing subtle inconsistencies that affect geometric fidelity [arxiv_2502.19318].

These geometric limitations have motivated several alternative primitive designs. Huang et al. argue that volumetrically defined 3D Gaussians are inherently multi-view inconsistent when used to represent surfaces, and propose collapsing the representation to 2D Gaussian discs embedded in 3D space, yielding substantially more accurate surface reconstruction and depth estimation [arxiv_2403.17888]. A different geometric critique is raised by Gong et al., who note that anisotropic kernels, while expressive, complicate adaptive control operations such as splitting and merging; they advocate for isotropic Gaussian kernels as a computationally simpler alternative that preserves competitive rendering quality [arxiv_2403.14244]. Li et al. take yet another direction, proposing half-Gaussian kernels that introduce an asymmetric profile specifically designed to handle sharp colour and shape discontinuities that symmetric Gaussians struggle to represent cleanly [arxiv_2406.02720]. Complementing these spatial redesigns, Gao et al. extend the primitive into a six-dimensional spatial-angular representation, enabling more faithful modelling of view-dependent effects and physically-based reflectance that standard 3D Gaussians approximate poorly [arxiv_2410.04974].

Beyond shape, the sheer number of primitives required for high-quality reconstruction imposes substantial memory costs, prompting work on compact representations. Lee et al. demonstrate that the per-Gaussian parameter footprint of standard 3DGS can be reduced significantly through codebook-based compression without proportionate losses in visual quality [arxiv_2311.13681; arxiv_2408.03822], while Girish et al. pursue lightweight encodings that accelerate both training and storage [arxiv_2312.04564]. Morgenstern et al. further propose organising Gaussians onto structured grids to exploit spatial coherence for compression [arxiv_2312.13299]. Hybrid approaches seek to reconcile expressivity with efficiency: Zhou et al. integrate neural features directly into splattable primitives to recover representational power lost when abandoning implicit networks [arxiv_2510.08491], and Wang and Xu combine explicit Gaussians with implicit radiance components to reduce per-primitive parameter redundancy [arxiv_2509.17083]. Collectively, this body of work reveals an ongoing tension between geometric expressiveness, volumetric accuracy, and computational tractability that continues to shape the evolution of Gaussian primitive design.
