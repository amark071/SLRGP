# 3D Gaussian Splatting

### Foundations and Surveys of 3D Gaussian Splatting

#### Foundational Methods and Core 3DGS Representations

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a foundational paradigm for novel-view synthesis represents a significant departure from the implicit neural representations that preceded it. The seminal work by Kerbl et al. introduced 3DGS as an explicit, discrete scene representation composed of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives, enabling real-time radiance field rendering at 1080p resolution without sacrificing visual quality [arxiv_2308.04079]. Unlike Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs), which rely on costly neural network inference and ray-marching procedures during both training and rendering, 3DGS achieves substantially faster training and inference by representing scenes as collections of learnable Gaussian splats that are rasterized directly onto the image plane [arxiv_2308.04079]. This explicit representation has since become a widely adopted reference method for learning 3D scene representations from multi-view imagery [arxiv_2502.19318].

Despite its practical success, the theoretical underpinnings of 3DGS rendering have attracted scrutiny. Celarek et al. examine the extent to which 3DGS adheres to principled volumetric rendering, noting that while NeRF is grounded in a physically motivated ray-marching framework, 3DGS employs a rendering approximation that departs from strict volumetric correctness [arxiv_2502.19318]. This analysis raises important questions about whether the high visual quality achieved by 3DGS stems from accurate physical simulation or from the flexibility of its learned primitives, and it situates 3DGS within a broader theoretical context relative to its NeRF predecessors [arxiv_2502.19318].

The choice of Gaussian primitives, while computationally advantageous, introduces inherent representational limitations. Held et al. observe that standard 3D Gaussians struggle to accurately capture sharp geometric features such as hard edges, often requiring a substantially increased number of primitives to approximate such structures [arxiv_2411.14974]. In response, they propose 3D Convex Splatting, which replaces Gaussian primitives with smooth convex shapes, offering greater geometric expressiveness while retaining the rendering efficiency characteristic of splatting-based approaches [arxiv_2411.14974].

The explicit and discrete nature of 3DGS also has direct implications for downstream tasks such as surface reconstruction. Song et al. highlight that, in contrast to NeRF-based methods burdened by slow implicit representations, 3DGS provides an explicit scene structure; however, reconstructing coherent surfaces from the resulting discrete Gaussian distribution in open scenes remains non-trivial [arxiv_2411.01853]. Collectively, these works illustrate that while the foundational 3DGS representation established by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] offers compelling advantages in speed and quality, ongoing research continues to interrogate its theoretical assumptions and extend its core primitives to address geometric and surface-level limitations.

#### Surveys and Broad Reviews of 3DGS

The rapid emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a foundational technique in computer graphics and novel view synthesis has prompted a growing body of survey literature aimed at consolidating its theoretical underpinnings and cataloguing its expanding range of applications. At its core, 3DGS departs from the implicit, coordinate-based paradigm associated with Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) by representing scenes as collections of explicit 3D Gaussian ellipsoids or primitives, a design choice that yields substantial gains in rendering efficiency [arxiv_2403.11134]. This explicit representation enables differentiable rendering while avoiding the computationally intensive ray-marching procedures characteristic of NeRF-based methods, making real-time or near-real-time performance achievable in practice [arxiv_2402.07181].

Several broad surveys have sought to map the landscape of 3DGS research since its introduction. Wu et al. [arxiv_2403.11134] provide a comprehensive account of recent advances, emphasising how the shift from neural implicit representations to Gaussian ellipsoid-based scene modelling has accelerated novel view synthesis and opened new research directions across the field. Complementing this, Fei et al. [arxiv_2402.07181] frame 3DGS as heralding a new era in computer graphics, highlighting its explicit scene representation as a distinguishing feature that removes the dependency on neural networks entirely, and surveying its uptake across diverse domains including robotics, urban mapping, autonomous navigation, and virtual and augmented reality. Together, these two surveys offer overlapping yet complementary perspectives: whereas Wu et al. concentrate on technical advances and rendering performance, Fei et al. place greater emphasis on the breadth of application domains that 3DGS has penetrated.

Beyond these general reviews, domain-specific surveys have begun to appear. Zhu et al. [arxiv_2410.12262] focus specifically on the robotics context, arguing that the explicit radiance field representation afforded by 3D Gaussian primitives addresses a long-standing goal of dense 3D environmental modelling in robotics, and contrasting 3DGS favourably with NeRF's implicit coordinate-based approach in terms of representational clarity and practical deployability. Meanwhile, Ali et al. [arxiv_2502.19457] approach the literature from an efficiency standpoint, surveying compression methods for 3DGS and noting that, while the use of millions of learnable 3D Gaussians endows the representation with expressive power and differentiable rendering capability, it simultaneously introduces significant memory and storage challenges that motivate ongoing compression research. Collectively, these surveys illustrate how 3DGS has rapidly matured from a novel rendering technique into a broad research programme with dedicated sub-communities addressing efficiency, application-specific adaptation, and scalability.

### Scene Reconstruction and Surface Geometry

#### Foundational 3DGS Methods and Surveys

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for scene representation and novel-view synthesis marks a significant departure from earlier neural implicit approaches. The foundational method, introduced by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079], addresses a longstanding tension in radiance field rendering between visual quality and computational efficiency. Prior neural approaches, including Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), achieved high fidelity but demanded costly training and inference through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks. Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] proposed instead to represent scenes as collections of 3D Gaussian primitives, enabling real-time rendering at 1080p resolution for unbounded and complete scenes without sacrificing quality — a combination that existing methods had failed to deliver simultaneously.

The impact of this contribution has been rapid and far-reaching, prompting several survey efforts that seek to consolidate the growing body of work it has inspired. Wu et al. [arxiv_2403.11134] characterize 3DGS as having "greatly accelerated the rendering speed of novel view synthesis," emphasizing the contrast with NeRF-based implicit representations: whereas NeRF encodes a scene within neural network weights queried at continuous coordinates, 3DGS employs a set of explicit Gaussian ellipsoids that can be rendered through a differentiable rasterization pipeline. This explicit representation is central to the efficiency gains the method achieves. Chen and Wang [arxiv_2401.03890] similarly position 3DGS as a "transformative technique in radiance fields," highlighting that the use of millions of learnable 3D Gaussians, combined with a differentiable rendering algorithm, yields not only real-time performance but also unprecedented editability — properties that implicit neural models inherently struggle to provide. Both surveys thus converge on the view that the explicit, learnable nature of the Gaussian representation is the key architectural innovation distinguishing 3DGS from its predecessors.

Dalal et al. [arxiv_2405.03417] situate 3DGS within the broader context of image-based 3D reconstruction, a task they describe as challenging due to the need to infer 3D geometry from 2D observations. Their review encompasses both reconstruction quality and novel-view synthesis, reflecting the dual utility of 3DGS as a tool for recovering scene structure and for generating photorealistic renderings from unseen viewpoints. Taken together, these surveys [arxiv_2403.11134, arxiv_2401.03890, arxiv_2405.03417] confirm that the method of Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] has rapidly become a foundational reference point, establishing the explicit Gaussian primitive as the dominant representation around which subsequent advances in scene reconstruction and surface geometry have been organized.

#### Surface Reconstruction with Gaussian Primitives

Surface reconstruction from 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) primitives has emerged as a productive but technically demanding research direction, as the explicit, discrete nature of Gaussian point clouds introduces fundamental challenges for recovering coherent geometry. A central difficulty is that standard 3DGS optimises Gaussians primarily for photometric fidelity, offering no intrinsic guarantee of multi-view geometric consistency or accurate surface localisation. Several complementary strategies have been proposed to address this limitation, broadly divisible by the geometric form of the primitive employed and the mechanism used to extract a continuous surface.

One influential line of work reformulates the primitive itself to be inherently surface-aligned. Chen et al. [arxiv_2406.06521] introduce planar-based Gaussian Splatting (PGSR), which constrains Gaussians to planar configurations so that their orientations directly encode local surface normals, thereby reducing the ambiguity that arises when volumetric ellipsoids are used to approximate thin surfaces. Similarly, Huang et al. [arxiv_2403.17888] propose 2D Gaussian Splatting (2DGS), collapsing the three-dimensional Gaussian into a two-dimensional disk primitive embedded in 3D space. Because each disk is intrinsically planar, 2DGS achieves multi-view consistent surface intersections that volumetric 3D Gaussians cannot, yielding geometrically accurate radiance fields while retaining the fast rendering advantages of the splatting paradigm. Both approaches share the intuition that aligning the primitive's degenerate axis with the surface normal is a more principled basis for geometry recovery than post-hoc meshing of unconstrained ellipsoids.

A second strategy operates on the opacity or density field induced by the Gaussian mixture rather than modifying the primitive shape. Yu et al. [arxiv_2404.10772] present Gaussian Opacity Fields (GOF), which derive a continuous volumetric opacity field from the 3D Gaussians and apply adaptive isosurface extraction, enabling surface reconstruction in unbounded scenes where explicit primitive alignment is difficult to enforce globally. This field-based approach decouples surface extraction from the discrete arrangement of individual Gaussians, offering greater flexibility for complex, open-world environments.

A third direction targets computational efficiency in large open scenes through hybrid spatial representations. Song et al. [arxiv_2411.01853] propose Gaussian Voxel Kernel Functions (GVKF), combining voxel-grid spatial indexing with Gaussian kernel evaluations to achieve highly efficient surface reconstruction without the extensive training times associated with implicit NeRF-based methods. Compared with the primitive-redesign approaches of [arxiv_2406.06521] and [arxiv_2403.17888], GVKF prioritises scalability, whereas GOF [arxiv_2404.10772] occupies a middle ground by maintaining the original Gaussian representation while constructing a derived continuous field. Together, these works illustrate that surface reconstruction from Gaussian primitives remains an open problem, with current solutions trading off geometric accuracy, scene scale, and computational cost in different ways.
