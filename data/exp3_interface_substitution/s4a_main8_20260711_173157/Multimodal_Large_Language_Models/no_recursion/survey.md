# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal Large Language Models

The rapid convergence of natural language processing and computer vision has given rise to a vibrant research area centered on Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), which integrate textual and visual — and sometimes auditory — information within unified architectures. Several comprehensive surveys have sought to map this evolving landscape from complementary angles. Caffagni et al. [arxiv_2402.12451] characterize the development of MLLMs as a genuine revolution in generative intelligence, emphasizing how these models provide dialogue-based, instruction-following interfaces that seamlessly connect visual and textual modalities. Similarly, Li et al. [arxiv_2309.10020] offer a taxonomic account of multimodal foundation models, tracing a trajectory from narrow specialist systems toward general-purpose assistants capable of handling diverse vision-language tasks, and organizing the field around five core research topics spanning both well-established and emerging directions.

Complementing these taxonomy-driven accounts, Pattnayak et al. [arxiv_2412.17759] survey the datasets, application categories, and taxonomies that underpin multimodal learning, drawing an explicit analogy to the human capacity to assimilate information through multiple senses and highlighting downstream applications such as visual question answering, text-to-video conversion, and image captioning. Munikoti et al. [arxiv_2406.05496] adopt a somewhat broader architectural lens, reviewing design elements for generalist multimodal AI across modalities including text, image, video, and sensor data, and situating recent advances within the longer trajectory of foundation model research in NLP and vision. Where Pattnayak et al. [arxiv_2412.17759] foreground data and application diversity, Munikoti et al. [arxiv_2406.05496] place greater emphasis on architectural challenges and opportunities, making the two surveys usefully complementary.

A recurring concern across the literature is the substantial computational cost associated with training and deploying MLLMs. Jin et al. [arxiv_2405.10739] address this directly, surveying efficient and lightweight MLLM designs and arguing that the extensive model sizes and high inference costs of current systems represent a significant barrier to widespread academic and industrial adoption. This efficiency-oriented perspective sits alongside evaluation-focused work: Xu et al. [ss_478371] present LVLM-eHub, a comprehensive benchmark covering thirteen representative large vision-language models, providing the community with standardized tools for assessing multimodal efficacy across diverse capabilities. Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that is simultaneously expanding its architectural ambitions, diversifying its modality coverage — as illustrated by the inclusion of audio in omni-perception frameworks [ss_478472] — and growing increasingly attentive to the practical demands of efficiency and rigorous evaluation. The breadth of these overviews underscores both the maturity and the continued dynamism of multimodal large language model research.

### Vision-Language Pretraining Methods

Early vision-language pretraining (VLP) research established foundational architectures and objectives that have since been substantially extended. One influential early contribution is the unified VLP model proposed by Zhou et al. [arxiv_1909.11059], which employs a shared multi-layer transformer for both encoding and decoding, enabling fine-tuning for either generative tasks such as image captioning or understanding tasks such as visual question answering (VQA). This unified design contrasted with contemporaneous approaches that maintained separate encoder and decoder networks, offering greater parameter efficiency and architectural simplicity.

A central debate in the field concerns the relative merits of contrastive versus generative pretraining objectives. Tschannen et al. [arxiv_2306.07915] conduct a systematic comparison of contrastive pretraining on image-text pairs against image captioning as a pretraining strategy, challenging the prevailing assumption that contrastive objectives are inherently superior for learning visual representations. Their findings suggest that captioning-based pretraining can serve as a scalable and competitive alternative, with important implications for how vision backbones within multimodal systems are trained.

A recurring limitation of web-crawled image-text pairs as pretraining data is their coarse alignment between visual and linguistic content. Liu et al. [arxiv_2305.11769] address this by jointly training a question generator and a dense captioner during pretraining, thereby encouraging finer-grained feature alignment that standard image-text pair objectives tend to overlook. This approach reflects a broader recognition, surveyed by Gan et al. [arxiv_2210.09263], that VLP methods can be categorized along multiple axes—including the nature of the pretraining data, the architectural coupling of modalities, and the downstream tasks targeted—and that no single objective uniformly dominates across all settings.

Scaling has emerged as another critical dimension. PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794] demonstrates that jointly scaling both the language and vision components of a multimodal model, while maintaining a flexible text-generation interface, yields strong performance across a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks in multiple languages. Similarly, InternVL [arxiv_2312.14238] argues that vision foundation models have lagged behind large language models in scale and proposes a large-scale vision-language model designed to close this gap through progressive alignment training.

Beyond the bimodal vision-language paradigm, VALOR [ss_478472] extends pretraining to three modalities—vision, audio, and language—using separate unimodal encoders combined with a multimodal decoder, demonstrating that omni-perceptual pretraining can improve performance on tasks requiring joint reasoning across all three modalities. Collectively, these works illustrate a trajectory from early unified transformer architectures toward increasingly large, diverse, and fine-grained pretraining frameworks, with ongoing tension between contrastive and generative objectives and growing interest in richer modality combinations [arxiv_2302.00389].
