# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Multimodal Pretraining and Foundation Models

The emergence of large-scale pretraining paradigms in natural language processing has catalysed a parallel movement in the multimodal domain, where vision and language are increasingly treated as jointly learnable modalities. As surveyed by [arxiv_2306.07198], the success of models such as BERT and the GPT series in establishing general-purpose representations through pretraining on large corpora has inspired analogous efforts in the vision-language space, prompting rapid development of models capable of reasoning across both textual and visual inputs. This trajectory is further contextualised by [arxiv_2309.10020], which offers a comprehensive taxonomy of multimodal foundation models, tracing their evolution from narrow specialist systems toward general-purpose assistants capable of handling diverse vision and vision-language tasks within a unified framework.

A central concern in this literature is how to design backbone architectures and pretraining objectives that support broad transfer across heterogeneous tasks. BEiT-3 [arxiv_2208.10442] addresses this challenge by framing images as a "foreign language" and applying masked data modelling uniformly across text, images, and image-text pairs. This approach advances what the authors describe as a "big convergence" of language, vision, and multimodal pretraining along three dimensions: backbone architecture, pretraining task design, and model scaling, ultimately achieving state-of-the-art transfer performance on both purely visual and vision-language benchmarks. The unification of modalities under a single pretraining objective stands in contrast to earlier specialist pipelines that required separate encoders and task-specific heads.

Complementing this architectural convergence, PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794] demonstrates that joint scaling of vision and language components is itself a powerful lever for multimodal capability. By training a model that generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, PaLI establishes a flexible task interface that accommodates a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages. The authors emphasise that effective scaling, when applied jointly to the image encoder and the language model, yields consistent gains, suggesting that the two modalities benefit from being co-scaled rather than optimised independently.

Taken together, these works illustrate a field converging on several shared principles: large-scale joint pretraining, unified architectures that treat vision and language symmetrically, and flexible interfaces that reduce the need for task-specific adaptation. The survey perspectives offered by [arxiv_2306.07198] and [arxiv_2309.10020] situate these individual contributions within a broader paradigm shift, underscoring that the transition from specialist to general-purpose multimodal models is driven not by any single innovation but by the compounding effects of architectural unification, pretraining scale, and increasingly diverse training data.

### Efficient Adaptation and Instruction Tuning of MLLMs

The efficient adaptation and instruction tuning of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has emerged as a central research concern, driven by the high computational costs of training and the need for fine-grained cross-modal alignment. A foundational challenge is that existing approaches often require expensive retraining of the underlying language model and offer limited adaptability to downstream tasks, with most work concentrating on zero-shot performance improvements that provide insufficient guidance for task-specific tuning [arxiv_2408.06610]. To address these limitations, CROME introduces cross-modal adapters that enable cost-effective multimodal adaptation without full language model retraining, representing a parameter-efficient alternative to conventional end-to-end fine-tuning paradigms [arxiv_2408.06610].

Complementing this line of work, researchers have explored how visual instruction tuning can be refined to achieve more precise cross-modal alignment. Standard visual instruction tuning methods rely on image-language instruction data to bridge the two modalities, yet this coarse alignment is insufficient for tasks requiring detailed spatial or positional understanding [arxiv_2308.13437]. In response, position-enhanced visual instruction tuning has been proposed to incorporate finer-grained positional signals into the alignment process, thereby improving the model's capacity to ground language in specific visual regions [arxiv_2308.13437]. This approach highlights that the quality and granularity of instruction data are as consequential as the architectural choices made during adaptation.

The scope of instruction tuning has also been extended beyond general vision-language benchmarks toward domain-specific visual tasks. While MLLMs have demonstrated strong performance on broad image understanding, they have not yet been systematically adapted for tasks requiring explicit and specialized visual comprehension [arxiv_2402.08360]. Visual Question Answering instruction frameworks have been proposed to unlock MLLM capabilities for such domain-specific multitasks, suggesting that targeted instruction design is a necessary complement to architectural efficiency gains [arxiv_2402.08360].

More broadly, the integration of textual and visual information within multimodal models—understood as extensions of LLMs—has been shown to support a diverse array of tasks including image captioning and visual question answering [arxiv_2403.08773]. Advances in multimodal representation learning, such as those embodied in the Veagle framework, further demonstrate that improving how visual and linguistic representations are jointly encoded is essential for robust cross-modal performance [arxiv_2403.08773]. Taken together, these contributions indicate that efficient adaptation, fine-grained alignment, and domain-aware instruction tuning are mutually reinforcing directions that collectively advance the practical deployment of MLLMs.
