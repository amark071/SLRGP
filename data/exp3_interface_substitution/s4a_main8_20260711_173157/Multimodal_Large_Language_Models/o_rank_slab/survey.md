# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

The rapid convergence of visual and linguistic modalities has catalyzed a new generation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), prompting extensive survey and benchmarking efforts. At the broadest level, [arxiv_2402.12451] characterizes this development as a revolution in generative intelligence, arguing that MLLMs capable of seamlessly integrating visual and textual modalities—while supporting dialogue-based, instruction-following interfaces—represent a qualitative leap beyond earlier unimodal systems. This framing is complemented by [arxiv_2309.10020], which traces the taxonomic evolution from narrow specialist models toward general-purpose multimodal assistants, organizing the landscape around five core research topics spanning both well-established and emerging capabilities. Together, these two surveys provide complementary macro-level perspectives: the former emphasizes the generative and conversational dimensions of recent MLLMs, while the latter foregrounds the architectural and capability transitions that made such generality possible. Extending this scope further, [arxiv_2410.05608] broadens the definition of multimodality beyond vision and language to encompass audio, video, and sensor data, underscoring that the field is moving toward truly heterogeneous input integration rather than remaining confined to image–text pairs.

Alongside these conceptual surveys, rigorous empirical evaluation has emerged as a pressing concern. [ss_478371] addresses this gap directly by constructing LVLM-eHub, a comprehensive benchmark evaluating thirteen representative large vision-language models across diverse capability dimensions. The existence of such a benchmark reflects a recognized need for holistic assessment frameworks, given that individual model reports often optimize for narrow metrics. This concern about evaluation is echoed implicitly in [arxiv_2406.06730], which identifies a specific capability deficit—the inability of many visually tuned MLLMs to comprehend text embedded within images—and introduces the TRINS instruction dataset to address it, highlighting how training data limitations can produce systematic blind spots even in otherwise capable models.

Efficiency and adaptability represent another axis of active investigation. [arxiv_2408.06610] proposes CROME, a cross-modal adapter framework designed to reduce the cost of training and task-specific adaptation in MLLMs, critiquing approaches that require full language model retraining and offer limited transferability. This work positions parameter-efficient adaptation as essential for practical deployment, a concern less prominent in the broader surveys but increasingly central as model scale grows.

Finally, interpretability remains an underexplored challenge. [arxiv_2203.17247] notes that while visualization tools have matured for NLP models, the internal mechanisms of vision-language transformers remain largely opaque, and introduces VL-InterpreT as an interactive tool for probing these representations. Relatedly, [arxiv_2409.07463] demonstrates domain-specific application of MLLMs to semiconductor electron microscopy, employing a teacher-student instruction-tuning strategy with GPT-4-generated data, illustrating how general-purpose multimodal frameworks can be adapted to highly specialized scientific contexts. Collectively, these works reveal a field simultaneously consolidating foundational architectures, expanding modality coverage, confronting evaluation and efficiency challenges, and beginning to grapple with interpretability.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Research into multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has increasingly turned toward systematic evaluation and interpretability as the field matures. A central concern in this literature is how to rigorously assess the capabilities of large vision-language models (LVLMs) across diverse tasks. Xu et al. [arxiv_2306.09265] address this directly by constructing LVLM-eHub, a comprehensive evaluation benchmark encompassing eight representative LVLMs and a broad suite of multimodal competencies. Their work highlights a persistent gap between the rapid deployment of such models and the availability of holistic evaluation frameworks capable of capturing their true efficacy across tasks ranging from visual reasoning to knowledge-grounded question answering.

Complementing this evaluative perspective, Garg and Bas [arxiv_2310.03211] examine the performance of instruction-tuned multimodal language models under zero-shot conditions. Their analysis focuses on models that integrate independently pretrained vision encoders into LLMs through model grafting, a technique that has become increasingly common as a means of extending language model capabilities into the visual domain. They find that while instruction tuning enables effective zero-shot generalization, performance varies considerably depending on the nature of the downstream task, underscoring the importance of benchmark diversity as advocated by [arxiv_2306.09265].

A distinct but related strand of inquiry concerns the interpretability and explainability of MLLMs. Dang et al. [arxiv_2412.02104] provide a comprehensive survey of this emerging area, arguing that the convergence of large language models and computer vision systems into multimodal AI has introduced new challenges for transparency. Their survey maps the landscape of explainability methods applicable to cross-modal architectures and emphasizes that as MLLMs are deployed in higher-stakes settings, the ability to interpret model decisions spanning text, vision, and audio becomes not merely desirable but necessary. This concern for interpretability sits in productive tension with the performance-oriented evaluations of [arxiv_2306.09265] and [arxiv_2310.03211], since optimizing for benchmark scores does not automatically yield more interpretable systems.

Taken together, these three contributions reflect a broader maturation in MLLM research: having established that multimodal instruction-tuned models can generalize effectively in zero-shot settings [arxiv_2310.03211], the community is now grappling with how to evaluate that generalization comprehensively [arxiv_2306.09265] and how to ensure that the resulting systems remain transparent and accountable [arxiv_2412.02104]. The interplay between evaluation rigor and interpretability is likely to define a significant portion of near-term research in this domain.
