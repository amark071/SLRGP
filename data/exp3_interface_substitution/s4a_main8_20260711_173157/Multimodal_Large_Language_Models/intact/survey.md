# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The rapid growth of large language models (LLMs) has catalyzed a parallel expansion in multimodal research, prompting scholars to survey and synthesize the field's progress from multiple vantage points. Early survey efforts focused primarily on vision-language pre-training (VLP) as a foundational paradigm, examining how models could be trained to jointly represent image and text data. [arxiv_2210.09263] provides one such foundational overview, grouping VLP approaches into categories addressing image-text tasks—such as image captioning, visual question answering, and image-text retrieval—as well as core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification. This taxonomy established a useful conceptual scaffold for understanding how pre-training objectives and architectures were being designed to bridge visual and linguistic modalities.

As the field matured, surveys began to reflect a broader and more ambitious scope. [arxiv_2309.10020] charts the evolution from narrow, task-specific specialist models toward general-purpose multimodal assistants, offering a comprehensive taxonomy that spans five core research topics. This transition narrative is significant: whereas earlier VLP models were largely optimized for individual benchmarks, the newer generation of multimodal foundation models is designed to handle diverse instructions across a wide range of vision and vision-language tasks within a single unified system. The framing of this shift as a progression from specialists to generalists captures an important structural change in how researchers conceptualize model capability and deployment.

More recent surveys have focused specifically on the class of models that integrate visual perception directly with the generative and reasoning capabilities of LLMs. [arxiv_2402.12451] examines what it terms the "revolution" of multimodal large language models, emphasizing their ability to seamlessly integrate visual and textual modalities while supporting dialogue-based, instruction-following interfaces. This work reflects the growing consensus that the combination of strong language model backbones with visual encoders represents a qualitatively new paradigm, rather than a mere incremental extension of prior VLP methods.

Complementing these vision-centric perspectives, [arxiv_2410.05608] takes a broader view by encompassing not only vision and language but also audio, sensor data, and video within the multimodal LLM framework. This tutorial-style survey traces the evolution of multimodal research from foundational concepts through to the latest technical challenges, positioning the field as one defined by the integration of heterogeneous data streams rather than any single modality pair. Together, these surveys collectively illustrate how the scope of multimodal LLM research has progressively widened—from image-text pre-training to general-purpose, multi-sensory intelligent systems—while converging on shared architectural principles and evaluation challenges.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has been shaped by advances in both architectural design and large-scale pretraining strategies that bridge visual and linguistic representations. A central challenge in this space is how to effectively align visual inputs with the powerful generative capabilities of pretrained language models, and researchers have pursued several distinct approaches to address this problem.

One influential line of work focuses on jointly scaling vision and language components during pretraining. PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794] exemplifies this paradigm by combining a large vision encoder with a multilingual language model trained on a diverse mixture of image-text tasks. By generating text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs through a unified interface, PaLI demonstrates that joint scaling across modalities yields strong performance across vision, language, and multimodal tasks in many languages. This work underscores the importance of not treating the vision and language towers as independently optimized components but rather as jointly trained elements whose combined capacity drives downstream performance.

Building on the broader trend of extending LLMs to multimodal settings, Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] highlights how multimodal models have emerged as natural extensions of large language models, inheriting their generalization capabilities while incorporating mechanisms to process visual information alongside text. Such models have demonstrated notable versatility across tasks including image captioning and visual question answering, reflecting the growing consensus that a flexible, unified architecture can serve as a foundation for diverse multimodal applications.

A complementary concern is the computational cost of adapting pretrained models to new modalities. CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] addresses this by introducing cross-modal adapters that enable efficient multimodal adaptation without requiring expensive retraining of the underlying language model. This approach is particularly relevant for task-specific fine-tuning scenarios where zero-shot generalization alone is insufficient, offering a more practical pathway for deploying MLLMs in resource-constrained settings. Compared to full joint pretraining approaches like PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], adapter-based methods such as CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] trade some degree of representational integration for substantially reduced training overhead.

Beyond the vision-language dyad, VALOR [ss_478472] extends the pretraining paradigm to encompass audio as a third modality, jointly modeling vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end framework with separate unimodal encoders feeding into a shared multimodal decoder. This omni-perceptual approach contrasts with the predominantly vision-language focus of models like PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794] and Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773], suggesting that richer cross-modal pretraining objectives may be necessary for models intended to operate in naturalistic, multi-sensory environments. Together, these works illustrate a trajectory from pairwise vision-language alignment toward more comprehensive multimodal architectures capable of handling heterogeneous sensory inputs.
