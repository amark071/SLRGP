# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal Large Language Models

The rapid convergence of vision and language processing has catalyzed an expansive body of survey literature seeking to map the landscape of multimodal large language models (MLLMs). These overviews collectively reflect both the breadth of the field and the diversity of analytical frameworks researchers have adopted to make sense of its rapid growth.

Among the most comprehensive treatments, Caffagni and colleagues situate MLLMs within the broader trajectory of generative intelligence, arguing that the integration of visual and textual modalities represents a foundational shift enabled by the success of large language models [arxiv_2402.12451]. Their survey emphasizes the dialogue-based and instruction-following capabilities that distinguish contemporary MLLMs from earlier vision-language systems, framing these properties as central to the models' practical utility. A similarly broad scope is adopted by Han et al., whose tutorial-oriented overview extends the definition of multimodality beyond vision and text to encompass audio, video, sensor data, and other heterogeneous input streams [arxiv_2410.05608]. By tracing the evolution of multimodal research from foundational concepts to current technical challenges, this work underscores that the field's ambitions have grown considerably beyond the image-text pairing that initially dominated the literature.

A complementary perspective is offered by Li et al., who organize the field around a taxonomy that foregrounds the transition from specialist models—designed for narrow, well-defined tasks—to general-purpose assistants capable of flexible, open-ended interaction [arxiv_2309.10020]. This evolutionary framing, which covers five core research topics spanning both established and emerging areas, provides a useful conceptual scaffold for understanding how architectural and training innovations have progressively expanded model capabilities. Pattnayak et al. adopt a similarly taxonomic approach but place greater emphasis on the diversity of datasets and application categories that now define the field, highlighting text-to-video conversion, visual question answering, and image captioning as representative use cases that illustrate the practical reach of multimodal learning [arxiv_2412.17759].

A distinct but complementary strand of survey work approaches MLLMs from a data-centric perspective. Bai et al. argue that data—spanning text, vision, audio, video, and 3D environments—plays a pivotal role in both the development and refinement of these models, and their review systematically examines how data collection, curation, and preprocessing choices shape model behavior [arxiv_2405.16640]. This perspective usefully complements architecture-focused surveys by drawing attention to the often underexamined infrastructural foundations upon which MLLM capabilities rest.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field characterized by rapid diversification in both modalities addressed and analytical frameworks employed, with researchers variously emphasizing architectural evolution, taxonomic organization, application breadth, and data quality as the primary lenses through which MLLM progress should be understood.

### Multimodal Pretraining Architectures and Representation Learning

The development of multimodal large language models has been substantially shaped by advances in pretraining architectures and representation learning strategies that seek to unify information across heterogeneous modalities. Early momentum in this space was driven by the success of large-scale unimodal pretraining paradigms such as BERT, ViT, and the GPT series, which demonstrated that general-purpose representations could be acquired through self-supervised objectives on massive datasets [arxiv_2306.07198]. This success catalyzed a parallel wave of research into joint vision-language pretraining, where the central challenge became learning aligned cross-modal representations that support downstream tasks such as Visual Question Answering, Natural Language for Visual Reasoning, and Vision-Language Retrieval [arxiv_2302.00389].

Comprehensive surveys of the field have documented the rapid proliferation of large-scale multimodal pretrained models, noting that the demand for generalized deep models capable of operating across modalities has driven architectural innovation at considerable scale [arxiv_2302.10035]. A recurring theme across these architectures is the tension between modality-specific encoding and cross-modal fusion: many systems employ dedicated encoders per modality to preserve intra-modal structure before projecting representations into a shared semantic space, a design choice that facilitates both modality-specific pretraining objectives and cross-modal alignment losses [arxiv_2302.00389; arxiv_2302.10035].

A notable extension of this paradigm is the move beyond the dominant vision-language dyad toward omni-perceptual pretraining that incorporates audio as a third modality. The VALOR framework exemplifies this direction by jointly modeling the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner, employing three separate encoders for single-modality representations alongside a decoder for multimodal conditional generation [arxiv_2304.08345; ss_478472]. This architecture contrasts with conventional vision-language models by explicitly capturing audio-visual correspondences that are otherwise discarded, thereby enriching the learned representations with temporal and acoustic cues that are particularly salient for video understanding tasks [ss_478472]. The inclusion of audio also necessitates new pretraining datasets and objectives that go beyond image-caption pairs, reflecting a broader trend toward richer, more naturalistic multimodal corpora [arxiv_2304.08345].

Taken together, these works illustrate an evolutionary trajectory in multimodal pretraining: from unimodal foundations and dual-encoder vision-language models toward more comprehensive architectures that integrate additional sensory streams. Cross-modal interaction and the exploitation of complementary information across modalities remain central design principles [arxiv_2302.00389], while scalable pretraining on diverse multimodal data continues to be identified as a prerequisite for robust generalization across the wide variety of tasks that multimodal large language models are expected to address [arxiv_2302.10035; arxiv_2306.07198].
