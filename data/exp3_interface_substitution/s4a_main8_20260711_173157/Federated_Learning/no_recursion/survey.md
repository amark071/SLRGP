# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past several years, giving rise to a growing body of survey literature that maps the field's conceptual foundations, technical challenges, and practical applications. At its core, federated learning addresses a fundamental tension in modern machine learning: data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across devices, organizations, and regions, yet legal and regulatory constraints often prohibit the direct sharing of raw data [arxiv_2104.14362]. By exchanging only locally trained models or computed gradients rather than underlying data, federated learning offers a privacy-respecting alternative to traditional centralized approaches, enabling collaborative model training without direct data exposure [arxiv_2301.01299].

Several surveys have sought to situate federated learning within the broader landscape of distributed machine learning. Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] trace the conceptual lineage from distributed machine learning to federated learning, emphasizing how the shift toward edge devices and organizational data silos has necessitated new algorithmic paradigms. This historical framing complements more recent systematic surveys, such as that of Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299], which provide a structured overview of advances in privacy-preserving collaborative learning and highlight how the field has matured in response to both theoretical and practical demands.

Beyond foundational overviews, the survey literature has increasingly focused on specific technical challenges that distinguish federated learning from conventional distributed settings. A particularly prominent concern is the non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) nature of data across participating clients. Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] offer a dedicated survey on this problem, demonstrating that performance degradation under heterogeneous data distributions represents one of the most significant obstacles to deploying federated learning at scale. Their analysis underscores that standard aggregation strategies developed under IID assumptions often fail when local data distributions diverge substantially, motivating a distinct line of algorithmic research.

Privacy and security considerations have similarly generated dedicated survey contributions. Liu et al. [arxiv_2203.17005] focus specifically on privacy-preserving aggregation mechanisms within federated learning, reflecting the growing concern that even the exchange of model updates—rather than raw data—can expose sensitive information through inference attacks. Their work highlights the practical necessity of integrating cryptographic and perturbation-based techniques into the aggregation process, a concern that distinguishes privacy-focused surveys from broader overviews.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that has evolved from general distributed learning principles toward increasingly specialized investigations of data heterogeneity, privacy guarantees, and system-level constraints, collectively establishing the conceptual vocabulary and open challenges that motivate ongoing research.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Differential privacy (DP) has become the dominant formal privacy framework applied within federated learning (FL), owing to its rigorous mathematical guarantees against inference attacks on private training data [arxiv_2405.08299]. At its core, DP-FL introduces calibrated noise into the learning process—typically through gradient perturbation—to bound the statistical influence any individual's data can exert on the released model [arxiv_2005.00218]. However, this protection comes at a well-documented cost: injecting noise degrades model utility, and the magnitude of degradation scales with model dimensionality and the number of communication rounds [arxiv_2211.15893].

A central challenge in the literature concerns the tension between privacy guarantees and model accuracy. Hu et al. [arxiv_2003.13761] address this by employing concentrated differential privacy, a tighter variant of the standard DP definition, to reduce the noise required while preserving meaningful privacy budgets across rounds. Similarly, Cheng et al. [arxiv_2203.03106] identify gradient divergence under user-level DP as a primary driver of accuracy loss, proposing local regularization and sparsification to counteract this effect. Sparsification is also central to the approach of Hu et al. [arxiv_2008.01558], who demonstrate that selectively transmitting sparse gradient updates amplifies the effective privacy guarantee, thereby permitting less noise for an equivalent privacy level. Fu et al. [arxiv_2211.15893] take a complementary direction by introducing adaptive noise scheduling, reducing injected perturbation in later training rounds when gradient sensitivity diminishes.

Communication efficiency represents a second major axis of research, since the bandwidth overhead of FL is compounded when DP mechanisms require additional randomness or encoding. Mohammadi et al. [arxiv_2101.12240] systematically examine how compression, partial participation, and periodic aggregation interact with DP constraints, showing that naive combinations can amplify training variance. Wang et al. [arxiv_2006.13039] propose a discrete Gaussian mechanism that simultaneously achieves strong privacy composition and reduced communication cost, outperforming prior work on both dimensions. Girgis and Diggavi [arxiv_2302.11152] leverage the multi-message shuffled privacy framework to construct communication-efficient distributed mean estimation, while Salehi et al. [arxiv_2409.13133] introduce correlated binomial noise via common randomness to jointly improve privacy and compression. Jiang et al. [arxiv_2209.12528] further address the practical concern of client dropout, proposing a distributed DP scheme resilient to participant failures without sacrificing privacy accounting.

Beyond noise mechanisms, personalization has emerged as a structural remedy for the privacy-accuracy tradeoff. Bietti et al. [arxiv_2202.05318] demonstrate that allowing clients to maintain local models alongside a shared global model improves the Pareto frontier between privacy loss and predictive performance under user-level DP. Hou et al. [arxiv_2304.05127] similarly analyze this balance across several FL algorithms, providing empirical evidence that algorithm choice substantially mediates how privacy budgets translate into accuracy degradation. Collectively, these works reveal that advances in DP-FL require co-optimizing noise mechanisms, gradient compression, and model architecture simultaneously, rather than treating each dimension in isolation [arxiv_2405.08299].
