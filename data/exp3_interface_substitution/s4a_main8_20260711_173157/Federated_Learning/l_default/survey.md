# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a prominent paradigm in distributed machine learning, attracting considerable scholarly attention and prompting numerous surveys and overviews that seek to map its theoretical foundations, practical applications, and open challenges. A foundational concern motivating this body of literature is the tension between the need to exploit distributed data and the legal or regulatory constraints that prevent direct data sharing across organizations and regions. As [arxiv_2104.14362] observes, federated learning has emerged as an efficient approach to exploit distributed data and computing resources precisely because conventional centralized methods are increasingly incompatible with modern data governance requirements.

Several surveys situate federated learning within the broader trajectory of distributed machine learning (DML), framing FL as an evolutionary refinement rather than an entirely novel paradigm. [arxiv_2010.09258] characterizes federated learning as an improved version of distributed machine learning that further offloads operations typically performed by a central server, repositioning that server as a coordinating assistant rather than a micromanaging authority. This architectural shift, the authors argue, yields meaningful gains in privacy and security, since raw data need not leave client devices. [arxiv_2104.14362] similarly traces the conceptual lineage from DML to FL, emphasizing that the distributed nature of both data and computing resources in end-user devices and organizations is what necessitates the federated approach.

Privacy preservation is a recurring theme across the survey literature. [arxiv_2011.14818] highlights that FL provides privacy-by-design, enabling collaborative model training across distributed clients—ranging from two to millions—without centralizing sensitive data. The authors extend their analysis to split learning as a complementary paradigm, situating both within the broader distributed collaborative machine learning (DCML) landscape and noting FL's growing relevance in health, finance, industry 4.0, and smart vehicles. This focus on domain applicability is echoed by [arxiv_2311.10832], which reviews the range of machine learning models deployed within federated settings and underscores FL's particular value in crisis scenarios demanding real-time decision-making with sensitive data.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that is simultaneously consolidating its theoretical identity—by distinguishing itself from classical distributed learning—and expanding its practical reach across high-stakes domains. The consistent emphasis on privacy, regulatory compliance, and heterogeneous client environments across [arxiv_2010.09258], [arxiv_2104.14362], [arxiv_2011.14818], and [arxiv_2311.10832] suggests that these concerns constitute the defining axes along which federated learning research continues to develop.

### Communication Efficiency in Federated Learning

#### Gradient/Model Compression and Quantization

Communication overhead remains one of the most significant practical barriers to deploying federated learning at scale, and a substantial body of research has focused on gradient and model compression as a primary mitigation strategy. The fundamental challenge is that transmitting full-precision gradient vectors or model updates between potentially millions of devices and a central server is both slow and costly, creating scalability bottlenecks that undermine the practical utility of federated systems [arxiv_2010.03246].

Several distinct compression paradigms have been explored in the literature. Sparsification, quantization, and related techniques have each been proposed as means of reducing the volume of information exchanged during training rounds [arxiv_2010.03246]. Among quantization-based approaches, ternary compression has attracted particular attention as an aggressive but tractable strategy: by constraining model or gradient values to a three-level representation, communication costs can be dramatically reduced while preserving a meaningful degree of model fidelity [arxiv_2003.03564]. Such extreme quantization is especially relevant in the context of smart mobile and IoT devices, where bandwidth constraints are severe and privacy considerations simultaneously motivate the federated paradigm itself [arxiv_2003.03564].

A key theoretical concern across these methods is whether compression can be integrated with local computation strategies—such as multiple local gradient steps—without sacrificing convergence guarantees, particularly under heterogeneous data distributions. Haddadpour et al. [arxiv_2007.01154] address this directly by providing a unified analysis that jointly treats gradient compression and local computation, offering sharp convergence guarantees for federated algorithms operating under both communication constraints and statistical heterogeneity. This unified perspective is valuable because prior work tended to analyze compression and local updates in isolation, leaving open questions about their interaction.

Complementing algorithmic approaches, information-theoretic frameworks offer a principled basis for understanding the fundamental limits of compression in federated learning. Yang et al. [arxiv_2204.10985] situate model compression within the theory of lossy distributed source coding, asking not merely whether a given scheme reduces communication cost, but whether existing schemes are optimal with respect to the rate-distortion tradeoff inherent in model aggregation. This perspective reveals that many heuristic compression methods may leave theoretical gains unrealized, motivating more principled design.

Taken together, these works reflect a progression from empirical compression heuristics toward rigorous theoretical foundations. While [arxiv_2003.03564] and [arxiv_2010.03246] focus on practical compression mechanisms and their optimization, [arxiv_2007.01154] and [arxiv_2204.10985] push toward unified and information-theoretically grounded analyses, suggesting that the field is maturing in its ability to characterize not just the performance of specific schemes but the boundaries of what compression can achieve in federated settings.

#### Source Coding, Entropy Coding, and Variable-Length Compression

Communication efficiency in federated learning has attracted substantial attention from researchers drawing on classical source coding and information-theoretic principles. A central question motivating this line of work is whether existing compression schemes approach the theoretical limits of what is achievable, and several studies have sought to ground practical algorithms in rate-distortion theory. [arxiv_2204.10985] directly addresses this gap by analyzing FL under a lossy distributed source coding framework, deriving bounds on the achievable compression-accuracy tradeoff and proposing optimization strategies that align practical schemes with information-theoretic limits. Similarly, [arxiv_2301.09269] introduces the M22 algorithm, which is explicitly inspired by rate-distortion theory and employs an M-magnitude weighted distortion measure combined with two degrees of freedom to balance compression rate against model update fidelity at the parameter server.

Variable-length and entropy coding represent a particularly important subclass of source coding techniques that have been adapted for FL communication. [arxiv_2402.03770] proposes Fed-CVLC, a framework that applies variable-length codes to compress communications between clients and the parameter server, exploiting the statistical non-uniformity of model updates to achieve coding lengths closer to the entropy bound. This contrasts with fixed-length quantization approaches, which cannot adapt codeword length to the empirical distribution of updates and therefore leave redundancy unexploited. [arxiv_2312.13461] takes a complementary direction by integrating error-bounded lossy compression through the SZ compressor into the FL pipeline, combining transform-based decorrelation with entropy coding backends to achieve high compression ratios while providing formal guarantees on the magnitude of introduced errors. [arxiv_2310.14693] similarly targets lightweight edge deployments and designs a compression pipeline that incorporates entropy coding as a final stage after quantization, demonstrating that the combination of these steps yields communication savings beyond what either technique achieves in isolation.

Predictive coding, another classical source coding paradigm, has also been adapted to exploit temporal redundancy across FL rounds. [arxiv_2111.08277] applies Wyner-Ziv coding—a form of distributed source coding with side information at the decoder—to gradient compression, leveraging the correlation between gradients in adjacent training rounds to reduce the rate required for accurate reconstruction. [arxiv_2108.00918] pursues a related idea through predictive coding, where the server maintains a prediction of each client's update and only the residual is transmitted and compressed, achieving significant overhead reduction in bandwidth-constrained wireless settings.

Across these works, a recurring theme is that purely fixed-rate schemes such as uniform quantization [arxiv_2003.03564] or sparse gradient transmission [arxiv_1903.02891] leave substantial coding gains on the table. By contrast, methods grounded in entropy coding, variable-length representations, or information-theoretic rate-distortion analysis [arxiv_2204.10985, arxiv_2301.09269, arxiv_2402.03770] more closely approach the fundamental limits of compression, though often at the cost of increased encoder complexity. The optimal balance between coding sophistication and computational overhead at resource-constrained clients remains an open challenge that motivates continued research at the intersection of source coding theory and federated learning system design.
