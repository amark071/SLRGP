# Federated Learning

### Privacy and Security Mechanisms in Federated Learning

Privacy and security represent foundational concerns in federated learning, given that even the exchange of model updates—rather than raw data—can expose sensitive information about participants' local datasets. Two principal mechanisms have emerged to address these concerns: differential privacy (DP) and secure aggregation, which are frequently deployed in combination to provide complementary guarantees.

Differential privacy introduces calibrated noise into the learning process to bound the statistical influence any individual data point can exert on the final model. Heikkilä [arxiv_2407.19286] examines the interaction between DP and secure aggregation in federated settings that involve multiple local gradient steps, noting that fine-grained protection granularities complicate the straightforward combination of these two techniques. Similarly, Hu, Guo, and Gong [arxiv_2003.13761] propose a concentrated differentially private framework designed to preserve model utility while protecting local data across communication rounds in which edge devices perform multiple steps of stochastic gradient descent before uploading results to a central server. Both works thus grapple with the tension between privacy guarantees and model performance, though Heikkilä's analysis places particular emphasis on the structural challenges introduced when secure aggregation is layered on top of DP in multi-step local training regimes, whereas Hu et al. focus more directly on the utility-privacy trade-off through the lens of concentrated DP.

Secure aggregation protocols constitute a complementary line of defence by ensuring that the server learns only the aggregate of client updates rather than any individual contribution. Jeon et al. [arxiv_2012.07183] develop a privacy-preserving decentralised aggregation protocol that avoids the costs and risks associated with centralised data collection, formulating the aggregation problem in a manner that prevents individual sites from revealing privacy-sensitive information. This decentralised orientation contrasts with the server-centric architectures assumed in [arxiv_2407.19286] and [arxiv_2003.13761], illustrating that secure aggregation can be designed for topologically diverse deployment scenarios.

Evaluating these mechanisms in practice presents its own challenges. Mugunthan, Peraire-Bueno, and Kagal [arxiv_2002.08423] address this gap by introducing PrivacyFL, a simulator specifically designed for privacy-preserving and secure federated learning environments. They observe that, although federated learning inherently reduces data privacy risks by keeping training data localised, residual leakage through model weights and parameters remains a concern—precisely the threat that DP and secure aggregation seek to mitigate. The availability of such simulation infrastructure is important because it enables systematic comparison of privacy mechanisms under controlled conditions, supporting the broader research programme represented by the other works reviewed here.

### Decentralized and Peer-to-Peer Federated Learning

#### Surveys and Overviews of Decentralized Federated Learning

The growing recognition of centralized federated learning's structural vulnerabilities has prompted substantial scholarly attention toward decentralized and peer-to-peer alternatives. Several surveys and overviews have sought to map this emerging landscape, each offering a distinct analytical lens through which the transition from server-dependent to fully distributed architectures can be understood.

A foundational observation shared across this literature is that conventional federated learning, while privacy-preserving in principle, introduces a single point of failure by routing all model updates through a central coordinator. [arxiv_2308.04604] frames this as a matter of trust concentration, arguing that decentralized federated learning (DFL) removes the coordinator entirely and replaces client-server orchestration with peer-to-peer coordination, rendering learning dynamics topology-dependent rather than server-dependent. This structural reframing is echoed by [arxiv_2306.02570], which situates DFL within the broader tradition of decentralized optimization, noting that most existing federated learning approaches remain confined to centralized settings that are inherently fragile and that the intersection of decentralized optimization and federated learning offers a principled path toward greater resilience and communication efficiency.

Where [arxiv_2306.02570] emphasizes the algorithmic continuity between decentralized optimization theory and federated practice, [arxiv_2503.07505] advances a more conceptually ambitious argument: that the fundamental difference between centralized FL (CFL) and decentralized FL (DFL) is not merely one of network topology but reflects divergent underlying training protocols. This perspective is notable for reframing the CFL-to-DFL transition as a qualitative rather than purely architectural shift, with distinct implications for privacy guarantees and robustness properties. By foregrounding the training protocol as the locus of difference, [arxiv_2503.07505] provides a more theoretically grounded taxonomy than surveys that treat topology as the primary distinguishing variable.

Complementing these broad overviews, [arxiv_2503.13624] acknowledges that neither fully centralized nor fully decentralized architectures are universally optimal, motivating semi-decentralized frameworks as practical intermediaries suited to heterogeneous deployment environments such as IoT ecosystems. This acknowledgment of architectural plurality is consistent with the observation in [arxiv_2209.07124] that server-less federated learning approaches have emerged in the literature precisely to address the scalability and security issues inherent in central-server designs, though at the cost of increased coordination complexity among peers.

Taken together, these surveys and overviews establish that decentralized federated learning is not a monolithic paradigm but a family of approaches united by the rejection of central coordination, differentiated by topology, protocol, and deployment context, and motivated by a shared concern with the fragility and trust assumptions embedded in conventional federated architectures [arxiv_2308.04604][arxiv_2306.02570][arxiv_2503.07505].

#### Byzantine Resilience and Robustness in Decentralized Settings

Decentralized and peer-to-peer federated learning architectures eliminate the central server that conventional FL relies upon, distributing the coordination of model training across participating nodes. While this shift addresses well-known bottlenecks such as single-point-of-failure and communication congestion [arxiv_2406.10416], it simultaneously introduces new and distinct vulnerabilities to Byzantine adversaries — participants who may broadcast arbitrary or malicious model updates to corrupt the global model [arxiv_2409.17754]. Understanding and mitigating these threats in fully decentralized settings has therefore become an active area of research.

A foundational challenge is that Byzantine resilience mechanisms developed for server-assisted FL do not transfer straightforwardly to peer-to-peer topologies. Fang et al. [arxiv_2406.10416] propose a Byzantine-robust decentralized FL framework that explicitly accounts for the absence of a trusted aggregator, while Guo et al. [arxiv_2002.08569] address Byzantine fault tolerance in decentralized stochastic gradient descent by enabling each node to defend locally against corrupted neighbors. Similarly, Fang, Yang, and Bajwa [arxiv_1908.08098] introduce BRIDGE, a Byzantine-resilient decentralized gradient descent algorithm designed for multi-agent settings where no central parameter server exists. These works collectively demonstrate that robustness in decentralized settings must be engineered at the level of individual node interactions rather than at a single aggregation point.

The role of network topology in determining Byzantine resilience has received dedicated attention. Bhattacharya et al. [arxiv_2407.05141] empirically investigate how different peer-to-peer graph structures affect a system's susceptibility to Byzantine attacks, finding that topology is a non-trivial determinant of robustness. This complements the theoretical analysis of Raynal et al. [arxiv_2303.03829], who introduce novel attacks against decentralized learning that can steer network convergence toward arbitrary or backdoored models, and who critically examine whether decentralized learning is inherently more robust than federated learning — concluding that the answer depends heavily on the specific threat model and network configuration.

Beyond attack modeling, several works focus on robust aggregation schemes tailored to decentralized environments. Cajaraville-Aboy et al. [arxiv_2409.17754] propose Byzantine-robust aggregation mechanisms specifically designed for decentralized FL, addressing the challenge of optimizing both security and convergence without a central coordinator. Ghavamipour et al. [arxiv_2404.17970] extend this line of work by combining Byzantine-robustness with privacy-preserving aggregation, recognizing that the two properties must be jointly satisfied in realistic peer-to-peer deployments, where the absence of a server means that clients are simultaneously potential adversaries and privacy stakeholders.

Taken together, these contributions reveal that Byzantine resilience in decentralized and peer-to-peer federated learning is a multi-dimensional problem, shaped by network topology [arxiv_2407.05141], the sophistication of the adversarial threat model [arxiv_2303.03829], and the design of local aggregation protocols [arxiv_2002.08569; arxiv_1908.08098; arxiv_2406.10416; arxiv_2409.17754; arxiv_2404.17970]. The field is converging toward solutions that must simultaneously guarantee convergence, robustness, and privacy in the absence of any trusted central authority.
