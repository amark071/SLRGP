# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past several years, resulting in a growing body of survey and overview literature that collectively maps the field's foundational principles, practical challenges, and future directions. At its core, federated learning addresses a fundamental tension in modern machine learning: data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across end-user devices, organizations, and geographic regions, yet legal and regulatory constraints often prohibit the direct sharing of such data [arxiv_2104.14362]. Rather than centralizing raw data, federated learning enables collaborative model training by exchanging only locally trained models or computed gradients, thereby preserving the privacy of each participating party [arxiv_2301.01299].

Several surveys have sought to situate federated learning within the broader landscape of distributed machine learning. Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] trace an explicit lineage from classical distributed machine learning paradigms to the federated setting, emphasizing how the shift toward edge devices and organizational boundaries necessitates new algorithmic and systems-level thinking. This historical framing complements the more contemporary treatment offered by Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299], whose systematic survey focuses on recent advances and characterizes federated learning as an effective paradigm for privacy-preserving collaborative learning, cataloguing developments in aggregation strategies, personalization, and robustness.

Beyond these broad overviews, specialized surveys have examined particular technical bottlenecks that constrain federated learning in practice. One prominent challenge concerns data heterogeneity: because participating clients generate data through different processes and environments, the local datasets are frequently non-independent and non-identically distributed (non-IID). Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a dedicated analysis of this problem, documenting how non-IID conditions systematically degrade model performance relative to centralized training and surveying the mitigation strategies that have been proposed in response. A complementary line of inquiry addresses communication efficiency. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2208.01200] observe that while federated learning avoids transmitting raw data—thereby reducing privacy risk—the iterative exchange of model updates between clients and a central server introduces substantial communication overhead, and their survey reviews contemporary techniques for compressing, quantizing, and scheduling these transmissions.

Taken together, these surveys reveal that federated learning research has matured from establishing foundational concepts toward tackling concrete systems challenges. The convergence of privacy preservation, statistical heterogeneity, and communication constraints defines the central research agenda, and the surveyed literature collectively provides the conceptual scaffolding upon which more specialized contributions in this thesis are built.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

#### Privacy-Utility Tradeoffs and Accuracy Optimization in DP-FL

The tension between privacy guarantees and model accuracy represents one of the central challenges in differentially private federated learning (DP-FL), and a growing body of research has sought to characterize and mitigate this tradeoff through a variety of complementary strategies.

A foundational concern is the degradation in model performance introduced by the noise mechanisms required for differential privacy. [arxiv_2003.13761] addresses this directly by proposing a concentrated differentially private approach that aims to preserve utility while providing formal privacy guarantees during the federated aggregation process, recognizing that the repeated injection of noise across communication rounds compounds accuracy loss. Similarly, [arxiv_2203.03106] investigates the root causes of performance degradation under user-level DP, identifying gradient perturbation and the resulting instability in local updates as key contributors. Their proposed remedy combines local regularization with gradient sparsification, reducing the effective noise impact without sacrificing the formal privacy guarantee. Together, these two works highlight that noise mechanism design and gradient-level interventions are complementary axes along which the privacy-utility tradeoff can be managed.

Beyond noise calibration, system-level and client-selection strategies have emerged as important levers. [arxiv_2304.05127] frames the problem as one of balancing privacy and performance at the system level, examining how the configuration of FL algorithms—including local computation cycles and communication rounds—affects the overall tradeoff. Extending this perspective to heterogeneous settings, [arxiv_2408.08642] proposes a biased client selection mechanism that accounts for the fact that different clients may operate under heterogeneous differential privacy requirements. By strategically selecting clients whose privacy budgets and data characteristics are better suited to the current training phase, their approach demonstrates that the aggregate noise injected into the global model can be reduced, thereby improving accuracy without weakening individual privacy protections.

A distinct but related line of inquiry concerns whether personalization can structurally improve the privacy-accuracy tradeoff. [arxiv_2202.05318] argues that optimizing for personalized local models alongside a shared global model under user-level DP constraints yields better accuracy than purely global approaches, because personalization reduces the sensitivity of each user's contribution to the global update. This finding is particularly significant because it suggests that architectural choices in the federated learning objective—not only noise mechanisms—can reshape the fundamental tradeoff curve. Taken together, these works collectively demonstrate that improving accuracy under DP-FL requires coordinated attention to noise design [arxiv_2003.13761][arxiv_2203.03106], system configuration [arxiv_2304.05127], client heterogeneity [arxiv_2408.08642], and model personalization [arxiv_2202.05318].

#### Communication Efficiency and Compression in DP-FL

The intersection of differential privacy and communication efficiency in federated learning represents one of the most active and technically demanding areas of current research, as both constraints impose costs on model utility and must be managed jointly rather than in isolation.

A foundational tension in this space is that the standard techniques used to reduce communication overhead — including model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation — tend to increase training variance, which compounds the utility degradation already introduced by privacy noise [arxiv_2101.12240]. This observation motivates a line of work that seeks mechanisms capable of satisfying both constraints simultaneously without incurring prohibitive accuracy losses.

One prominent approach involves the application of local differential privacy (LDP) via Gaussian mechanisms to the gradient updates shared during federated stochastic gradient descent. Kim et al. [arxiv_2102.04737] analyze the resulting three-way trade-off among privacy, utility, and communication, demonstrating that quantization of the privatized gradients can be jointly optimized with the noise level to navigate this trade-off more efficiently. Their framework highlights that compression and privacy noise are not independent sources of degradation but interact in ways that must be modeled carefully.

Building on the distributed mean estimation (DME) perspective, Girgis and Diggavi [arxiv_2302.11152] propose a communication-efficient private DME scheme grounded in the multi-message shuffled (MMS) privacy framework. By leveraging the shuffling model — in which a trusted or semi-trusted shuffler anonymizes client messages before aggregation — their approach achieves tighter privacy amplification than pure LDP while simultaneously constraining the number of bits transmitted per client. This work thus bridges the shuffled privacy literature with the communication compression literature, offering a more favorable privacy-utility-communication frontier than mechanisms relying solely on local randomization.

A complementary strategy is pursued by Salehi et al. [arxiv_2409.13133], who introduce CorBin-FL, a mechanism that exploits correlated randomness shared among clients to achieve differential privacy guarantees. By coordinating the stochastic binning process across participants, CorBin-FL reduces the magnitude of noise each individual client must inject, thereby improving utility relative to independent LDP mechanisms while maintaining rigorous privacy accounting and bounded communication cost. This use of common randomness represents a structurally different approach from the shuffling model, yet both [arxiv_2302.11152] and [arxiv_2409.13133] share the insight that introducing structured dependencies among client-side randomness can relax the privacy-communication-utility trade-off that purely local mechanisms face.

Taken together, these works illustrate a clear trajectory in the field: from treating privacy and communication as separate constraints [arxiv_2101.12240] toward designing unified mechanisms — whether through shuffling [arxiv_2302.11152], correlated randomness [arxiv_2409.13133], or joint quantization-noise optimization [arxiv_2102.04737] — that address both simultaneously.
