# Federated Learning

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent distributed machine learning paradigm in which edge devices collaboratively train a shared model under the coordination of a central server, without requiring participants to share their raw local data [arxiv_2003.13761]. At each communication round, devices perform local stochastic gradient descent updates before uploading results to the server for aggregation [arxiv_2003.13761]. Despite its privacy-preserving intent, this architecture introduces well-documented vulnerabilities: a malicious aggregation server may exploit uploaded model parameters to infer sensitive information about participants' training data [arxiv_2201.07730]. Addressing these vulnerabilities has motivated a substantial body of work combining differential privacy (DP) and cryptographic secure aggregation techniques, though these approaches frequently impose significant computational or utility costs [arxiv_2201.07730].

A recurring challenge in federated learning concerns the statistical heterogeneity of local data. When training data are not independently and identically distributed (Non-IID) across devices, model performance degrades considerably relative to centralized training [arxiv_2106.06843]. Surveys of this problem have catalogued the various forms of data heterogeneity and the algorithmic strategies proposed to mitigate their effects, underscoring that Non-IID data remains one of the most consequential open problems in the field [arxiv_2106.06843].

Beyond statistical challenges, the conventional centralized federated learning topology itself presents structural risks, as reliance on a single aggregation server creates a single point of failure and a concentrated privacy threat [arxiv_2306.02570]. This has motivated growing interest in fully decentralized, peer-to-peer federated learning architectures that eliminate the central coordinator. Jeon et al. develop a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that formulates aggregation as a distributed optimization problem [arxiv_2012.07183], while Lu et al. extend this line of work to dynamic, time-varying communication graphs characteristic of high-mobility environments, proposing the first privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithm for such settings [arxiv_2210.00325]. Abd El-Kareem et al. further examine the trade-off between privacy and utility when combining decentralized aggregation with differential privacy in peer-to-peer machine learning scenarios [arxiv_2311.16008].

The interaction between secure aggregation and differential privacy has received particular scrutiny when multiple local gradient steps are performed before aggregation. Heikkilä demonstrates that fine-grained protection granularities, such as per-step privacy accounting, complicate the straightforward combination of these two techniques [arxiv_2407.19286]. Complementing this, the SCOTCH framework proposes an efficient secure computation approach specifically designed to reduce the overhead typically associated with cryptographic aggregation in differentially private federated learning [arxiv_2201.07730]. Collectively, these works illustrate that the federated learning literature is converging on the need to jointly optimize for privacy, communication efficiency, and model utility across both centralized and decentralized deployment settings.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

Federated learning has attracted sustained scholarly attention as a paradigm for privacy-preserving collaborative model training, and a cluster of works occupying the middle tier of relevance elaborates on both its foundational mechanisms and its persistent technical challenges. A systematic survey by Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299] situates federated learning as an effective paradigm for privacy-preserving collaboration, emphasising that only locally trained models or computed gradients are exchanged rather than raw data, thereby limiting direct exposure of sensitive information. This architectural choice, while privacy-enhancing in principle, introduces a well-documented vulnerability: gradient interchange itself can leak private information. This concern is taken up directly by Liu et al. [arxiv_2203.17005], whose survey of privacy-preserving aggregation methods documents the growing academic and industrial interest in closing the gap between federated learning's privacy promise and its practical limitations, noting that aggregation represents a critical attack surface.

The tension between privacy guarantees and system practicality is further explored in work on secure aggregation protocols. Xu et al. [arxiv_2501.05053] propose TAPFed, a threshold-based secure aggregation scheme designed to address privacy leakage arising from gradient exchange in traditional federated learning platforms. Their contribution is notable because it targets a structural weakness that purely architectural solutions—such as keeping data local—cannot resolve on their own. Compared with the survey-level treatment offered by Liu et al. [arxiv_2203.17005], which catalogues existing aggregation strategies broadly, TAPFed [arxiv_2501.05053] offers a concrete cryptographic mechanism, illustrating how the field has progressed from problem identification toward targeted protocol design.

Collectively, these works reflect a maturing research agenda. The survey by Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299] provides the broader context of recent advances, mapping the landscape of federated learning challenges including data heterogeneity and communication overhead, while the aggregation-focused contributions [arxiv_2203.17005, arxiv_2501.05053] drill into one of the most consequential sub-problems. Together they underscore a recurring theme in the federated learning literature: that privacy cannot be treated as an inherent property of the distributed architecture alone but must be actively engineered at the level of the protocols governing model update exchange. This insight connects naturally to wider discussions of communication efficiency and system-level constraints that appear throughout the broader federated learning literature, reinforcing the view that security, privacy, and efficiency must be co-designed rather than addressed in isolation.
