# Neural Architecture Search

### Survey and Overview of NAS

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a prominent research area driven by the recognition that architectural design choices are among the most consequential determinants of deep learning performance. As noted by Wistuba, Rawat, and colleagues, the growing interest in both the automation of machine learning and deep learning has inevitably led to the development of a wide variety of automated methods for neural architecture search, with many advances in deep learning springing directly from architectural improvements [arxiv_1905.01392]. This observation underscores the motivation for systematizing and automating what was previously a labor-intensive, expert-driven process.

At a high level, the NAS landscape can be organized around a small number of dominant paradigms. Kim and colleagues provide a useful taxonomy, classifying the main research trends into neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches [arxiv_2108.08474]. This tripartite classification reflects the field's evolution from biologically inspired search heuristics toward more sample-efficient gradient-based and proxy-model strategies. The same work situates NAS within the broader goal of finding optimal or near-optimal neural network models, a challenge that has attracted sustained attention across a wide range of application domains [arxiv_2108.08474].

Among these paradigms, reinforcement learning has received particularly focused treatment. Shao's dedicated survey on RL-based NAS highlights that the structures and hyperparameters of deep neural network architectures make a substantial difference to task performance, and that the process of exploring optimal configurations has historically involved considerable tedious human intervention [arxiv_2409.18163]. RL-based approaches address this by framing architecture design as a sequential decision-making problem, allowing an agent to learn policies that generate high-performing architectures with reduced manual effort [arxiv_2409.18163].

A further dimension of complexity arises when NAS is extended beyond single-objective optimization. Shariatzadeh and colleagues observe that earlier NAS attempts focused exclusively on prediction accuracy, but that Multi-Objective Neural Architecture Search has since emerged to jointly optimize competing criteria such as accuracy, computational cost, and memory footprint [arxiv_2307.09099]. This development reflects the practical reality that deployed models must satisfy hardware and latency constraints alongside predictive performance requirements, and it connects NAS tightly to the broader fields of Hyperparameter Optimization and AutoML [arxiv_2307.09099]. Taken together, these surveys illustrate a field that has matured from narrow accuracy-focused search into a rich, multi-dimensional optimization problem spanning diverse algorithmic strategies and deployment contexts.

### Differentiable and Continuous Search Methods

Differentiable and continuous neural architecture search (NAS) methods emerged as a response to the well-documented inefficiencies of discrete search strategies. Early NAS approaches grounded in reinforcement learning (RL) or evolutionary algorithms operate over discrete architecture spaces, a characteristic that renders them computationally prohibitive when scaling to complex search problems [arxiv_1808.07233]. To address this limitation, Luo, Tian, and colleagues proposed mapping the discrete architecture space into a continuous, low-dimensional embedding, enabling gradient-based optimization to navigate the search landscape far more efficiently than prior combinatorial methods [arxiv_1808.07233]. This foundational insight — that architecture design can be reformulated as a continuous optimization problem — underpins the broader family of differentiable NAS (DNAS) techniques.

The rise of DNAS, and in particular the popularity of DARTS as one of its earliest prominent instantiations, catalyzed a substantial body of follow-on research. As surveyed by Heuillet and colleagues, DNAS methods are faster than RL- or evolutionary-algorithm-based counterparts by several orders of magnitude, which explains their rapid adoption as the dominant paradigm for automated network design [arxiv_2304.05405]. The central mechanism shared across DNAS approaches is the relaxation of the discrete architecture selection problem into a differentiable hyperparameter optimization, allowing architecture parameters and network weights to be jointly optimized via gradient descent [arxiv_2304.05405].

Nevertheless, gradient-based methods are not without their own shortcomings. Guo, Wu, and colleagues highlight that a persistent challenge in NAS more broadly — including differentiable variants — is the requirement to train a large number of candidate architectures, a burden that is particularly acute when targeting deployment on embedded and mobile platforms [arxiv_2103.02126]. Their work on differentiable neural architecture learning specifically targets efficient network design under such resource constraints, situating DNAS within the practical context of hardware-aware optimization [arxiv_2103.02126].

A complementary critique is raised by Mills and colleagues, who question the effectiveness and generalizability of gradient methods when applied to non-convex architecture optimization landscapes [arxiv_2109.12425]. While differentiable search converts the problem into one amenable to gradient descent, the non-convexity of the resulting objective can undermine solution quality. In response, their L²NAS framework proposes leveraging continuous-action reinforcement learning as an alternative optimizer over the continuous architecture space, retaining the benefits of continuous parameterization while circumventing some failure modes of pure gradient-based approaches [arxiv_2109.12425]. Taken together, these works illustrate both the appeal and the ongoing limitations of continuous and differentiable formulations in neural architecture search.
