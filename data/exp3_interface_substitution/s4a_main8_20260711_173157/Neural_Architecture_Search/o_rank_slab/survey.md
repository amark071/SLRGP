# Neural Architecture Search

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-8

Neural architecture search (NAS) has emerged as a prominent research direction driven by the recognition that network architecture choices are critical determinants of deep learning performance, even as the computational demands of discovering such architectures remain substantial [arxiv_1905.01392]. Early NAS methods operated predominantly in discrete search spaces using reinforcement learning (RL) or evolutionary algorithms (EA), both of which suffer from high inefficiency due to the combinatorial nature of the problem [arxiv_1808.07233]. To address this limitation, continuous relaxation strategies have been proposed, most notably the approach of Neural Architecture Optimization, which maps discrete architectures into a continuous embedding space amenable to gradient-based optimization [arxiv_1808.07233]. However, differentiable architecture search methods that reformulate the problem as hyperparameter optimization via gradient descent have themselves attracted scrutiny, with concerns raised about their effectiveness and generalizability on non-convex architecture landscapes [arxiv_2109.12425]. In response, L²NAS reframes architecture search as a continuous-action reinforcement learning problem, aiming to overcome the shortcomings of purely gradient-driven approaches [arxiv_2109.12425].

A complementary challenge arises when NAS must operate under constrained data and compute budgets, as is typical in meta-learning scenarios. The AT-NAS framework addresses this by combining gradient-based meta-learning with EA-based NAS to enable knowledge transfer across tasks, thereby reducing the resource requirements that ordinarily prerequisite successful architecture search [arxiv_2110.05842]. This cross-task perspective contrasts with single-task NAS pipelines and reflects a broader trend toward more sample- and compute-efficient search strategies.

Survey-level analyses have sought to organize the rapidly expanding NAS literature into coherent taxonomies. One influential classification distinguishes neuro-evolutionary algorithms, RL-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches, arguing that each category offers distinct trade-offs in search efficiency and result quality [arxiv_2108.08474]. A more focused survey concentrating on RL-based NAS traces how automation of architecture and hyperparameter selection has evolved in response to the tedious human intervention traditionally required [arxiv_2409.18163]. An important practical insight emerging from empirical work is that architectural choices and hyperparameter settings interact non-trivially, rendering the common practice of separating NAS from hyperparameter tuning suboptimal; joint search over both spaces, even with limited training epochs, yields superior results [arxiv_1807.06906]. Building on the RL paradigm, recent scalable approaches train an RL agent to search for a distribution of good architectures rather than a single optimum, demonstrating competitive performance against strong baselines on standard benchmarks such as NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 [arxiv_2410.01431]. Collectively, these works illustrate a field progressively moving from computationally prohibitive discrete search toward efficient, generalizable, and jointly optimized architecture discovery frameworks.

### Rank slab 2: positions 9-15

The literature in this slab addresses a cluster of interconnected challenges in neural architecture search, broadly concerned with search efficiency, search space definition, and the limitations of locality in gradient-based methods.

A recurring concern across these works is the computational burden inherent to NAS and the strategies devised to mitigate it. Siddiqui et al. [arxiv_2502.03553] observe that while NAS has demonstrated considerable promise for automating network design, its practical utility is constrained by the training costs associated with evaluating large numbers of candidate architectures. Their work distinguishes between modular search strategies, which restrict exploration to repeating building blocks, and global search, which considers the entire architecture simultaneously, arguing that the latter offers richer representational possibilities despite its greater expense. This tension between search scope and computational feasibility is similarly acknowledged by Pouy et al. [arxiv_2310.17669], who emphasize that identifying an optimized architecture is a time-consuming task and that the definition of the search space itself is a critical and underappreciated design decision. Their contribution focuses on principled methods for constraining the search space so that practitioners with limited domain expertise can still obtain competitive architectures, addressing a practical bottleneck that purely algorithmic improvements do not resolve.

Within the differentiable NAS paradigm, Bodin et al. [arxiv_2104.10450] identify a distinct pathology: the tendency of DARTS-based methods to converge to locally suboptimal or outright detrimental architectures, a phenomenon they term performance collapse. They argue that existing remedies address only surface symptoms and that the underlying issue is the inherently local nature of the gradient-based search, which prevents adequate exploration of the architecture space. This diagnosis complements the efficiency-oriented framing of Siddiqui et al. [arxiv_2502.03553] and Pouy et al. [arxiv_2310.17669] by highlighting that speed gains achieved through differentiable relaxations may come at the cost of solution quality if the optimization landscape is poorly navigated. Together, these three works collectively suggest that the field faces a dual challenge: reducing wall-clock search time while simultaneously ensuring that the architectures discovered are globally competitive rather than locally convenient.

Taken as a group, the papers in this slab reflect a maturing recognition that early NAS research, which celebrated dramatic reductions in search cost relative to reinforcement learning and evolutionary baselines, may have underestimated structural problems in how search spaces are defined and how gradient signals propagate through them. The contributions of Bodin et al. [arxiv_2104.10450], Pouy et al. [arxiv_2310.17669], and Siddiqui et al. [arxiv_2502.03553] each probe a different facet of this broader problem, suggesting that robust NAS methodology requires simultaneous attention to search scope, space design, and optimization dynamics rather than optimizing any single dimension in isolation.
