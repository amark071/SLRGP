# Neural Architecture Search

### Surveys and Overviews of NAS

The automation of neural network design has emerged as a major research direction within deep learning, driven by the recognition that architectural choices critically determine model performance. Early survey work by Wistuba, Rawat, and colleagues [arxiv_1905.01392] situates Neural Architecture Search (NAS) within the broader context of automated machine learning, noting that while deep learning has produced remarkable advances, the computational intensity of manually designing architectures motivates systematic automated approaches. This foundational overview captures the field at a moment of rapid expansion, cataloguing the diverse methods that had emerged to address the challenge of architecture optimization.

Building on such broad characterizations, Kim and colleagues [arxiv_2108.08474] offer a more structured taxonomy of NAS research trends, organizing the landscape into three principal paradigms: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. This tripartite classification reflects a consensus that had solidified in the community by the early 2020s, and the authors additionally foreground the question of computational efficiency, framing acceleration of the search process as a central concern motivating methodological innovation. Their emphasis on trends and trajectory complements the more descriptive stance of earlier surveys, shifting attention toward where the field was heading rather than merely cataloguing what had been accomplished.

Within this broader landscape, reinforcement learning-based NAS has attracted dedicated survey attention. Shao [arxiv_2409.18163] examines this subfield specifically, arguing that RL-based methods represent a principled response to the challenge of navigating large and complex architecture spaces, where the search for optimal structures and hyperparameters would otherwise require extensive and tedious human intervention. This focused treatment highlights the degree to which NAS has matured into distinct methodological communities, each warranting its own systematic review.

A particularly significant development captured in the survey literature is the rise of differentiable NAS. Heuillet and colleagues [arxiv_2304.05405] document how Differentiable Neural Architecture Search (DNAS) rapidly became the dominant paradigm, driven largely by the influence of DARTS. Crucially, they contrast DNAS with the RL-based and evolutionary approaches surveyed elsewhere, noting that differentiable methods are faster by several orders of magnitude—a distinction that explains the approach's rapid adoption. Taken together, these surveys reveal a field evolving from broad exploratory methods toward increasingly efficient and specialized techniques, with computational cost serving as a persistent organizing concern across the literature.

### Reinforcement Learning-based NAS

Reinforcement learning-based neural architecture search (RL-based NAS) has emerged as one of the most prominent paradigms for automating the design of deep neural network architectures. In this framework, an RL agent—typically a recurrent controller—learns to propose architectures by treating architecture generation as a sequential decision-making problem, receiving validation performance as a reward signal [arxiv_2409.18163]. Comprehensive surveys have documented the rapid proliferation of such methods, noting that the critical role of architecture choice in deep learning has motivated substantial investment in automated search strategies [arxiv_1905.01392].

Despite their strong empirical results, early RL-based NAS methods are widely criticized for their prohibitive computational demands, often requiring hundreds of GPU-days to converge [arxiv_1707.04873]. Several lines of work have sought to address this bottleneck. Cai et al. [arxiv_1707.04873] proposed reusing and transforming existing networks rather than training each candidate from scratch, substantially reducing the cost of exploration. Complementarily, Liu et al. [arxiv_2105.11694] identified the rollout bottleneck—exhaustive proxy-task training for each sampled architecture—as a key source of inefficiency, and introduced an uncertainty-aware pipeline to accelerate convergence. Nagy and Boros [arxiv_2110.06751] similarly focused on improving sample efficiency, arguing that better use of limited architecture evaluations is essential for practical deployment.

Beyond raw efficiency, researchers have explored modifications to the search process itself. Lee et al. [arxiv_1910.10397] proposed decoupling the search over network structure from the search over operations, sampling each from independent multinomial distributions, which they demonstrated improves search efficiency compared to conventional joint-search approaches. Mills et al. [arxiv_2109.12425] took a different angle by reformulating architecture search as a continuous-action RL problem, arguing that gradient-based differentiable methods face generalizability issues on non-convex architecture spaces and that continuous-action RL offers a more principled alternative. Ding et al. [arxiv_2001.06679] introduced a broad scalable architecture combined with parameter sharing inspired by ENAS, achieving fast training through shallow topologies. Cassimon et al. [arxiv_2410.01431] further examined scalability by training an RL agent to search across rather than for a single architecture, benchmarking against strong baselines on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301.

Controller training strategies have also received dedicated attention. Choi et al. [arxiv_2103.08157] proposed pretraining the RL controller using a locality-based self-supervised objective that exploits structural similarity among architectures, reducing the computational overhead associated with learning from scratch. Meanwhile, Luo et al. [arxiv_1808.07233] argued that conducting search in discrete spaces—as most RL-based methods do—is inherently inefficient, and advocated for embedding architectures in a continuous latent space, though this approach blurs the boundary between RL-based and gradient-based NAS.

RL-based NAS has also been extended to specialized domains. Gao et al. [arxiv_2006.04027] applied it to continual learning, where the agent must balance plasticity and stability across sequential tasks. Yang et al. [arxiv_2204.07615] addressed tabular datasets, a domain largely overlooked by image-centric NAS methods, incorporating rejection sampling to enforce resource constraints during the RL-guided search. Together, these works illustrate both the versatility and the ongoing computational challenges that characterize the RL-based NAS literature.
