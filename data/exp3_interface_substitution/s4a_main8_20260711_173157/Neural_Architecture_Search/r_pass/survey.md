# Neural Architecture Search

### Surveys and Overviews of NAS

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a prominent research area aimed at automating the design of deep neural network architectures, reducing reliance on costly and time-consuming manual engineering. Early surveys established the foundational landscape of the field: [arxiv_1905.01392] provided one of the first comprehensive overviews, noting that the growing interest in both the automation of machine learning and deep learning had inevitably led to a wide variety of automated methods for architecture search, while also acknowledging that deep learning techniques are computationally intensive, making the search process a significant practical challenge. This observation set the stage for subsequent work focused on improving the efficiency and scalability of NAS methods.

More recent overviews have traced the evolution of the field from its origins in expert-driven design toward fully automated, algorithm-driven optimization. [arxiv_2403.17012] offers a comprehensive account of this trajectory, emphasizing NAS's expansion into diverse application domains such as medical imaging and natural language processing, and highlighting how the shift away from manual design has broadened the accessibility and applicability of deep learning. This evolutionary perspective complements the earlier survey of [arxiv_1905.01392] by situating NAS within the broader context of AutoML and demonstrating how the field has matured over time.

A recurring theme across the literature is the classification of NAS methods according to their underlying search strategies. [arxiv_2108.08474] organizes the main research trends into three categories—neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches—and examines how each contributes to the acceleration of the search process. This taxonomy is useful for understanding the trade-offs between search quality and computational cost that have driven much of the field's methodological innovation. Reinforcement learning-based approaches, in particular, have received dedicated attention: [arxiv_2409.18163] surveys this subfield specifically, noting that while deep learning has successfully automated feature extraction, the exploration of optimal network structures and hyperparameters has historically required substantial human intervention, motivating the development of RL-driven search strategies.

Beyond single-objective optimization, the field has increasingly recognized that prediction accuracy alone is an insufficient criterion for practical deployment. [arxiv_2307.09099] surveys multi-objective NAS, observing that earlier NAS efforts focused narrowly on accuracy but that subsequent work has incorporated additional objectives, connecting the field more explicitly to hyperparameter optimization and the broader AutoML paradigm. Together, these surveys reveal a field that has grown substantially in scope, moving from foundational automation toward nuanced, efficiency-aware, and multi-criteria optimization frameworks.

### Reinforcement Learning-Based NAS

#### RL-Based NAS: Core Methods and Frameworks

Reinforcement learning-based neural architecture search (RL-based NAS) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for automating the design of deep neural networks, with a growing body of work focused on refining its core mechanisms, improving scalability, and reducing computational overhead.

At the foundation of RL-based NAS lies the concept of a controller—typically a recurrent neural network—that generates architectural configurations and is trained via policy gradient methods using validation performance as a reward signal. While this framework has demonstrated strong empirical results, its practical adoption has been constrained by the substantial computational resources required to evaluate candidate architectures. Addressing this limitation, [arxiv_2110.06751] investigates strategies for improving the sample efficiency of RL-based NAS, recognizing that the large number of architecture evaluations needed during search represents a critical bottleneck. By reducing the number of samples required to identify high-performing architectures, such approaches make RL-based NAS more tractable in resource-constrained settings.

A complementary strategy for improving efficiency involves pretraining the controller prior to the main search phase. [arxiv_2103.08157] proposes a locality-based self-supervised pretraining scheme applicable to controller-based NAS frameworks. The method exploits structural similarities between architectures to define a pretraining objective, enabling the controller to develop a more informed initialization before engaging in the reward-driven search. This approach directly targets the high computational cost that has been widely criticized as an intrinsic limitation of NAS, and it is designed to be generally applicable across different controller-based systems.

Beyond efficiency, the design of the search space itself poses fundamental challenges for RL-based NAS. [arxiv_1909.03615] identifies that non-continuous and high-dimensional search spaces create significant optimization difficulties for RL controllers. To address this, the authors propose Neural Architecture Search in Embedding Space (NASES), which maps discrete architectural representations into a continuous embedding space, thereby facilitating smoother optimization and broadening the applicability of RL-based methods to more complex search scenarios.

Scalability represents another central concern in the field. [arxiv_2410.01431] evaluates a novel RL-based NAS solution designed not merely to return a single optimal architecture but to learn a generalizable search policy across architectures. Benchmarked on NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 against strong baselines, this work demonstrates that RL agents can be trained to navigate large search spaces in a scalable manner. Taken together, these contributions reflect a maturing research agenda in which the core RL-based NAS framework is being systematically refined along the axes of sample efficiency, controller initialization, search space representation, and scalability.

#### Improving Efficiency and Sample Complexity in RL-NAS

Early reinforcement learning-based neural architecture search methods demonstrated strong convergence properties but imposed prohibitive computational demands, a tension that has driven substantial methodological innovation. As noted in survey literature, RL-based NAS algorithms represent one of the three principal paradigms in the field, yet their practical deployment has long been constrained by the cost of evaluating candidate architectures [arxiv_2108.08474]. The broader trajectory of NAS research has consequently been shaped by efforts to close the gap between the theoretical appeal of RL-based search and its real-world feasibility [arxiv_2403.17012].

A central bottleneck is what has been termed the rollout problem: each sampled architecture must undergo exhaustive training on proxy tasks before a reward signal can be returned to the controller, making convergence slow and resource-intensive. FNAS addresses this directly by proposing an uncertainty-aware pipeline that accelerates rollout convergence, framing the inefficiency not as an inherent property of RL-NAS but as an engineering challenge amenable to principled probabilistic treatment [arxiv_2105.11694]. A complementary perspective is taken by work focused explicitly on sample efficiency, which argues that the number of architecture evaluations required by standard RL controllers can be substantially reduced through more informed sampling strategies, thereby lowering the barrier to applying RL-NAS in resource-constrained settings [arxiv_2110.06751].

The geometry of the search space itself contributes to inefficiency. High-dimensional, non-continuous spaces make optimization difficult for RL controllers, and one response has been to embed discrete architecture representations into a continuous latent space, enabling smoother gradient-like signals to guide the search [arxiv_1909.03615]. A related strategy recasts architecture selection as a continuous-action RL problem rather than a discrete one, arguing that continuous-action formulations avoid some of the non-convexity pitfalls that afflict both gradient-based differentiable search and standard discrete RL controllers [arxiv_2109.12425]. These two approaches converge on a shared intuition: relaxing the discrete structure of the search space can meaningfully improve the efficiency of RL-driven optimization.

Beyond search space reformulation, scalability has been addressed through controller design. Recent work proposes an RL agent trained to search for good architecture distributions rather than a single optimal point, demonstrating competitive performance against strong baselines on standard benchmarks while maintaining scalability [arxiv_2410.01431]. Transfer learning offers yet another lever: by reusing knowledge from previously searched tasks, an RL-based controller can reduce the number of fresh evaluations required on a new problem [arxiv_1710.10776]. Proxy-based evaluation methods further accelerate the pipeline by replacing full training with cheaper surrogate signals, with end-to-end optimization proxies representing a recent advance that avoids the limitations of predictor-based surrogates [arxiv_2509.05656]. Collectively, these contributions illustrate that improving efficiency in RL-NAS is a multi-front endeavour, requiring simultaneous attention to search space structure, controller design, evaluation cost, and knowledge reuse.
