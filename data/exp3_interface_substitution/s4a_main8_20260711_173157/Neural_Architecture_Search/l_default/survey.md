# Neural Architecture Search

### Surveys and Overviews of NAS

#### Surveys and Comprehensive Overviews of NAS

Neural architecture search has emerged as a major research direction in deep learning, motivated by the need to automate the design of high-performing neural architectures that would otherwise depend heavily on researchers' prior knowledge and experience [arxiv_2006.02903]. As the field has matured, several surveys and comprehensive overviews have been produced that attempt to synthesize its rapid growth, characterize its core challenges, and map out its evolving methodological landscape.

Among the most thorough of these overviews, Ren et al. [arxiv_2006.02903] provide a wide-ranging survey that frames NAS as a response to the limitations of manual architecture design, cataloguing the challenges researchers face and the solutions that have been proposed across diverse application domains. Their treatment emphasizes that the automatic representation capabilities driving deep learning's breakthroughs are intimately tied to architectural choices, making systematic search methods both practically important and scientifically compelling.

A complementary perspective is offered by Kim et al. [arxiv_2108.08474], who organize the NAS literature around three dominant research trends: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. Rather than providing an exhaustive taxonomy, their survey foregrounds the question of computational efficiency, framing the trajectory of the field as a sustained effort to accelerate search without sacrificing solution quality. This acceleration-oriented framing distinguishes their contribution from broader taxonomic surveys and reflects a practical concern that has increasingly shaped NAS research.

The most expansive of the recent overviews is that of White et al. [arxiv_2301.08727], whose synthesis draws on insights derived from reviewing approximately one thousand NAS papers. Their scope spans computer vision, natural language understanding, speech recognition, and reinforcement learning, reflecting the degree to which NAS has diffused across subfields of deep learning. By grounding their analysis in such a large corpus, White et al. are positioned to identify cross-cutting patterns, recurring pitfalls, and open problems that narrower surveys may overlook. Their work thus serves both as a reference compendium and as a critical assessment of where the field stands after a decade of intensive development.

Taken together, these three surveys reveal a field that has grown from early proof-of-concept demonstrations into a mature research area with well-defined sub-problems and a diverse methodological toolkit. While Ren et al. [arxiv_2006.02903] and Kim et al. [arxiv_2108.08474] provide structured entry points into the challenges and dominant algorithmic families, White et al. [arxiv_2301.08727] offer the broadest empirical grounding. Collectively, they establish the conceptual vocabulary and historical context necessary for situating more specialized contributions in gradient-based, evolutionary, weight-sharing, and meta-learning approaches to NAS.

#### Differentiable and Gradient-Based NAS Methods

Differentiable and gradient-based neural architecture search methods have emerged as a dominant paradigm within the broader NAS literature, largely because they reframe the combinatorial architecture selection problem as a continuous optimisation task amenable to standard gradient descent. The foundational approach, DARTS, constructs a differentiable search space through a continuous relaxation of discrete operation choices and jointly optimises model weights alongside architecture parameters in a weight-sharing supernet [arxiv_2108.04392]. This formulation dramatically reduces search cost compared with reinforcement learning or evolutionary strategies, and has consequently attracted substantial follow-on research [arxiv_1905.13577].

A central and recurring concern in this literature is performance collapse, whereby the search procedure converges to degenerate architectures dominated by parameter-free operations such as skip connections, pooling layers, or zero operations. Several independent analyses have identified this pathology and proposed distinct remedies. Hou et al. attribute collapse to an imbalance in operation competition and introduce Single-DARTS to stabilise the search [arxiv_2108.08128], while Movahedi et al. argue that the root cause lies in a lack of harmonisation across cells and propose Λ-DARTS to coordinate operation selection globally [arxiv_2210.07998]. Bodin et al. characterise the problem as excessive locality in the search trajectory and advocate for less local exploration strategies [arxiv_2104.10450], whereas Zhu et al. critique the reliance on Hessian eigenvalue regularisation and propose operation-level progressive search as a more principled alternative [arxiv_2302.05632].

Beyond stability, a second major thread concerns the accuracy of gradient estimation within the bilevel optimisation framework that underlies DARTS. He et al. demonstrate that gradient errors arising from second-order approximations cause the optimisation to fail to converge to locally optimal solutions, and propose a mixed-level reformulation to address this [arxiv_2003.12238]. Similarly, Zhang et al. introduce iDARTS, which employs stochastic implicit gradients to obtain more accurate and scalable estimates of the architecture gradient [arxiv_2106.10784]. Yao et al. approach the same problem through proximal iterations, improving convergence in the differentiable search space [arxiv_1905.13577], while Maile et al. recast DARTS as a constrained bilevel problem and show that regularisation and scheduling further improve optimisation quality [arxiv_2106.11655].

A third strand addresses the discretisation step, in which the continuous supernet is collapsed into a final discrete architecture. Wang et al. demonstrate that selecting operations solely on the basis of the largest architecture parameters is unreliable and propose alternative selection criteria [arxiv_2108.04392], a concern echoed by Yang et al., who introduce operation strength as a more faithful proxy for post-discretisation performance [arxiv_2409.14433]. Zhu et al. additionally employ self-distillation during the search stage to reduce the gap between supernet and stand-alone architecture performance [arxiv_2302.05629].

Complementary directions include stochastic relaxations, such as SNAS, which reformulates NAS as an optimisation over architecture distribution parameters trained end-to-end via backpropagation [arxiv_1812.09926], and UNAS, which combines differentiable NAS with reinforcement learning signals to permit optimisation of non-differentiable objectives [arxiv_1912.07651]. Search space limitations inherent to the original DARTS formulation have also been targeted; Heuillet et al. propose D-DARTS, which nests networks at the cell level to expand the accessible search space without abandoning weight-sharing efficiency [arxiv_2108.09306], and Zhang et al. augment gradient-based search with hardware-aware objectives to produce architectures suitable for resource-constrained devices [arxiv_2103.12529]. Collectively, these works illustrate that while differentiable NAS offers compelling efficiency advantages, substantial research effort continues to be directed at resolving its stability, optimisation, and discretisation shortcomings.

### Differentiable and Gradient-Based Architecture Search

Differentiable and gradient-based neural architecture search (NAS) emerged as a response to the computational inefficiency of earlier discrete search strategies. Methods grounded in reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms operate over discrete architecture spaces, a characteristic that renders the search process highly inefficient [arxiv_1808.07233]. To overcome this limitation, continuous relaxation techniques were introduced, enabling architecture parameters to be optimized via gradient descent rather than through costly discrete sampling. Luo et al. propose encoding architectures into a continuous, differentiable space so that standard optimization methods can be applied directly, substantially reducing the computational burden of the search [arxiv_1808.07233]. This paradigm shift from discrete to continuous search spaces represents one of the most consequential methodological advances in automated network design.

Building on this foundation, subsequent work has sought to extend differentiable NAS to practical deployment scenarios, particularly for resource-constrained embedded and mobile platforms. Guo et al. highlight that a central challenge in NAS remains the requirement to train a large number of candidate architectures, a problem that differentiable approaches partially mitigate by sharing parameters across the search space [arxiv_2103.02126]. Their differentiable neural architecture learning framework is specifically designed to improve efficiency in this regard, targeting settings where computational budgets are tightly constrained.

Despite these advances, the gradient-based formulation is not without its own limitations. Zhang et al. observe that architectures produced by existing differentiable NAS methods are typically optimized solely for classification accuracy, without adapting to the computational constraints of target hardware [arxiv_2103.12529]. Their enhanced gradient approach addresses this gap by incorporating hardware-aware objectives into the differentiable optimization process, thereby producing architectures that are both performant and deployable under resource restrictions. This represents an important refinement of the basic differentiable NAS paradigm, moving it closer to practical utility.

More fundamentally, questions have been raised about whether gradient descent is a reliable solver for the non-convex architecture optimization problems that differentiable NAS formulates. Mills et al. argue that converting architecture search into a hyperparameter optimization problem solved by gradient methods does not guarantee effective or generalizable solutions, given the non-convex nature of the underlying objective [arxiv_2109.12425]. This critique motivates hybrid approaches that combine the efficiency of continuous relaxation with alternative optimization strategies, suggesting that purely gradient-based methods may require further theoretical and empirical scrutiny before their limitations are fully resolved.
