# Neural Architecture Search

### Survey and Overview of NAS Methods

#### Surveys and Overviews of NAS Methods

## Surveys and Overviews of NAS Methods

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a central research direction within deep learning, driven by the recognition that network architecture design is a critical determinant of model performance. The growing complexity and diversity of NAS methods has prompted several comprehensive surveys aimed at systematically categorising and evaluating the field's progress.

An early and influential survey by Wistuba, Rawat et al. [arxiv_1905.01392] situates NAS within the broader context of automated machine learning, observing that the surge of interest in automating deep learning has produced a wide variety of architectural search methods. The authors emphasise that architectural choices are not merely secondary engineering decisions but are often the direct source of significant advances in deep learning, while simultaneously acknowledging the substantial computational burden that such techniques impose. This tension between performance gains and resource costs has remained a defining challenge for the field.

Building on this foundation, Kim et al. [arxiv_2108.08474] provide a more recent characterisation of NAS research trends, organising the landscape into three principal paradigms: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. This tripartite taxonomy reflects the historical evolution of the field, from biologically inspired optimisation strategies and reward-driven policy learning toward more parameter-sharing, efficiency-oriented frameworks. By mapping each approach's relative strengths and limitations, the authors offer a structured account of how the field has progressively sought to reduce the prohibitive search costs associated with earlier methods.

The computational efficiency concern identified in these broader surveys is addressed more directly by Liu, Zhang, and Jin [arxiv_2206.01520], whose survey focuses specifically on computationally efficient NAS. They highlight that, despite NAS's appeal in democratising deep learning for users without extensive expertise, the requirement for a large number of performance estimations during the search process renders many methods impractical. Their work thus frames efficiency not merely as a desirable property but as a prerequisite for wider adoption.

A complementary perspective is offered by Heuillet et al. [arxiv_2304.05405], who survey differentiable NAS (DNAS) methods, with particular attention to the influence of DARTS. In contrast to reinforcement learning and evolutionary approaches, DNAS reformulates the discrete architecture search problem as a continuous optimisation task, achieving speed improvements of several orders of magnitude. Taken together, these surveys illustrate a clear trajectory in NAS research: from computationally intensive, black-box search strategies toward gradient-based, highly efficient methods capable of scaling to practical deployment scenarios.

#### Differentiable and Gradient-Based NAS

## Differentiable and Gradient-Based Neural Architecture Search

Differentiable and gradient-based approaches to Neural Architecture Search (NAS) have emerged as a prominent paradigm for reducing the computational cost associated with discrete search strategies. By relaxing the discrete architecture selection problem into a continuous space, these methods enable the joint optimization of architecture parameters and network weights via standard gradient descent, yielding substantial improvements in search efficiency.

A foundational challenge in this family of methods concerns the formulation of the search problem as bilevel optimization, wherein architecture parameters and model weights constitute separate levels of an optimization hierarchy. As noted by [arxiv_2003.12238], many recently proposed NAS methods can be cast within this bilevel framework; however, efficient implementation requires approximations of second-order methods, and the authors demonstrate that gradient errors introduced by such approximations can lead to suboptimality, causing the optimization procedure to fail to converge to even a locally optimal solution. Their Mixed-Level reformulation (MiLeNAS) addresses this limitation by restructuring the bilevel problem to mitigate approximation error. Similarly, [arxiv_1905.13577] observes that while DARTS constructs a differentiable search space optimized by gradient descent, convergence in discrete search spaces can still demand substantial computational resources, motivating the use of proximal iterations to improve efficiency.

A complementary line of work focuses on the stochastic treatment of architecture selection. [arxiv_1812.09926] proposes Stochastic Neural Architecture Search (SNAS), which reformulates NAS as an optimization over parameters of an architecture distribution, enabling operation parameters and architecture distribution parameters to be trained within the same round of backpropagation. This approach preserves the completeness and differentiability of the NAS pipeline while offering an economical end-to-end solution, distinguishing it from deterministic relaxation strategies such as DARTS.

Despite the appeal of differentiable NAS, the architecture selection step that concludes the search phase has attracted critical scrutiny. [arxiv_2108.04392] highlights that in weight-sharing supernets, the standard practice of selecting operations with the largest architecture parameters does not reliably yield high-performing architectures, identifying a systematic disconnect between supernet optimization and final architecture quality. This observation underscores that gradient-based search efficiency does not automatically translate into reliable architecture selection, and motivates rethinking the evaluation criteria applied at the end of the search phase.

Collectively, these works illustrate that while differentiable NAS methods offer compelling efficiency advantages, open challenges remain in bilevel optimization fidelity [arxiv_2003.12238], stochastic search formulations [arxiv_1812.09926], and the validity of architecture selection procedures [arxiv_2108.04392].

### Differentiable and Gradient-Based Architecture Search

## Differentiable and Gradient-Based Architecture Search

Early neural architecture search (NAS) methods relied predominantly on reinforcement learning and evolutionary algorithms to navigate discrete architecture spaces, a strategy that proved computationally prohibitive due to the vast number of candidate architectures requiring evaluation [arxiv_1808.07233]. A pivotal shift in the field came with the introduction of continuous relaxation techniques, which reformulate architecture search as a gradient-based optimization problem over a differentiable space, thereby dramatically reducing search costs.

Luo et al. [arxiv_1808.07233] proposed Neural Architecture Optimization (NAO), embedding discrete architectures into a continuous representation through an encoder–decoder framework, enabling gradient descent to guide the search process directly. This continuous embedding approach contrasts with earlier discrete methods by allowing the optimization landscape to be traversed more efficiently, avoiding the combinatorial explosion inherent to exhaustive or stochastic discrete search. Similarly, Xie et al. [arxiv_1812.09926] introduced Stochastic Neural Architecture Search (SNAS), which reformulates NAS as an optimization over the parameters of a distribution across architectures. SNAS trains both neural operation parameters and architecture distribution parameters within the same backpropagation pass, preserving end-to-end differentiability while maintaining the completeness of the NAS pipeline. Compared to NAO, SNAS more explicitly models the stochastic nature of architecture selection, offering a principled probabilistic treatment within the gradient-based paradigm.

Despite the efficiency gains of differentiable NAS, a notable limitation of foundational methods such as DARTS lies in the misalignment between the shallow network used during architecture search and the deeper network employed for final evaluation. Yu et al. [ss_478250] addressed this gap through Cyclic Differentiable Architecture Search (CDAS), which introduces interaction between the search and evaluation networks rather than optimizing them independently. This cyclic coupling allows feedback from the evaluation network to inform the search process, improving the fidelity of the discovered architectures and yielding performance gains over standard DARTS-style approaches.

Practical deployment considerations have further shaped the trajectory of differentiable NAS research. Guo et al. [arxiv_2103.02126] specifically targeted the challenge of designing efficient architectures for embedded and mobile platforms, noting that even gradient-based methods can incur substantial costs when large candidate sets must be trained. Their work on Differentiable Neural Architecture Learning emphasizes hardware-aware efficiency as a first-class objective, extending the differentiable NAS paradigm beyond accuracy-centric benchmarks toward real-world deployment constraints. Collectively, these contributions illustrate a progression from foundational continuous relaxation toward increasingly refined methods that address alignment, stochasticity, and efficiency within the gradient-based NAS framework.
