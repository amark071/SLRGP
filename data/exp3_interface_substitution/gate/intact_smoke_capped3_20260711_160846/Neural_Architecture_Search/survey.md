# Neural Architecture Search

### Differentiable and Gradient-Based Architecture Search

Differentiable and gradient-based approaches to neural architecture search (NAS) emerged as a response to the prohibitive computational costs associated with earlier discrete search strategies. Methods grounded in reinforcement learning (RL) or evolutionary algorithms require evaluating a large number of candidate architectures independently, a process that is both time-consuming and resource-intensive [arxiv_2103.02126]. The central insight motivating differentiable NAS is that by relaxing the discrete architecture selection problem into a continuous one, standard gradient descent can be applied to jointly optimize both the network weights and the architecture parameters, dramatically reducing search cost [arxiv_1808.07233].

One of the earliest and most influential instantiations of this idea is the Neural Architecture Optimization (NAO) framework proposed by Luo et al., which encodes architectures into a continuous embedding space and performs optimization directly within that space, enabling efficient gradient-based search [arxiv_1808.07233]. Building on the same principle, DARTS and related methods—whose widespread adoption is credited with establishing differentiable NAS as the dominant paradigm—reformulate architecture search as a hyperparameter optimization problem solvable via gradient descent, achieving speedups of several orders of magnitude over RL- and EA-based predecessors [arxiv_2304.05405].

Despite these advances, differentiable methods are not without limitations. Concerns have been raised about the effectiveness and generalizability of gradient methods when applied to the non-convex architecture optimization landscape, motivating hybrid approaches that combine gradient-based search with other optimization strategies [arxiv_2109.12425]. Stochastic Neural Architecture Search (SNAS) addresses some of these concerns by reformulating NAS as an optimization over a distribution of architectures, training both operation parameters and architecture distribution parameters within a single round of back-propagation while preserving end-to-end differentiability [arxiv_1812.09926]. This stochastic formulation maintains the computational efficiency of gradient-based search while offering a more principled probabilistic treatment of the architecture selection process.

More recent work has sought to extend differentiable NAS to resource-constrained deployment scenarios, particularly for embedded and mobile platforms, where the efficiency of the search process must be balanced against the inference cost of the discovered architectures [arxiv_2103.02126]. Collectively, these contributions illustrate a trajectory in which the field has progressively refined the differentiable relaxation paradigm—moving from basic continuous encodings [arxiv_1808.07233] through stochastic reformulations [arxiv_1812.09926] to hardware-aware and efficiency-oriented variants [arxiv_2103.02126]—while ongoing debate about gradient reliability continues to stimulate complementary hybrid approaches [arxiv_2109.12425].

### Reinforcement Learning-Based Architecture Search

#### Efficiency and Scalability in RL-Based NAS

Efficiency and scalability represent two of the most pressing challenges in reinforcement learning-based neural architecture search, as the iterative nature of RL agents evaluating candidate architectures imposes substantial computational demands. Early RL-based NAS methods demonstrated promising results in discovering high-performing architectures, yet their practical adoption was severely constrained by the requirement for vast computational resources, often involving hundreds of GPUs operating over extended periods [arxiv_1707.04873]. A central bottleneck identified in this line of work is the practice of designing and training each candidate network from scratch during the exploration phase, which compounds the already significant cost of policy optimization [arxiv_1707.04873].

To address these resource demands, several strategies have been proposed. Cai et al. introduced network transformation techniques that reuse previously trained networks rather than initializing each candidate from scratch, thereby substantially reducing the computational overhead associated with architecture evaluation [arxiv_1707.04873]. A complementary perspective is offered by Liu et al., who identify the rollout bottleneck—exhaustive training of each sampled architecture on proxy tasks—as the primary source of inefficiency in RL-based NAS [arxiv_2105.11694]. Their proposed pipeline, FNAS, incorporates uncertainty-aware mechanisms to accelerate rollout convergence, enabling faster and more resource-efficient architecture search while preserving the convergence guarantees that make RL-based approaches attractive relative to gradient-based alternatives [arxiv_2105.11694].

Sample efficiency constitutes a related but distinct dimension of scalability. Nagy and Boros argue that improving the number of useful architectural evaluations obtained per unit of search effort is critical for making RL-based NAS tractable in practical settings, and they propose modifications to the RL search strategy specifically aimed at reducing the number of sampled architectures required to identify strong candidates [arxiv_2110.06751]. This concern with sample efficiency aligns with the broader goal of enabling NAS on more modest computational budgets, an objective shared across multiple works in this area [arxiv_1707.04873; arxiv_2105.11694; arxiv_2110.06751].

More recent work has shifted focus toward the scalability of the RL agent itself, examining whether a single learned agent can generalize its search behavior across diverse architecture spaces rather than returning only a single optimal solution. Cassimon et al. evaluate a novel RL-based NAS framework on both NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 benchmarks, demonstrating that an RL agent can learn to search effectively across settings and compete with strong baselines [arxiv_2410.01431]. Taken together, these contributions reflect a trajectory from reducing per-evaluation cost, to improving sample efficiency, to developing agents capable of scalable and generalizable architecture search.

#### Decoupled, Stochastic, and Sampling-Based Search Strategies

Reinforcement learning (RL) has served as one of the foundational paradigms in neural architecture search, wherein an agent is trained to propose architectures that are subsequently evaluated and used to update the policy toward higher-performing designs [arxiv_1905.01392]. Despite its theoretical appeal, standard RL-based NAS has historically been constrained by substantial computational demands, largely attributable to the rollout bottleneck, which requires exhaustive training of each sampled architecture on proxy tasks before a reward signal can be propagated back to the controller [arxiv_2105.11694]. To address this inefficiency, Liu et al. propose FNAS, a pipeline that accelerates rollout convergence through uncertainty-aware mechanisms, thereby reducing the resource overhead that has traditionally limited RL-based approaches relative to gradient-based alternatives [arxiv_2105.11694].

A complementary strategy for improving efficiency involves decoupling the search process into distinct subproblems. Lee et al. introduce an algorithm that separates structure search from operation search, sampling candidate models independently from multinomial distributions defined over two disjoint policy spaces [arxiv_1910.10397]. This decoupling significantly reduces the combinatorial complexity of the joint search space and improves overall search efficiency compared to conventional coupled approaches [arxiv_1910.10397]. Similarly, Nagy and Boros focus on sample efficiency, arguing that the number of architecture evaluations required by RL agents can be substantially reduced through more informed sampling strategies, making the search process more tractable in practice [arxiv_2110.06751].

Scalability represents another active concern. Cassimon et al. propose a scalable RL-based NAS framework in which the agent learns to search across a distribution of good architectures rather than converging to a single optimum, demonstrating competitive performance on both NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301 benchmarks against strong baselines [arxiv_2410.01431]. This distributional perspective connects RL-based search to stochastic and sampling-based formulations more broadly. In the stochastic NAS literature, Xie et al. propose SNAS, which reformulates architecture search as an optimization over parameters of a distribution, enabling joint end-to-end training of operation weights and architecture distribution parameters within a single backpropagation pass [arxiv_1812.09926]. Akimoto et al. extend this direction by developing an adaptive stochastic natural gradient framework for one-shot NAS, explicitly targeting robustness to hyperparameter sensitivity and broad applicability across search spaces [arxiv_1905.08537].

Bayesian and posterior-based perspectives offer yet another angle on sampling-based search. Zhou et al. formulate NAS as estimation of a joint posterior over architecture and weight pairs, using this posterior to guide the sampling of candidate architectures more efficiently than uninformed stochastic methods [arxiv_1906.09557]. Constraint-aware sampling has also been explored: Yang et al. propose TabNAS, which incorporates rejection sampling to enforce resource constraints during architecture search on tabular datasets, demonstrating that constraint satisfaction can be embedded directly into the sampling procedure rather than treated as a post-hoc filter [arxiv_2204.07615]. Collectively, these works illustrate a clear trajectory in RL-based and stochastic NAS toward decoupled, distribution-aware, and constraint-conscious search strategies that prioritize both scalability and sample efficiency.
