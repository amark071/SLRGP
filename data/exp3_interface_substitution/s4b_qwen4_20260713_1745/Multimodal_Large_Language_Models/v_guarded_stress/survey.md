# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have emerged as a pivotal area of research, driven by the increasing demand for systems capable of seamlessly integrating and processing multiple modalities such as text, images, audio, and video. Several recent surveys provide comprehensive overviews of the field, highlighting its evolution, technical challenges, and future directions. These works collectively underscore the transition from specialized multimodal models to more general-purpose assistants, while emphasizing the foundational role of vision-language pre-training (VLP) techniques.

A foundational contribution to understanding MLLMs comes from Zhe Gan and colleagues, who survey vision-language pre-training methods in their 2022 paper [arxiv_2210.09263]. They categorize VLP approaches into three main areas: image-text tasks, core computer vision tasks, and cross-modal reasoning tasks. Their analysis reveals how early efforts focused primarily on tasks like image captioning and visual question answering, laying the groundwork for more complex multimodal reasoning. This survey serves as a precursor to later works that expand beyond traditional vision-language tasks into broader multimodal integration.

Building on this foundation, the 2023 paper by Chunyuan Li et al. [arxiv_2309.10020] presents a comprehensive survey of multimodal foundation models, emphasizing their evolution from specialist models to general-purpose assistants. The authors identify five core topics within the research landscape, including multimodal perception, reasoning, generation, interaction, and deployment. This work highlights the increasing complexity of multimodal models and their ability to handle diverse tasks across modalities, setting the stage for the development of MLLMs that can interact with users through natural dialogue interfaces.

In 2024, Davide Caffagni and colleagues [arxiv_2402.12451] further elaborate on the revolution brought about by MLLMs, emphasizing their role in generative intelligence. Their survey explores how these models integrate visual and textual modalities while providing a dialogue-based interface and instruction-following capabilities. This paper underscores the importance of MLLMs in bridging the gap between human-like interaction and machine understanding, particularly in applications requiring seamless multimodal reasoning and response generation.

Complementing these surveys, Soyeon Caren Han et al. [arxiv_2410.05608] offer a tutorial-style overview that extends the scope of multimodal models beyond traditional text and image inputs to include sensors, audio, and video. Their work provides a broader perspective on the evolution of multimodal research, highlighting the integration of diverse data forms and the technical challenges involved. The authors also discuss recent advancements in multimodal pretrained models and the key innovations driving their performance improvements.

Taken together, these surveys illustrate a clear trajectory in multimodal research—from early vision-language pre-training techniques to the development of sophisticated MLLMs capable of processing multiple modalities and engaging in complex interactions. While each paper contributes unique insights, they collectively highlight the growing importance of multimodal integration in building intelligent systems that can understand and respond to the world in a more human-like manner.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have emerged as a promising direction in artificial intelligence, aiming to bridge the gap between vision and language understanding. These models extend traditional Large Language Models (LLMs) by incorporating visual inputs, enabling them to perform a wide range of tasks that involve both modalities [arxiv_2403.08773]. The architectural designs and pretraining strategies of these models are central to their effectiveness and adaptability across diverse applications.

One notable advancement is the development of CROME (Cross-Modal Adapters for Efficient Multimodal LLM), introduced by Ebrahimi et al. [arxiv_2408.06610]. CROME addresses the challenge of cost-effective training and adaptation in MLLMs by introducing cross-modal adapters that allow efficient integration of visual information without requiring extensive retraining of the underlying language model. This approach enhances adaptability while reducing computational overhead, making it suitable for task-specific fine-tuning scenarios.

In contrast, PaLI (Pathways Language and Image model) by Chen et al. [arxiv_2209.06794] takes a different approach by focusing on joint scaling of language and vision capabilities across multiple languages. PaLI is designed to handle a broad spectrum of vision, language, and multimodal tasks through a unified interface. Its training strategy emphasizes scalability and flexibility, enabling the model to generate text based on both visual and textual inputs. This design highlights the importance of multilingual support and task versatility in vision-language models.

Expanding beyond traditional vision-language models, VALOR (Vision-Audio-Language Omni-Perception Pretraining model) proposed by Liu et al. [ss_478472] introduces a more comprehensive multimodal framework that integrates vision, audio, and language. VALOR employs separate encoders for each modality and a unified decoder to model cross-modal interactions. This end-to-end architecture allows for more holistic perception and generation tasks, demonstrating the potential of multimodal models to handle richer sensory inputs than traditional vision-language models.

Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] further emphasizes the importance of multimodal representation learning, showcasing how integrating textual and visual information can lead to improved performance across various tasks. While Veagle does not propose a novel architecture, it highlights the broader trend of enhancing multimodal models through advanced representation learning techniques.

Comparing these models reveals a spectrum of approaches to multimodal integration. CROME focuses on efficiency and adaptability through modular adapters, PaLI emphasizes scalability and multilingual support, VALOR introduces a broader sensory scope by including audio, and Veagle underscores the importance of representation learning. Together, these works illustrate the evolving landscape of vision-language pretraining, where architectural innovation and training strategies are key to advancing multimodal understanding and generation capabilities.
