# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has attracted substantial scholarly attention as a paradigm for enabling collaborative model training without centralising raw data, and a growing body of survey literature has sought to map its foundations, challenges, and trajectories. A foundational framing is offered by [arxiv_2104.14362], which traces the conceptual lineage of federated learning from earlier distributed machine learning frameworks. The authors argue that as data and computing resources became increasingly dispersed across end-user devices, regions, and organisations, legal and regulatory constraints made direct data sharing impractical, positioning federated learning as an efficient mechanism for exploiting distributed data while respecting such constraints. This historical and architectural perspective situates federated learning within a broader evolution of distributed computation, providing a useful baseline against which more specialised surveys can be read.

Building on this general framing, [arxiv_2301.01299] offers a systematic treatment of recent advances in the field, emphasising the privacy-preserving properties that distinguish federated learning from traditional centralised approaches. The authors highlight that, rather than transmitting raw data, federated learning exchanges only locally trained model parameters or computed gradients, thereby limiting direct exposure of sensitive information. Their survey covers a wide range of developments, reflecting the rapid maturation of the field since its initial formulation, and underscores the paradigm's applicability across different parties with heterogeneous data holdings.

A more focused concern is addressed by [arxiv_2106.06843], which concentrates specifically on the challenge of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data across local devices. This survey identifies a critical limitation acknowledged more broadly in the literature: models trained under federated conditions frequently underperform relative to their centralised counterparts, and this performance gap is substantially attributable to statistical heterogeneity in local data distributions. By providing a detailed analysis of non-IID scenarios, [arxiv_2106.06843] complements the broader overviews offered by [arxiv_2104.14362] and [arxiv_2301.01299], drilling into one of the most persistent practical obstacles to deploying federated systems at scale.

Communication efficiency represents another axis along which survey literature has developed. [arxiv_2208.01200] addresses this dimension directly, noting that while federated learning was conceived in part to mitigate the privacy risks associated with transmitting raw user data to a central server, the iterative exchange of model updates between clients and server introduces its own communication overhead. The authors frame federated learning as a particular instantiation of distributed machine learning equipped with privacy protection mechanisms, and survey contemporary strategies for reducing communication costs without sacrificing model quality. This concern for efficiency complements the privacy and statistical heterogeneity themes foregrounded in the other surveys, together suggesting that the field's core challenges are multidimensional.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a research community grappling simultaneously with privacy guarantees [arxiv_2301.01299], data heterogeneity [arxiv_2106.06843], communication constraints [arxiv_2208.01200], and the broader relationship between federated and distributed learning paradigms [arxiv_2104.14362]. The convergence of these concerns across independently conducted overviews underscores their centrality to the federated learning research agenda.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as a foundational mechanism for strengthening privacy guarantees in federated learning (FL), where the distributed nature of training introduces unique vulnerabilities despite the absence of direct data sharing. The core challenge lies in balancing the privacy-utility trade-off: stronger privacy guarantees typically degrade model accuracy, and researchers have pursued a range of architectural and algorithmic strategies to navigate this tension.

One prominent line of work focuses on refining the noise injection mechanisms that underpin DP in FL. Hu, Guo, and Gong [arxiv_2003.13761] propose a concentrated differentially private framework that operates at the level of local stochastic gradient descent updates, adding calibrated noise to the computation results that edge devices upload to the central server at each communication round. Their approach leverages concentrated differential privacy—a tighter variant of the classical DP definition—to preserve model utility while still providing rigorous privacy guarantees. This framing highlights a recurring design decision in DP-FL: because devices perform multiple local gradient steps before aggregation, the composition of privacy costs across rounds must be carefully managed to avoid excessive noise accumulation.

The interaction between DP and secure aggregation introduces further complexity, particularly when fine-grained protection granularities are required. Heikkilä [arxiv_2407.19286] examines the conditions under which secure aggregation can be meaningfully combined with DP in settings involving multiple local update steps. The analysis reveals that naive combinations of these two techniques can undermine the privacy accounting, since secure aggregation protocols are typically designed under assumptions that may not hold when clients execute several local iterations before contributing to the aggregate. This work thus cautions against treating DP and secure aggregation as straightforwardly composable, and calls for more careful protocol design when both are deployed together.

A complementary strand of research investigates decentralized aggregation as an alternative to the standard central-server paradigm, motivated in part by the single point of failure and trust that a central aggregator represents. Jeon et al. [arxiv_2012.07183] develop a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that distributes the aggregation process across participating nodes, thereby reducing reliance on any single trusted party while maintaining differential privacy properties. Their formulation addresses the challenge of coordinating gradient aggregation across geographically dispersed regions without centralizing sensitive information. Abd El-Kareem, Saleh, and colleagues [arxiv_2311.16008] similarly explore decentralized aggregation combined with DP, situating their contribution within the broader context of peer-to-peer machine learning over mobile networks, where concerns about personal data control are especially acute. Comparing these two works, both converge on decentralization as a structural remedy for the trust limitations of central aggregation, yet they differ in their specific threat models and the communication topologies they assume.

Taken together, these contributions illustrate that integrating differential privacy into federated learning is not a monolithic problem but rather a cluster of interrelated challenges spanning noise calibration [arxiv_2003.13761], protocol composition [arxiv_2407.19286], and aggregation architecture [arxiv_2012.07183, arxiv_2311.16008]. Progress in each dimension is necessary for FL systems that are simultaneously private, accurate, and robust to diverse deployment conditions.
