# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has attracted substantial scholarly attention over the past several years, giving rise to a growing body of survey literature that seeks to map its theoretical foundations, practical challenges, and evolving methodologies. At its core, federated learning addresses a fundamental tension in modern machine learning: data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across end-user devices, regional infrastructures, and organizational boundaries, yet legal and regulatory constraints often prohibit the direct sharing of such data [arxiv_2104.14362]. Rather than centralizing raw data, federated learning enables collaborative model training by exchanging only locally trained models or computed gradients, thereby offering a principled mechanism for privacy-preserving learning [arxiv_2301.01299].

Several surveys have approached this field from complementary angles. Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] situate federated learning within the broader historical trajectory of distributed machine learning, tracing how the field evolved in response to the practical impossibility of aggregating sensitive data across organizational silos. This historical framing is useful for understanding federated learning not as an isolated innovation but as a response to structural constraints in data governance. By contrast, Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299] adopt a more systematic and contemporaneous perspective, cataloguing recent advances across multiple dimensions of the federated learning pipeline, including aggregation strategies, privacy guarantees, and system heterogeneity. Together, these two surveys offer complementary temporal scopes: one situating federated learning in its distributed computing lineage, the other capturing the rapid proliferation of techniques that have emerged as the paradigm has matured.

A recurring theme across the survey literature is the challenge posed by non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data. When local datasets reflect the heterogeneous behaviors, demographics, or environments of individual clients, globally aggregated models may suffer significant performance degradation relative to centralized counterparts [arxiv_2106.06843]. Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a dedicated analysis of this problem, systematically categorizing the types of data heterogeneity encountered in practice and reviewing the algorithmic responses that have been proposed to mitigate their effects. This focus on non-IID data represents one of the most active sub-areas within federated learning research, as it strikes at the statistical assumptions that underpin many classical convergence guarantees.

Communication efficiency constitutes another central concern surveyed in the literature. Because federated learning requires repeated rounds of model exchange between clients and a central server, the cumulative communication overhead can be prohibitive, particularly in bandwidth-constrained or large-scale deployments [arxiv_2208.01200]. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2208.01200] survey contemporary techniques aimed at reducing this overhead, including gradient compression, quantization, and sparsification, while also situating these methods within the dual imperative of maintaining privacy and model quality. This survey complements the broader overviews by drilling into the systems-level constraints that must be addressed for federated learning to be viable at scale.

Taken together, these surveys illustrate that federated learning is a multidimensional research area in which privacy, statistical heterogeneity, and communication efficiency are deeply intertwined challenges, each demanding dedicated methodological attention.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as a foundational technique for strengthening privacy guarantees in federated learning (FL), where the distributed nature of training introduces unique challenges around data leakage and model inversion attacks. The core challenge lies in balancing the competing demands of privacy and model utility, a tension that has motivated a range of algorithmic innovations across both centralized and decentralized FL architectures.

In centralized FL settings, one prominent approach involves injecting calibrated noise into gradient updates before they are transmitted to the aggregating server. [arxiv_2003.13761] addresses this challenge through a concentrated differential privacy framework designed to preserve model utility while providing rigorous privacy guarantees. Their approach leverages the properties of concentrated DP, which offers tighter composition bounds than standard DP, allowing for more precise control over the cumulative privacy loss across multiple communication rounds. This is particularly important in FL, where edge devices perform multiple local steps of stochastic gradient descent before uploading updates, meaning that privacy accounting must carefully track noise accumulation over both local iterations and global rounds.

The interaction between multiple local steps and DP mechanisms is further examined by [arxiv_2407.19286], who investigates the combination of DP with secure aggregation in settings where fine-grained protection granularities are required. A key insight from this work is that secure aggregation and DP are not straightforwardly composable when clients perform multiple local update steps, as the structure of the aggregated noise changes in ways that can either weaken privacy guarantees or inflate the noise required to maintain them. This finding has direct implications for system design, suggesting that practitioners must carefully consider the interaction between local training dynamics and the chosen privacy mechanism rather than treating them as independent components.

Moving beyond centralized aggregation, several works have explored decentralized architectures as a means of reducing reliance on a potentially untrusted central server. [arxiv_2012.07183] develops a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that avoids the need for a central aggregator entirely, formulating the aggregation problem in a way that prevents individual participants from inferring the private data of others. This peer-to-peer approach is complementary to DP in that it addresses a different threat model, namely the honest-but-curious server, rather than external adversaries observing model updates. [arxiv_2311.16008] similarly investigates decentralized aggregation combined with differential privacy, situating the work within the broader context of mobile and peer-to-peer networks where data sovereignty concerns are particularly acute. Comparing these two decentralized approaches, both [arxiv_2012.07183] and [arxiv_2311.16008] recognize that FL alone does not provide sufficient privacy guarantees and that additional mechanisms are necessary, though they differ in their specific protocol designs and the network topologies they target.

Taken together, these works illustrate that differential privacy in FL is not a monolithic solution but rather a design space shaped by choices about aggregation architecture, local training procedures, and the specific adversarial threat model under consideration. The interplay between DP noise, secure aggregation, and decentralized protocols remains an active area of research with significant implications for deploying privacy-preserving FL systems at scale.
