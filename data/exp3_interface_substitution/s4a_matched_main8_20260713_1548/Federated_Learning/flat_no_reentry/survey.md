# Federated Learning

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has attracted substantial scholarly attention in recent years, prompting the publication of numerous surveys and overviews that collectively map the field's theoretical foundations, practical challenges, and emerging directions. At its core, federated learning addresses a fundamental tension in modern machine learning: data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across end-user devices, regional infrastructures, and organizational boundaries, yet legal and regulatory constraints prevent the direct sharing of raw data [arxiv_2104.14362]. By exchanging only locally trained models or computed gradients rather than underlying data, federated learning offers a privacy-preserving alternative to traditional centralized approaches that require aggregating data from all participating parties [arxiv_2301.01299].

Several surveys have sought to situate federated learning within the broader landscape of distributed machine learning. Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] trace an explicit lineage from conventional distributed machine learning to federated learning, framing the latter as an efficient mechanism for exploiting distributed data under privacy constraints. This historical perspective complements more recent systematic treatments, such as that of Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299], which provide a comprehensive account of advances in privacy-preserving collaborative learning, emphasizing how the paradigm has matured into a robust framework applicable across diverse parties and settings.

A recurring concern across these overviews is the communication overhead inherent to federated learning. Because model updates must be transmitted repeatedly between clients and a central server, the communication cost can become a significant bottleneck, particularly in large-scale deployments. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2208.01200] address this challenge directly, surveying contemporary strategies aimed at improving communication efficiency while maintaining the privacy protection mechanisms that distinguish federated learning from traditional distributed training. Their work highlights that, unlike conventional distributed machine learning where raw user data traverses the network, federated learning confines data locally and transmits only model-level information, thereby reducing privacy risks even as it introduces new efficiency considerations [arxiv_2208.01200].

Another prominent theme in the survey literature concerns the statistical heterogeneity of data across clients. When local datasets are not independent and identically distributed—a condition commonly referred to as Non-IID data—federated models tend to exhibit degraded performance relative to centralized counterparts [arxiv_2106.06843]. Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a detailed analysis of this problem, cataloguing the sources of data heterogeneity and reviewing algorithmic responses designed to mitigate its adverse effects. This focus on Non-IID conditions represents a natural complement to the communication-efficiency perspective of Zhao et al. [arxiv_2208.01200], as both lines of inquiry identify structural properties of federated settings that distinguish them from idealized distributed learning scenarios.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field organized around several intersecting challenges: enabling collaboration under legal and regulatory constraints [arxiv_2104.14362], preserving privacy through model-level rather than data-level exchange [arxiv_2301.01299], managing communication costs at scale [arxiv_2208.01200], and contending with the statistical diversity of real-world distributed data [arxiv_2106.06843]. The convergence of these concerns across independent survey efforts underscores their centrality to federated learning research and provides the conceptual scaffolding upon which more specialized investigations are built.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as a foundational mechanism for strengthening privacy guarantees in federated learning (FL), where the distributed nature of training introduces unique vulnerabilities despite the absence of direct data sharing. The core challenge lies in balancing the competing demands of privacy and model utility, a tension that has motivated a substantial body of research into how DP techniques can be most effectively integrated into FL pipelines.

A central contribution to this area is the development of concentrated differential privacy (CDP) frameworks tailored to the FL setting. [arxiv_2003.13761] propose a CDP-based approach that operates across communication rounds, wherein edge devices perform multiple local stochastic gradient descent steps before uploading results to a central server. Their framework seeks to preserve model utility while enforcing rigorous privacy guarantees, recognising that the iterative, multi-round structure of FL requires careful noise calibration to prevent excessive utility degradation over time. This work highlights that naive application of standard DP mechanisms to FL can be insufficient, as the repeated exposure of model updates across rounds compounds privacy costs in ways that demand more sophisticated accounting.

The interaction between DP and secure aggregation—another prominent privacy technique in FL—has received increasing scrutiny. [arxiv_2407.19286] examines the conditions under which secure aggregation can be meaningfully combined with DP in FL settings that involve multiple local update steps. A key finding is that fine-grained protection granularities, such as per-sample or per-device protections, introduce non-trivial complications when secure aggregation is applied, as the two mechanisms make partially conflicting assumptions about what information the server is permitted to observe. This analysis underscores that the combination of DP and secure aggregation, while intuitively appealing, requires careful protocol design to avoid undermining the guarantees of either technique.

The question of centralised versus decentralised aggregation further complicates the deployment of DP in FL. Both [arxiv_2311.16008] and [arxiv_2012.07183] address privacy-preserving decentralised aggregation as an alternative to the standard server-centric FL architecture. [arxiv_2311.16008] situates this problem within the broader context of peer-to-peer machine learning, arguing that decentralised aggregation can reduce reliance on a trusted central server while still incorporating DP to guard against inference attacks. [arxiv_2012.07183] similarly develops a decentralised aggregation protocol and frames the privacy problem as one of preventing distributed sites from revealing sensitive data even to one another. Compared to [arxiv_2003.13761], which assumes a central orchestrator, these decentralised approaches shift the threat model and consequently alter how DP noise must be injected and calibrated.

Collectively, these works illustrate that differential privacy in federated learning is not a monolithic solution but a family of techniques whose effectiveness depends heavily on architectural choices—centralised or decentralised aggregation, the number of local update steps, and the granularity of the privacy guarantee sought. The interplay between DP and complementary mechanisms such as secure aggregation, as highlighted by [arxiv_2407.19286], remains an active area where theoretical guarantees and practical deployability are still being reconciled.
