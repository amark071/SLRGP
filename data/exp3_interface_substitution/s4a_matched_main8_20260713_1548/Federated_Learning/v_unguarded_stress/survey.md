# Federated Learning

### Injected invalid unbalanced organization

Federated learning has emerged as a compelling paradigm for enabling collaborative model training across distributed data sources without requiring the direct exchange of raw data [arxiv_2104.14362]. In this framework, participating clients—whether individual devices, regional nodes, or distinct organizations—train local models and share only model updates or gradients with a central aggregating server, thereby preserving the privacy of underlying datasets [arxiv_2301.01299]. While this architecture offers substantial advantages in terms of data governance and regulatory compliance, it simultaneously introduces a range of challenges that are particularly acute when the participating organizations are heterogeneous, unbalanced in their data contributions, or when invalid or malicious participants are injected into the federation [arxiv_2106.06843].

One of the most extensively documented problems in federated learning is the non-independent and identically distributed (Non-IID) nature of data across clients. When training data are not uniformly distributed among local devices or organizations, the performance of globally aggregated models degrades significantly compared to centralized learning baselines [arxiv_2106.06843]. This degradation is compounded when the federation involves organizations of unequal size or capacity, as larger organizations may contribute disproportionately more data or computational resources, skewing the global model toward their local distributions. Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] situate this challenge within the broader transition from distributed machine learning to federated learning, noting that the inability to directly share data across organizational boundaries—owing to legal and regulatory constraints—forces practitioners to contend with statistical heterogeneity as a structural feature rather than an incidental one. The survey by Liu et al. [arxiv_2301.01299] reinforces this perspective, emphasizing that the exchange of locally trained models or gradients, rather than raw data, while protective of privacy, does not inherently resolve the distributional mismatch that arises when organizations contribute unbalanced datasets.

The problem of injected invalid participants—clients that are either compromised, behave maliciously, or submit corrupted updates—adds a further dimension of vulnerability to federated systems. When such participants are present alongside legitimately unbalanced organizations, the aggregation process becomes doubly unreliable: not only must the server contend with statistically skewed updates from heterogeneous contributors, but it must also identify and mitigate the influence of adversarial or faulty inputs. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2208.01200] highlight that the communication-intensive nature of federated learning, in which model parameters are repeatedly transmitted between clients and a central server, creates multiple points of exposure where invalid updates can be introduced, whether through deliberate poisoning or inadvertent data corruption. This observation underscores the need for robust aggregation mechanisms that can function reliably even when the composition of the federation is neither balanced nor entirely trustworthy.

Differential privacy has been widely proposed as a mechanism for bounding the information leakage associated with individual client contributions, and its interaction with unbalanced or invalid participation is a subject of active investigation. Hu, Guo, and Gong [arxiv_2003.13761] develop a concentrated differentially private federated learning framework in which edge devices perform multiple local stochastic gradient descent steps before uploading results to the server. Their approach attempts to preserve utility while enforcing privacy guarantees, but the utility-privacy trade-off becomes more difficult to navigate when client contributions are highly unequal, as the noise calibration required for differential privacy may disproportionately suppress the signal from smaller or less-represented organizations. Heikkilä [arxiv_2407.19286] extends this line of inquiry by examining the combination of differential privacy with secure aggregation in settings involving multiple local steps, finding that fine-grained protection granularities introduce additional complexity when client participation is irregular or when some clients submit invalid updates. The interaction between secure aggregation protocols and the presence of injected invalid participants is particularly delicate, since secure aggregation is designed to prevent the server from inspecting individual updates, which simultaneously limits the server's ability to detect and exclude malicious or corrupted contributions.

Decentralized aggregation approaches have been proposed as an alternative to the standard server-centric federated learning architecture, with the aim of distributing the aggregation burden and reducing reliance on a single point of trust or failure. Jeon et al. [arxiv_2012.07183] develop a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that avoids the need for a central server by allowing distributed sites to collaboratively aggregate model updates without revealing their individual data. This approach can, in principle, reduce the impact of a single invalid or compromised aggregator, but it does not fully resolve the challenge of unbalanced organizational participation, since the peer-to-peer communication structure may still be dominated by organizations with greater connectivity or computational resources. Abd El-Kareem et al. [arxiv_2311.16008] similarly explore decentralized aggregation in conjunction with differential privacy, finding that while decentralization can improve resilience and privacy in peer-to-peer settings, the trade-off between privacy guarantees and model utility remains sensitive to the distribution of data across participants and the presence of nodes that contribute invalid or low-quality updates.

Taken together, the literature reveals that the challenges of injected invalid participants and unbalanced organizational contributions are deeply intertwined in federated learning systems. The statistical heterogeneity arising from unbalanced data distributions [arxiv_2106.06843] interacts with the security vulnerabilities introduced by invalid participants [arxiv_2208.01200], and both phenomena complicate the application of privacy-preserving mechanisms such as differential privacy [arxiv_2003.13761, arxiv_2407.19286] and secure or decentralized aggregation [arxiv_2012.07183, arxiv_2311.16008]. Addressing these compounded challenges requires approaches that are simultaneously robust to distributional imbalance, resilient to adversarial injection, and capable of maintaining meaningful privacy guarantees—a combination that remains an open and pressing research problem in the field [arxiv_2301.01299].
