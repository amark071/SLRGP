# Federated Learning

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-4

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed devices without requiring the centralisation of raw data. At its core, the framework enables a set of edge devices to collaboratively optimise a shared model under the coordination of a central server, with each participant performing local computation — typically multiple steps of stochastic gradient descent — before uploading only the resulting model updates [arxiv_2003.13761]. This architecture is motivated by both practical and regulatory constraints: as data and computing resources become increasingly distributed across end users, organisations, and geographic regions, laws and regulations frequently prohibit the direct sharing of such resources for machine learning purposes, making federated learning an efficient alternative [arxiv_2104.14362].

Despite its promise, federated learning faces a number of well-documented challenges. One of the most extensively studied concerns the statistical heterogeneity of local data distributions. When the training data held by individual devices are not independent and identically distributed (Non-IID), models trained under the federated paradigm tend to exhibit substantially degraded performance relative to those trained in a centralised setting [arxiv_2106.06843]. This degradation arises because locally optimised updates may diverge in directions that are misaligned with the global objective, a phenomenon that becomes more pronounced as the degree of data heterogeneity increases. Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a detailed taxonomy of Non-IID scenarios and survey the range of algorithmic strategies proposed to mitigate their effects, situating data heterogeneity as one of the central open problems in the field.

Privacy preservation represents another foundational concern. Although federated learning inherently avoids the direct transmission of raw data, the model updates shared with the central server can still leak sensitive information about participants' local datasets. Hu, Guo, and Gong [arxiv_2003.13761] address this vulnerability through the lens of differential privacy, proposing mechanisms that inject calibrated noise into the update process to provide concentrated differential privacy guarantees while seeking to preserve model utility. Their work highlights the tension between privacy and performance that is characteristic of this line of research. A complementary approach is explored by Jeon et al. [arxiv_2012.07183], who argue that the centralised aggregation step itself constitutes a structural privacy risk, particularly in settings where data spans multiple regions. Rather than relying solely on noise-based mechanisms, they develop a decentralised aggregation protocol that removes the need for a trusted central aggregator, thereby reducing the attack surface for privacy breaches. Taken together, these two contributions illustrate that privacy in federated learning can be pursued through both cryptographic and statistical means, and that the choice of approach carries distinct trade-offs in terms of communication overhead, scalability, and the strength of the privacy guarantees offered.

Collectively, the literature surveyed here reflects the multi-dimensional nature of federated learning research. Surveys such as those by Liu et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] and Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide the conceptual scaffolding within which more targeted contributions on privacy [arxiv_2003.13761; arxiv_2012.07183] and data heterogeneity [arxiv_2106.06843] can be situated, underscoring that advances in the field require simultaneous attention to statistical, systems, and security considerations.

### Rank slab 2: positions 5-8

The intersection of decentralized architectures and privacy-preserving mechanisms represents a particularly active frontier within federated learning research, with several contributions addressing the structural and cryptographic challenges that arise when sensitive data must be collaboratively exploited without centralised coordination.

On the architectural side, [arxiv_2210.00325] tackles the open problem of enabling fully decentralised, peer-to-peer federated learning in dynamic network environments. The authors propose a consensus-based algorithm that allows distributed learners to perform global model aggregation without any central coordinator, even as the communication graph between participants changes over time due to high mobility. This work is significant because it removes the single point of failure and trust that a central aggregation server would otherwise represent. [arxiv_2306.02570] approaches the same structural concern from a complementary angle, situating decentralised optimisation theory within the federated learning paradigm. The authors observe that the majority of existing federated learning frameworks remain anchored to centralised settings, which are inherently vulnerable to server-side failures and attacks. By bridging decentralised optimisation with federated learning, [arxiv_2306.02570] provides a theoretical grounding that complements the more protocol-oriented contribution of [arxiv_2210.00325], together suggesting that the field is converging on decentralised designs as a principled response to both robustness and privacy concerns.

Privacy preservation, however, is not solely a matter of removing the central server; even in decentralised or semi-centralised settings, the model parameters exchanged between participants can leak sensitive information about underlying training data. [arxiv_2201.07730] addresses this threat in the context of secure aggregation, noting that a malicious aggregation server can exploit gradient or parameter updates to infer private dataset characteristics. The SCOTCH framework proposed in that work introduces an efficient secure computation approach that mitigates such leakage without incurring the prohibitive overheads typically associated with differential privacy or fully homomorphic encryption. This efficiency focus distinguishes [arxiv_2201.07730] from heavier cryptographic approaches and makes it practically relevant for real-world deployments.

[arxiv_2402.10145] extends privacy considerations further by addressing two compounding challenges simultaneously: incomplete data and non-IID distributions across participants. The authors employ chaotic maps as a lightweight cryptographic primitive to add an additional privacy layer on top of the federated learning protocol, while also proposing mechanisms to handle the statistical heterogeneity that characterises realistic federated scenarios. In doing so, [arxiv_2402.10145] occupies a somewhat broader scope than [arxiv_2201.07730], which focuses narrowly on secure aggregation efficiency, whereas the former integrates privacy with data quality concerns.

Taken together, these four contributions illustrate two converging directions: the architectural shift from centralised to decentralised coordination [arxiv_2210.00325, arxiv_2306.02570], and the cryptographic and algorithmic effort to ensure that parameter exchange does not compromise participant privacy regardless of topology [arxiv_2201.07730, arxiv_2402.10145]. The works collectively underscore that neither decentralisation alone nor encryption alone is sufficient; robust federated learning requires both structural and cryptographic safeguards operating in concert.
