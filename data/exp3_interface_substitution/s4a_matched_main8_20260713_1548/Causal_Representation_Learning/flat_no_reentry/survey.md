# Causal Representation Learning

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning

Disentangled causal representation learning has emerged as a critical subfield that addresses fundamental limitations in conventional disentanglement approaches, particularly the unrealistic assumption that underlying generative factors are statistically independent. Early disentanglement methods, largely unsupervised and grounded in independence priors, have been shown to be insufficient for capturing the true generative structure of real-world data [arxiv_2112.05746]. This recognition has motivated a growing body of work that explicitly incorporates causal relationships among latent variables into the representation learning framework.

A foundational contribution in this direction is the Disentangled gEnerative cAusal Representation (DEAR) learning method proposed by Shen and colleagues, which operates under weak supervision to learn representations in which the latent factors of interest may be causally related rather than independent [arxiv_2010.02637]. The authors demonstrate formally that methods relying on independent priors fail to disentangle causally structured factors, thereby motivating the need for a generative model that explicitly encodes a causal graph over latent variables. This work represents an important departure from the independence-based paradigm and establishes a theoretical basis for causally informed disentanglement.

Building on similar motivations, Reddy and colleagues examine the properties of causally disentangled representations more broadly, arguing that disentanglement methods must adopt a causal view of the generative process to be meaningful for downstream applications such as fairness and interpretability [arxiv_2112.05746]. Their work extends prior explorations of weak supervision and correlated features by grounding the analysis in causal generative assumptions, thereby providing a more principled evaluation framework for disentanglement. Compared to DEAR [arxiv_2010.02637], this contribution places greater emphasis on the conceptual and evaluative dimensions of causal disentanglement rather than proposing a specific architectural solution.

More recently, Fan and colleagues address the same independence limitation through a different architectural lens, proposing a Causal Flow-based Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) that models interdependencies among generative factors through normalizing flows within a causal structure [arxiv_2304.09010]. This approach acknowledges that standard VAE-based disentanglement methods are inadequate when factors exhibit causal dependencies, and offers a flexible generative framework capable of capturing such structure. The use of flow-based models distinguishes this work from DEAR [arxiv_2010.02637], which relies on a generative adversarial framework, suggesting that multiple architectural paradigms can be adapted to accommodate causal structure in latent spaces.

A complementary perspective is offered by Moran and Aragam, who situate disentangled causal representation learning within the broader goal of interpretability in deep generative models [arxiv_2504.11609]. Their work highlights that despite the impressive empirical performance of modern generative AI systems, the implicit representations learned by deep neural networks remain largely opaque, and that causal representation learning offers a principled pathway toward more interpretable models. This framing connects the technical agenda of causal disentanglement to practical concerns about transparency and trustworthiness in AI systems.

Taken together, these works converge on the view that independence assumptions in latent space are both theoretically unjustified and practically limiting, and that explicitly modeling causal relationships among factors is essential for learning representations that are truly disentangled, interpretable, and useful across diverse real-world settings [arxiv_2010.02637; arxiv_2112.05746; arxiv_2304.09010; arxiv_2504.11609].

### Identifiability Theory for Causal Representations

Identifiability theory constitutes a foundational pillar of causal representation learning (CRL), addressing the fundamental question of when and under what conditions latent causal variables and their relational structure can be uniquely recovered from high-dimensional observations. A central challenge in this endeavor is that prior work has often relied on restrictive assumptions—such as linearity of the mixing function or the latent causal model—or has required strong forms of supervision, including counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views or explicit temporal structure [arxiv_2306.00542]. Relaxing these constraints while preserving identifiability guarantees has therefore become a primary theoretical objective in the field.

One important line of work addresses identifiability under nonparametric conditions. Von Kügelgen et al. [arxiv_2306.00542] study the task of inferring latent causal variables and their causal relations from high-dimensional mixtures without imposing parametric restrictions on either the mixing function or the latent model, instead leveraging unknown interventions as the primary source of identifying information. This nonparametric stance represents a significant departure from earlier approaches and broadens the applicability of identifiability results to more realistic settings.

Complementing this, Varıcı et al. [arxiv_2310.15450] establish both identifiability and achievability results under a general nonparametric latent causal model paired with a general transformation model mapping latent variables to observations. A notable contribution of their framework is that identifiability is achieved using two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph, and crucially, the analyst need not know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node. This relaxation of the pairing requirement addresses a practical limitation present in frameworks that assume known intervention targets, and the accompanying achievability results demonstrate that the identified representations can in principle be attained algorithmically [arxiv_2310.15450].

Zhang et al. [arxiv_2402.05052] approach identifiability from the perspective of multiple distributions, considering settings where measured variables such as image pixels are functions of underlying latent causal variables such as concepts or objects. Their framework targets the recovery of both the latent variables and the causal graph governing them across changing environments, with the goal of supporting reliable prediction and principled intervention in non-stationary conditions [arxiv_2402.05052]. This multi-distribution perspective is particularly relevant for applications where distributional shift is endemic.

A distinct but related theoretical contribution is offered by Wendong et al. [arxiv_2305.17225], who introduce Causal Component Analysis (CauCA) as an intermediate problem bridging Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and full CRL. Whereas ICA recovers statistically independent latent variables and CRL targets causally related and thus often statistically dependent variables together with the unknown causal graph, CauCA occupies a middle ground that isolates specific identifiability challenges arising from causal dependence [arxiv_2305.17225]. This decomposition of the problem space is theoretically illuminating, as it clarifies which aspects of identifiability are inherited from classical ICA theory and which require genuinely new causal reasoning.

Taken together, these works reveal a progressive relaxation of assumptions in identifiability theory for causal representations, moving from linear and supervised settings toward nonparametric, multi-environment, and intervention-agnostic frameworks, each contributing distinct technical tools and expanding the theoretical foundations of the field.
