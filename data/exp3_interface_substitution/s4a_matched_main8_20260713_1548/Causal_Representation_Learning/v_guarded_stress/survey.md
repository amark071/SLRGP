# Causal Representation Learning

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning

Disentangled causal representation learning has emerged as a critical research direction that bridges the goals of learning interpretable latent structures with the principled reasoning afforded by causal modeling. A central motivation across this body of work is the inadequacy of standard disentanglement approaches, which typically impose independence assumptions on latent variables. As several authors have noted, such assumptions are overly restrictive and fail to capture the causal dependencies that frequently govern real-world generative processes [arxiv_2010.02637; arxiv_2304.09010].

Early disentanglement methods operated under unsupervised settings with independence priors, yet these approaches have been shown to be insufficient when the underlying factors of variation are causally related rather than statistically independent [arxiv_2112.05746]. Shen et al. directly address this limitation by proposing the Disentangled gEnerative cAusal Representation (DEAR) learning framework, which explicitly models causal relationships among latent factors rather than enforcing their independence [arxiv_2010.02637]. Their work demonstrates that methods relying on independent priors systematically fail to disentangle causally structured factors, motivating the need for supervision signals that can identify the causal structure underlying the data. This represents a meaningful departure from purely unsupervised disentanglement paradigms and situates weak supervision as a practical mechanism for recovering causally meaningful representations.

Complementing this perspective, Reddy et al. examine the notion of causally disentangled representations more broadly, arguing that fairness and interpretability goals—long associated with disentangled representations—are better served when the generative process is understood through a causal lens [arxiv_2112.05746]. Their work extends prior explorations of weak supervision and correlated features by explicitly incorporating a causal view of the data-generating mechanism, thereby connecting disentanglement to the structural causal model literature. This causal framing is significant because it provides a principled basis for evaluating whether learned representations genuinely reflect the underlying factors of variation or merely capture spurious statistical regularities.

From an architectural standpoint, Fan et al. propose a Causal Flow-based Variational Auto-Encoder that integrates normalizing flows with a causal prior to model interdependencies among generative factors [arxiv_2304.09010]. This approach directly confronts the independence assumption embedded in standard VAE-based disentanglement methods, offering a generative framework capable of representing causal factor interactions. The use of flow-based models to parameterize causal structure provides greater flexibility than fixed graphical priors and aligns with the broader goal of learning low-dimensional representations where each dimension corresponds to a meaningful generative factor.

More recently, Moran and Aragam situate disentangled causal representation learning within the broader challenge of interpretability in deep generative models, arguing that the implicit representations learned by modern generative AI systems lack the transparency necessary for reliable deployment [arxiv_2504.11609]. Their work frames causal representation learning as a pathway toward interpretable generative models, underscoring the practical stakes of this research agenda beyond theoretical identifiability. Taken together, these contributions reflect a coherent progression: from recognizing the failure of independence-based disentanglement, through the incorporation of causal structure and weak supervision, toward scalable architectures and interpretability-oriented objectives.

### Identifiability Theory for Causal Representations

Identifiability theory constitutes a foundational pillar of causal representation learning (CRL), addressing the central question of when and under what conditions latent causal variables and their relational structure can be uniquely recovered from high-dimensional observations. A growing body of work has sought to establish rigorous guarantees under progressively weaker assumptions, moving the field toward more practically applicable frameworks.

A central contribution in this space is the work of von Kügelgen and colleagues, who study nonparametric identifiability of causal representations from unknown interventions [2306.00542]. Their framework departs from prior approaches that relied on weak supervision such as counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views, temporal structure, or restrictive assumptions like linearity on the mixing function or the latent causal model. By relaxing these constraints, the work broadens the theoretical scope of what can be identified without strong parametric commitments, representing a meaningful step toward more general identifiability results.

Complementing this direction, Varıcı and colleagues establish both identifiability and achievability results under a fully general nonparametric latent causal model combined with a general transformation mapping latent variables to observations [2310.15450]. A notable feature of their framework is the use of two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph, where crucially the learner does not need to know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node. This relaxation of the pairing assumption is practically significant, as it removes a form of supervision that is often unavailable in real-world settings. Compared to [2306.00542], which also pursues nonparametric identifiability, the work in [2310.15450] places particular emphasis on achievability—demonstrating not only that identification is theoretically possible but that it can be realized algorithmically.

Zhang and colleagues approach the identifiability problem from the perspective of multiple distributions, proposing a general setting in which measured variables are arbitrary functions of latent causal variables and the goal is to recover both the variables and the causal graph [2402.05052]. Their framework accommodates a broad class of distribution shifts, making it relevant to problems involving changing environments or interventional data drawn from heterogeneous sources. This multi-distribution perspective offers a complementary angle to the intervention-centric analyses of [2306.00542] and [2310.15450], highlighting that distributional diversity across environments can itself serve as an identification lever.

A further contribution that bridges independent component analysis (ICA) and CRL is Causal Component Analysis (CauCA), introduced by Wendong and colleagues [2305.17225]. CauCA is framed as an intermediate problem between classical ICA, which targets statistically independent latent variables, and full CRL, which targets causally related and thus often statistically dependent variables along with their unknown graph structure. By situating CauCA between these two paradigms, the authors provide a structured theoretical stepping stone, and their identifiability analysis clarifies how causal dependence among latent variables complicates recovery relative to the independent case.

Taken together, these works collectively advance identifiability theory for causal representations by progressively weakening assumptions about supervision, parametric form, and intervention knowledge, while extending the scope of what can be formally guaranteed across diverse data-generating settings [2306.00542, 2310.15450, 2402.05052, 2305.17225].
