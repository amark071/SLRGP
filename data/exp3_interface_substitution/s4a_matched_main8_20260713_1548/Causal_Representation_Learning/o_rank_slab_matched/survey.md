# Causal Representation Learning

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-4

Early efforts in causal representation learning have converged on a shared recognition that standard disentanglement methods, which enforce statistical independence among latent variables, are insufficient for capturing the richer relational structure that governs real-world generative processes. This subsection reviews four foundational contributions that collectively reframe the problem by incorporating causal structure into the latent space.

A central critique motivating this line of work is articulated by [arxiv_2010.02637], which demonstrates formally that methods relying on independent priors fail to disentangle causally related factors of variation. In response, the authors propose DEAR (Disentangled gEnerative cAusal Representation learning), a method that explicitly models causal dependencies among latent variables under weakly supervised conditions. By moving beyond the independence assumption, DEAR represents a principled departure from earlier variational approaches and establishes that appropriate supervisory signals can render causally structured representations identifiable.

This concern with the inadequacy of independence-based disentanglement is echoed and extended by [arxiv_2112.05746], which similarly argues that prior work on representation learning has lacked a causal view of the generative process. While [arxiv_2010.02637] focuses on the generative modeling machinery and the role of weak supervision, [arxiv_2112.05746] approaches the problem from the perspective of evaluating and characterizing causally disentangled representations more broadly, with attention to practical concerns such as fairness and interpretability. Together, these two contributions establish a shared theoretical foundation: that disentanglement must be understood through the lens of causal structure rather than mere statistical independence, and that doing so has tangible downstream benefits for model trustworthiness.

Building on this foundation, [arxiv_2210.13647] advances the identifiability agenda by targeting a specific limitation common to both prior works—namely, the reliance on restrictive functional form assumptions such as independent sources or linear transitions between latent states. The authors introduce a temporally disentangled representation learning framework that exploits temporal structure as a form of side information, enabling strong identifiability results for causally related latent variables without requiring these constraining assumptions. This temporal approach complements the weakly supervised strategy of [arxiv_2010.02637] by offering an alternative source of inductive bias that is more naturally available in sequential data settings.

Whereas the above three contributions focus primarily on the structure of the latent space and the conditions under which it can be identified, [arxiv_2207.05723] addresses the complementary challenge of recovering causal relationships from high-dimensional observations. The authors introduce Decoder BCD, a latent variable decoder model designed for Bayesian causal discovery, framing the problem explicitly as one of learning causal representations from complex data. This work is notable for its probabilistic treatment of causal structure, which stands in contrast to the more deterministic identifiability arguments pursued in [arxiv_2010.02637] and [arxiv_2210.13647]. Collectively, these four contributions delineate the core tensions in causal representation learning: between supervised and unsupervised regimes, between identifiability theory and practical inference, and between modeling latent causal structure and recovering it from observed data.

### Rank slab 2: positions 5-8

The theoretical foundations of causal representation learning (CRL) have advanced considerably through work addressing identifiability under increasingly general conditions. A central challenge in this literature is determining when latent causal variables and their relational structure can be uniquely recovered from observed high-dimensional data, without imposing overly restrictive assumptions on either the mixing function or the underlying causal model.

Von Kügelgen and colleagues [arxiv_2306.00542] make a notable contribution by investigating nonparametric identifiability of causal representations from unknown interventions. Their work departs from prior approaches that typically depend on weak supervision—such as counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views or temporal structure—or that impose linearity constraints on the mixing function or latent causal model. By relaxing these assumptions, [arxiv_2306.00542] broadens the theoretical scope of identifiability results, demonstrating that meaningful recovery of latent causal structure is achievable under more realistic and less constrained settings. This represents a significant step toward closing the gap between idealized theoretical frameworks and the messier conditions encountered in practice.

Complementing this line of inquiry, Varıcı and colleagues [arxiv_2310.15450] address CRL under a fully general nonparametric latent causal model combined with a general transformation model mapping latent variables to observations. Their central contribution is the establishment of both identifiability and achievability results using two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph. A particularly notable feature of their framework is that one need not know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node—a practically important relaxation that reduces the supervision burden. Together, [arxiv_2306.00542] and [arxiv_2310.15450] form a coherent body of work pushing identifiability theory toward greater generality, though they differ in their specific intervention assumptions and the mechanisms through which identifiability is established.

Alongside these theoretical developments, a parallel strand of research focuses on constructing deep learning architectures capable of operationalizing causal representation learning. Deng and colleagues [arxiv_2211.03374] provide a broad survey of deep causal learning, encompassing representation, discovery, and inference. They highlight the limitations of traditional causal learning methods when confronted with high-dimensional unstructured variables, combinatorial optimization challenges, and unobserved confounders—precisely the difficulties that motivate the theoretical work in [arxiv_2306.00542] and [arxiv_2310.15450]. Their review situates deep learning as a promising vehicle for overcoming these obstacles, though it also acknowledges the substantial open problems that remain.

More specifically, Fan and colleagues [arxiv_2304.09010] propose a causal flow-based variational autoencoder designed for disentangled causal representation learning. Their approach directly addresses a limitation common to many VAE-based methods: the assumption of independence among generative factors. In real-world scenarios, latent factors are frequently interdependent and exhibit causal structure, and [arxiv_2304.09010] incorporates normalizing flows to model these dependencies explicitly. This architectural innovation connects the practical modeling agenda to the theoretical concerns raised by [arxiv_2306.00542] and [arxiv_2310.15450], illustrating how identifiability insights can inform the design of generative models that respect causal structure rather than assuming it away.
