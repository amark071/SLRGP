# Causal Representation Learning

### Injected invalid unbalanced organization

Causal representation learning (CRL) has emerged as a principled framework for recovering latent causal variables and their structural relationships from high-dimensional observational data, yet the field remains characterized by a heterogeneous collection of assumptions, methodological commitments, and theoretical guarantees that do not always cohere into a unified research program. A close reading of the literature reveals an organization that is, in important respects, unbalanced: certain sub-problems—particularly identifiability under interventional supervision—have attracted concentrated formal attention, while others, such as the integration of causal structure into generative modeling for practical interpretability, remain comparatively underspecified. This subsection surveys the landscape of CRL with attention to these asymmetries.

A foundational tension in the field concerns the treatment of statistical dependence among latent factors. Classical disentangled representation learning, rooted in variational autoencoders, assumes that the true generative factors are mutually independent, an assumption that simplifies inference but is demonstrably violated in most real-world settings [arxiv_2010.02637]. Shen and colleagues argue explicitly that prior methods with independent priors fail to disentangle causally related factors, motivating their Disentangled Generative Causal Representation (DEAR) framework, which accommodates causal relationships among latent variables under weak supervision [arxiv_2010.02637]. This critique is echoed by Reddy and colleagues, who observe that even more recent approaches incorporating weak supervision or correlated features have proceeded without a causal view of the generative process, leaving a conceptual gap between the statistical machinery of disentanglement and the causal semantics that practitioners often implicitly invoke [arxiv_2112.05746]. Fan and colleagues further reinforce this diagnosis, noting that the independence simplification does not hold in many real-world scenarios where factors are interdependent and exhibit causal structure, and they propose a causal flow-based VAE to address this limitation [arxiv_2304.09010]. Taken together, these contributions converge on the same structural critique, yet they do so largely in parallel, with limited cross-citation and without a shared formal language for characterizing the degree to which causal dependence among latent variables undermines identifiability.

On the identifiability side, the literature is considerably more technically developed but exhibits its own imbalances. Von Kügelgen and colleagues study nonparametric identifiability of causal representations from unknown interventions, relaxing prior work that relied on linearity assumptions, known intervention targets, or restrictive parametric forms for the mixing function or latent causal model [arxiv_2306.00542]. Their contribution is notable for its generality, yet it presupposes access to counterfactual or interventional views of the data, a form of supervision that may be unavailable in many applied settings. Varıcı and colleagues pursue a complementary direction, establishing identifiability and achievability results under a general nonparametric latent causal model using two hard uncoupled interventions per node, and crucially demonstrating that one need not know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node [arxiv_2310.15450]. This relaxation of the known-target assumption represents genuine progress, but the requirement for hard interventions per node still constitutes a strong structural demand on the data-collection process. Zhang and colleagues address a somewhat different facet of the same problem, considering the recovery of latent causal variables and their relational graph from multiple distributions without imposing linearity on the functional relationships [arxiv_2402.05052]. The cumulative picture is one in which identifiability theory has advanced substantially under interventional regimes, while the gap between these theoretical guarantees and the conditions available in observational or weakly supervised settings remains wide and undertheorized.

Wendong and colleagues introduce Causal Component Analysis (CauCA) as an intermediate problem situated between Independent Component Analysis and full CRL, recovering causally related latent variables together with the unknown causal graph [arxiv_2305.17225]. This framing is methodologically useful because it makes explicit the additional difficulty introduced by causal dependence relative to the ICA setting, and it provides a structured way to think about what assumptions are doing work in existing proofs. However, CauCA's position as an "intermediate" problem also highlights the organizational fragmentation of the field: it is not obvious how results proved in the CauCA setting translate to the richer settings considered by von Kügelgen and colleagues [arxiv_2306.00542] or Varıcı and colleagues [arxiv_2310.15450], and the relationships among these frameworks are not systematically mapped in the literature.

A further imbalance concerns the relationship between theoretical identifiability results and the practical goal of building interpretable generative models. Moran and Aragam explicitly orient their work toward interpretable deep generative models, arguing that the surprising performance of modern generative AI is accompanied by notorious opacity, and that CRL offers a path toward recovering meaningful latent structure [arxiv_2504.11609]. This motivation is broadly shared across the field, yet the majority of formal contributions focus on asymptotic or population-level identifiability rather than on finite-sample estimation, architectural design, or the downstream utility of recovered representations. The disentanglement-oriented contributions of Shen and colleagues [arxiv_2010.02637], Reddy and colleagues [arxiv_2112.05746], and Fan and colleagues [arxiv_2304.09010] are more directly concerned with practical generative modeling, but they engage less systematically with the identifiability literature, creating a division between theoretically rigorous but practically distant results and empirically motivated but theoretically undergrounded methods. This structural gap—between the identifiability-focused and the generative-modeling-focused strands of CRL—constitutes perhaps the most consequential organizational imbalance in the current literature, and addressing it will likely require both richer theoretical frameworks and more deliberate synthesis across sub-communities.
