# Causal Representation Learning

### Disentangled Causal Representation Learning

Disentangled causal representation learning has emerged as a significant research direction that seeks to move beyond classical disentanglement methods by explicitly incorporating causal structure into the latent space. Traditional disentanglement approaches typically assume statistical independence among latent factors, an assumption that proves overly restrictive in real-world settings where generative factors are often causally related to one another. Addressing this limitation has motivated a growing body of work that integrates causal reasoning into the design of representation learning frameworks.

A foundational contribution in this space is the Disentangled gEnerative cAusal Representation (DEAR) learning method proposed by [arxiv_2010.02637], which operates under weak supervision to learn representations in which the underlying factors of interest may be causally related rather than independent. The authors demonstrate that conventional disentanglement methods relying on independent priors systematically fail to disentangle causally structured factors, motivating the need for a generative model that explicitly encodes causal dependencies among latent variables. This insight is complementary to the analysis offered by [arxiv_2112.05746], which similarly critiques the independence assumption prevalent in earlier unsupervised disentanglement models and argues for a causal view of the generative process. While [arxiv_2010.02637] focuses on the practical failure modes of independent-prior methods and proposes a concrete supervised remedy, [arxiv_2112.05746] provides a broader conceptual framing of what it means for representations to be causally disentangled, examining how weak supervision and correlated features interact with causal generative assumptions.

On the architectural side, variational autoencoders (VAEs) have served as the predominant backbone for disentangled representation learning, yet most VAE-based methods inherit the independence assumption at the level of the prior distribution. [arxiv_2304.09010] addresses this gap by proposing a causal flow-based VAE that explicitly models interdependencies among generative factors through a causal structure, enabling the learned representations to reflect the true causal relationships present in the data. This approach acknowledges that real-world factors are frequently interdependent and that ignoring such dependencies leads to representations that are neither faithful nor interpretable. The concern with interpretability is echoed by [arxiv_2504.11609], which situates disentangled causal representation learning within the broader challenge of making deep generative models interpretable. That work argues that the implicit representations learned by deep neural networks, while powerful, remain opaque, and that causal representation learning offers a principled pathway toward models whose internal structure can be understood and audited.

Taken together, these contributions reveal a coherent trajectory in the field: from early disentanglement methods that assumed independence, through weakly supervised approaches that accommodate causal dependencies [arxiv_2010.02637, arxiv_2112.05746], to architecturally sophisticated generative models that embed causal structure directly into the latent space [arxiv_2304.09010], all motivated in part by the overarching goal of producing representations that are not only disentangled but also interpretable and aligned with the true causal mechanisms underlying observed data [arxiv_2504.11609].

### Identifiability Theory for Causal Representations

Identifiability theory constitutes the theoretical backbone of causal representation learning (CRL), addressing the fundamental question of when and under what conditions latent causal variables and their relational structure can be uniquely recovered from high-dimensional observations. A central challenge in this domain is that the mapping from latent causal variables to observed data is typically unknown, nonparametric, and potentially highly complex, making recovery without additional assumptions or data structures extremely difficult.

Early contributions to CRL identifiability often imposed restrictive assumptions, such as linearity of the mixing function or parametric constraints on the latent causal model, and frequently relied on forms of weak supervision including counterfactual pre- and post-intervention views or temporal structure [arxiv_2306.00542]. Recognizing these limitations, von Kügelgen and colleagues [arxiv_2306.00542] pursued a more general nonparametric identifiability framework, demonstrating that causal representations can be identified from unknown interventions without requiring such restrictive structural assumptions. Their work highlights the importance of leveraging distributional changes induced by interventions across multiple environments as a source of identifying information, even when the precise nature of those interventions is not observed.

Building on this direction, Varıcı and colleagues [arxiv_2310.15450] establish both identifiability and achievability results under a fully general nonparametric latent causal model combined with a general transformation model. A notable feature of their framework is that identifiability is achieved using two hard uncoupled interventions per node in the latent causal graph, and crucially, the learner need not know which pair of intervention environments corresponds to which node. This result is significant because it relaxes the assumption of known intervention targets, a condition that is often unrealistic in practice, while still guaranteeing recovery of the latent structure up to well-defined equivalence classes.

Complementing these intervention-based approaches, Zhang and colleagues [arxiv_2402.05052] address CRL from a multiple-distributions perspective under a general setting, focusing on recovering latent causal variables and their causal graph from observed variables that are arbitrary functions of the latents. Their framework accommodates a broad class of data-generating processes and distributional shifts, providing identifiability guarantees that do not presuppose specific intervention mechanisms, thereby extending the scope of CRL theory to settings where the source of distributional heterogeneity may be varied or unknown.

A conceptually distinct but closely related line of work explores the relationship between Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and CRL. Wendong and colleagues [arxiv_2305.17225] introduce Causal Component Analysis (CauCA) as an intermediate problem bridging ICA, which targets statistically independent latent variables, and CRL, which targets causally related and thus often statistically dependent latent variables along with their unknown causal graph. This framing clarifies the structural differences between the two problems and illuminates how identifiability arguments from ICA can be extended or adapted to the causally dependent setting.

Taken together, these contributions delineate a rapidly maturing identifiability theory for causal representations, progressively relaxing assumptions about linearity, known interventions, and independence, while establishing rigorous conditions under which latent causal structure can be recovered from observational and interventional data [arxiv_2306.00542; arxiv_2310.15450; arxiv_2402.05052; arxiv_2305.17225].
