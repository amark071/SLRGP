# Neural Architecture Search

### Survey and Overview of NAS

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a critical subfield of automated machine learning, driven by the recognition that the choice of network architecture is among the most consequential decisions in deep learning system design. As noted by Wistuba, Rawat, and colleagues, the growing interest in both automation of machine learning and deep learning has inevitably led to the development of a wide variety of automated methods for neural architecture search, with many advances in deep learning springing directly from architectural improvements [arxiv_1905.01392]. This observation situates NAS not merely as a convenience tool but as a foundational research direction with broad implications for the field.

At a high level, NAS methods can be organized according to the search strategies they employ. Kim and colleagues provide a useful taxonomy, classifying the main research trends of NAS into three broad families: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches [arxiv_2108.08474]. This tripartite classification reflects the historical trajectory of the field, from early evolutionary and RL-driven methods that required enormous computational budgets, toward more efficient one-shot and weight-sharing paradigms that dramatically reduce search costs. The survey by Kim et al. further emphasizes that the acceleration of search is a central concern motivating contemporary NAS research, underscoring the practical tension between architectural quality and computational feasibility [arxiv_2108.08474].

Reinforcement learning-based NAS has attracted particular scholarly attention as one of the earliest and most influential paradigms. Shao's dedicated survey on RL-based NAS highlights that while deep learning has successfully automated feature extraction, the structures and hyperparameters of deep neural networks still make a substantial difference to task performance, and the process of exploring optimal configurations has historically involved considerable human intervention [arxiv_2409.18163]. RL-based approaches address this by framing architecture design as a sequential decision-making problem, allowing an agent to iteratively propose and evaluate candidate architectures. Compared to the broader overview offered by Wistuba et al. [arxiv_1905.01392], Shao's work [arxiv_2409.18163] provides a more granular treatment of the RL-specific mechanisms, reward formulations, and controller designs that have been proposed to automate this process.

A further dimension of complexity arises when NAS is extended beyond single-objective optimization. Shariatzadeh and colleagues observe that earlier NAS attempts focused exclusively on prediction accuracy, but that multi-objective NAS (MO-NAS) has since emerged to simultaneously optimize competing criteria such as accuracy, latency, energy consumption, and model size [arxiv_2307.09099]. This development reflects the growing deployment of neural networks in resource-constrained environments where raw accuracy is insufficient as a sole criterion. The relationship between MO-NAS and the broader AutoML and hyperparameter optimization literature is also noted by Shariatzadeh et al., situating NAS within a wider ecosystem of automated model design [arxiv_2307.09099].

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that has matured from computationally prohibitive but pioneering RL and evolutionary methods toward more efficient and multi-faceted search frameworks, with ongoing challenges around search cost, transferability, and the balancing of multiple real-world performance objectives.

### Differentiable and Continuous Search Methods

Differentiable and continuous search methods represent a significant departure from earlier discrete-space approaches to neural architecture search (NAS), offering substantial gains in computational efficiency and optimization tractability. Early NAS methods grounded in reinforcement learning (RL) or evolutionary algorithms operated over discrete architecture spaces, a characteristic that rendered the search process highly inefficient [arxiv_1808.07233]. To address this limitation, Luo, Tian, and colleagues proposed mapping discrete architecture representations into a continuous embedding space, enabling gradient-based optimization to guide the search process [arxiv_1808.07233]. This reformulation is conceptually important because it transforms what was previously a combinatorial problem into one amenable to standard continuous optimization techniques.

The broader trend toward differentiable NAS (DNAS) has been comprehensively surveyed by Heuillet and colleagues, who observe that DNAS rapidly established itself as the dominant paradigm for automated architecture discovery, largely owing to the influence of DARTS as one of the first major differentiable methods [arxiv_2304.05405]. Compared with RL- and evolutionary algorithm-based predecessors, DNAS methods are faster by several orders of magnitude, a property that has driven their widespread adoption [arxiv_2304.05405]. The central mechanism shared across DNAS approaches is the relaxation of the discrete architecture selection problem into a continuous hyperparameter optimization problem, typically solved via gradient descent through a jointly trained supernet.

Guo, Wu, and colleagues extend this line of work with a focus on efficiency for resource-constrained deployment scenarios, noting that a fundamental bottleneck in NAS more broadly is the requirement to train a large number of candidate architectures [arxiv_2103.02126]. Their differentiable neural architecture learning framework targets embedded and mobile platforms, situating efficiency not merely as a search-time concern but as a deployment-time objective [arxiv_2103.02126]. This dual emphasis distinguishes their contribution from earlier continuous methods that prioritized search speed without explicitly optimizing for inference-time hardware constraints.

Despite the appeal of gradient-based continuous search, important critiques have emerged regarding its reliability. Mills and colleagues raise concerns about the effectiveness and generalizability of gradient methods when applied to non-convex architecture optimization landscapes [arxiv_2109.12425]. Their work, L²NAS, proposes an alternative that retains the continuous-action formulation but replaces gradient descent with continuous-action reinforcement learning, arguing that RL-based optimization is better suited to navigating the non-convex structure of the architecture search problem [arxiv_2109.12425]. This positions L²NAS as a hybrid approach that borrows the continuous search space formulation pioneered by differentiable methods while rejecting gradient descent as the optimization engine, thereby bridging the conceptual divide between RL-based and differentiable NAS paradigms.

Taken together, these works illustrate an evolving consensus that continuity in the search space is a desirable property, while the question of which optimization algorithm best exploits that continuity remains actively debated. The transition from discrete to continuous search spaces [arxiv_1808.07233], the rise of DARTS-inspired differentiable methods [arxiv_2304.05405], the hardware-aware extensions of DNAS [arxiv_2103.02126], and the RL-based challenges to gradient optimization [arxiv_2109.12425] collectively define the current frontier of this research direction.
