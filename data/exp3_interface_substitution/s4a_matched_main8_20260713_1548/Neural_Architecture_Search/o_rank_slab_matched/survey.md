# Neural Architecture Search

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-4

The field of neural architecture search (NAS) has attracted substantial scholarly attention as researchers seek to automate the design of high-performing deep learning models. Comprehensive surveys have documented the rapid proliferation of NAS methods, noting that the choice of network architecture is critical to deep learning performance and that many significant advances in the field stem directly from architectural improvements [arxiv_1905.01392]. The computational intensity of deep learning, however, makes exhaustive manual architecture design increasingly impractical, motivating the systematic study and classification of automated search strategies.

Taxonomic efforts have helped organize the NAS landscape into coherent methodological families. Kim et al. [arxiv_2108.08474] classify the dominant research trends into three broad categories: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. This tripartite taxonomy reflects the historical progression of the field, from biologically inspired search heuristics through policy-gradient methods to more recent weight-sharing paradigms, and provides a useful framework for situating individual contributions relative to one another.

Among the most influential directions within NAS is the shift from discrete to continuous search spaces, motivated by the observation that discrete optimization is inherently inefficient. Luo et al. [arxiv_1808.07233] argue that methods grounded in reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms operate in discrete spaces that impose severe computational costs, and they propose instead an approach based on continuous optimization, enabling gradient-based techniques to be applied directly to architecture design. This reformulation of architecture search as a continuous optimization problem represents a conceptual departure from earlier combinatorial approaches and has opened new avenues for efficiency.

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of gradient-based methods for NAS is not without controversy. Mills et al. [arxiv_2109.12425] acknowledge that differentiable architecture search, which converts the search over discrete architectures into a hyperparameter optimization problem solvable by gradient descent, has achieved remarkable results, but they raise substantive concerns about the generalizability and effectiveness of gradient methods when applied to non-convex architecture optimization problems. In response to these limitations, Mills et al. propose L²NAS, which reframes architecture search using continuous-action reinforcement learning rather than relying solely on gradient descent, thereby attempting to retain the benefits of a continuous search space while circumventing the pitfalls of pure gradient-based optimization.

Taken together, these works illustrate a clear trajectory in NAS research: from broad surveys that establish the scope and taxonomy of the field [arxiv_1905.01392, arxiv_2108.08474], through foundational proposals for continuous and differentiable search [arxiv_1808.07233], to more nuanced critiques and refinements that question whether gradient-based continuity alone is sufficient [arxiv_2109.12425]. The tension between search efficiency and optimization reliability emerges as a central theme, with successive contributions both building upon and challenging the assumptions of their predecessors.

### Rank slab 2: positions 5-8

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has evolved across several interconnected research directions, each addressing distinct limitations in the automation of deep learning design. Among the contributions occupying the middle tier of scholarly influence, four works collectively illuminate the breadth of methodological diversity in the field.

A foundational concern motivating much of this research is the sheer complexity of jointly optimising architectural choices and associated hyperparameters. Zela et al. [arxiv_1807.06906] demonstrate that treating these two optimisation problems sequentially — as was conventional practice — is fundamentally suboptimal, because architectural decisions and hyperparameter settings interact in non-trivial ways. Their work further identifies that the common heuristic of using very few training epochs during the main NAS phase, followed by a much larger epoch budget during post-processing evaluation, introduces a systematic mismatch that can mislead the search. By proposing an efficient joint search framework, they argue that co-optimisation yields architectures whose performance is more faithfully estimated during the search itself. This insight has important downstream implications for any NAS paradigm that relies on proxy evaluations, and it implicitly challenges the validity of benchmarks that decouple these concerns.

Reinforcement learning (RL) constitutes one of the most historically prominent paradigms for NAS, and two of the reviewed works engage directly with its capabilities and limitations. Shao [arxiv_2409.18163] provides a broad survey of RL-based NAS, contextualising the field within the wider automation of feature extraction enabled by deep learning. The survey underscores that while deep neural networks have dramatically reduced the need for manual feature engineering, the exploration of optimal structures and hyperparameters remains labour-intensive, motivating the use of RL agents to navigate the architecture search space. Complementing this survey perspective, Cassimon, Mercelis, and Mets [arxiv_2410.01431] offer an empirical contribution by evaluating a novel scalable RL-based solution that learns to search for good architectures rather than converging on a single optimal design. Crucially, their agent is assessed on both NAS-Bench-101 and NAS-Bench-301, enabling comparison against established strong baselines including local search methods. This dual-benchmark evaluation lends credibility to their scalability claims and situates their contribution within a reproducible experimental ecosystem. Taken together, these two RL-focused works span the descriptive and the prescriptive: Shao [arxiv_2409.18163] maps the landscape, while Cassimon et al. [arxiv_2410.01431] advance it with a concrete algorithmic proposal.

A fourth distinct direction concerns the applicability of NAS under resource-constrained, few-data conditions. Rong et al. [arxiv_2110.05842] identify that standard NAS requires both abundant labelled data and substantial compute, prerequisites that are rarely satisfied in meta-learning scenarios. Their proposed Across-Task NAS (AT-NAS) addresses this by combining gradient-based meta-learning with evolutionary algorithm-based NAS, enabling the transfer of architectural knowledge across tasks. This cross-task perspective is notably absent from the other three works reviewed here, which largely assume sufficient resources for single-task optimisation. The integration of meta-learning principles into NAS thus represents a qualitatively different research trajectory, one that prioritises generalisation and efficiency over raw performance on well-resourced benchmarks.
