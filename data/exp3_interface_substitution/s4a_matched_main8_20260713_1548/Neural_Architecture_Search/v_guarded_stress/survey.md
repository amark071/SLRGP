# Neural Architecture Search

### Survey and Overview of NAS

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a critical subfield of automated machine learning, driven by the recognition that the choice of network architecture is fundamentally consequential to deep learning performance. As noted by [arxiv_1905.01392], many of the most significant advances in deep learning can be traced directly to architectural improvements, yet the design of such architectures has historically demanded substantial human expertise and computational resources. The growing interest in automating this process has consequently produced a wide variety of NAS methods, each attempting to reduce the burden of manual architecture engineering while maintaining or improving predictive performance.

Early surveys of the field sought to impose taxonomic order on this rapidly expanding body of work. [arxiv_1905.01392] provides a broad overview of automated methods for neural architecture search, situating NAS within the wider context of automated machine learning (AutoML) and acknowledging the computational intensity that has long constrained practical adoption. Building on this foundation, [arxiv_2108.08474] offers a more focused classification of research trends, organising the NAS landscape into three principal paradigms: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. This tripartite taxonomy reflects a natural progression in the field, from biologically inspired search heuristics toward gradient-based and sample-efficient methods that seek to accelerate the overall search process. Indeed, the explicit emphasis on acceleration in [arxiv_2108.08474] signals a maturing research agenda in which computational efficiency has become as important a criterion as raw predictive accuracy.

The reinforcement learning strand of NAS has itself attracted dedicated survey attention. [arxiv_2409.18163] focuses specifically on RL-based NAS, contextualising it within the broader challenge of automating not only feature extraction but also the optimisation of network structures and hyperparameters. The paper highlights that the manual exploration of these design spaces involves considerable human intervention, motivating the use of RL agents to navigate architecture search spaces more systematically. This perspective complements the broader taxonomy offered by [arxiv_2108.08474] by providing greater depth on one of its three identified paradigms.

A further evolution in the field is captured by [arxiv_2307.09099], which surveys multi-objective NAS. Whereas earlier NAS efforts, as [arxiv_2307.09099] observes, focused predominantly on optimising prediction accuracy as a single objective, Multi-Objective NAS (MONAS) extends the search to simultaneously consider competing criteria such as model size, latency, and energy consumption. This shift reflects the growing deployment of deep learning models in resource-constrained environments and connects NAS closely to the broader concerns of Hyperparameter Optimisation and AutoML. Taken together, these surveys illustrate a clear trajectory: from foundational efforts to automate architecture design [arxiv_1905.01392], through the classification and acceleration of core search strategies [arxiv_2108.08474] and the deepening of RL-based approaches [arxiv_2409.18163], toward a more holistic, multi-objective conception of what constitutes an optimal architecture [arxiv_2307.09099]. This evolution underscores both the maturity and the continued dynamism of NAS as a research domain.

### Differentiable and Continuous Search Methods

Differentiable and continuous search methods represent a significant departure from earlier discrete-space approaches to neural architecture search (NAS), which relied on reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms and were widely criticised for their computational inefficiency. As noted in [arxiv_1808.07233], conventional methods that operate over discrete architecture spaces are highly inefficient, motivating the development of continuous relaxations that permit gradient-based optimisation. This insight underpins the broader shift toward differentiable NAS (DNAS), wherein the combinatorial search problem is reformulated as a continuous hyperparameter optimisation task amenable to standard gradient descent procedures.

The survey by [arxiv_2304.05405] situates this transition historically, attributing much of the momentum behind DNAS to the popularity of DARTS—one of the first major methods in this paradigm—and observing that DNAS is faster than reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithm-based predecessors by several orders of magnitude. This dramatic reduction in computational cost has made differentiable search methods the dominant trend in automated neural network design in recent years. Similarly, [arxiv_2103.02126] highlights the growing attention paid to automated CNN design, particularly in the context of deployment on embedded and mobile platforms, where efficiency constraints make the scalability of DNAS especially attractive compared to approaches that require training large numbers of candidate architectures.

Despite these advantages, differentiable methods are not without limitations. [arxiv_2109.12425] raises substantive concerns about the effectiveness and generalisability of gradient-based methods when applied to non-convex architecture optimisation problems. Because the underlying search landscape is non-convex, gradient descent may converge to poor local optima or exhibit instability, undermining the reliability of the discovered architectures. To address these shortcomings, [arxiv_2109.12425] proposes L²NAS, which reframes architecture search as a continuous-action reinforcement learning problem, thereby retaining the continuous parameterisation that enables efficient search while replacing gradient descent with a reinforcement learning policy that is potentially better suited to navigating non-convex spaces. This represents an interesting hybrid position: the method preserves the continuous search space introduced by differentiable approaches but departs from pure gradient optimisation, suggesting that the two paradigms need not be mutually exclusive.

The method proposed in [arxiv_1808.07233], termed Neural Architecture Optimisation, similarly encodes architectures into a continuous space and performs search via gradient-based updates, offering a straightforward and efficient alternative to discrete search. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2103.02126] introduces differentiable neural architecture learning with an explicit focus on efficiency for resource-constrained hardware, reinforcing the practical motivation that runs throughout this line of research.

Taken together, these works illustrate both the promise and the open challenges of differentiable and continuous NAS. While the reformulation of architecture search as a continuous optimisation problem has yielded substantial gains in search efficiency [arxiv_2304.05405], [arxiv_1808.07233], concerns about non-convexity and generalisation [arxiv_2109.12425] continue to motivate methodological innovation, and the tension between gradient-based and reinforcement learning-based continuous search remains an active area of investigation.
