# Neural Architecture Search

### Survey and Overview of NAS

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a critical subfield of automated machine learning, driven by the recognition that the choice of network architecture is itself a decisive factor in the performance of deep learning systems. As [arxiv_1905.01392] observe, many of the most significant advances in deep learning can be traced directly to architectural improvements, yet the design of such architectures has historically demanded substantial human expertise and computational resources. NAS seeks to automate this process, and the rapid proliferation of methods in this space has motivated several comprehensive surveys aimed at organizing and contextualizing the field.

A foundational observation shared across the literature is that NAS methods can be broadly categorized according to their underlying search strategies. [arxiv_2108.08474] classify the principal research trends into three families: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning-based algorithms, and one-shot architecture search approaches. This tripartite taxonomy reflects both the historical development of the field and the practical trade-offs between search quality and computational cost. Reinforcement learning-based approaches, in particular, have attracted sustained attention, and [arxiv_2409.18163] devote an entire survey to this paradigm, noting that while deep learning has successfully automated feature extraction, the exploration of optimal network structures and hyperparameters has continued to require considerable human intervention—a gap that reinforcement learning-guided NAS directly addresses.

A recurring theme across these surveys is the tension between predictive accuracy and practical efficiency. Early NAS efforts concentrated almost exclusively on maximizing task performance, typically measured as classification accuracy on benchmark datasets. However, as [arxiv_2307.09099] document, this single-objective framing has increasingly given way to multi-objective formulations that simultaneously optimize for accuracy alongside hardware-relevant constraints such as latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption. This shift reflects the growing deployment of deep learning models on resource-constrained edge devices, where a model that achieves marginal accuracy gains at the cost of prohibitive inference time is of limited practical value. The relationship between NAS and the broader AutoML ecosystem is also highlighted by [arxiv_2307.09099], who situate multi-objective NAS within the context of hyperparameter optimization, underscoring that architecture search is rarely separable from the tuning of other model configuration choices.

The computational burden of NAS remains a central concern. [arxiv_1905.01392] emphasize that deep learning techniques are inherently computationally intensive, a challenge that is compounded when the learning algorithm must itself search over a large space of candidate architectures. One-shot and weight-sharing strategies, identified by [arxiv_2108.08474] as a major trend, have been developed precisely to mitigate these costs by amortizing the evaluation of candidate architectures across a shared supernet. Collectively, these surveys illustrate that the field has matured from early proof-of-concept demonstrations toward a diverse ecosystem of methods that balance automation, efficiency, and multi-criteria optimization, setting the stage for the more targeted methodological contributions reviewed in subsequent sections.

### Differentiable and Continuous Search Methods

Differentiable and continuous search methods represent a significant departure from earlier discrete-space approaches to neural architecture search (NAS), which relied on reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms and were widely criticized for their computational inefficiency. As noted in foundational work on neural architecture optimization, conducting architecture search in a discrete space is highly inefficient, motivating the shift toward continuous relaxations that permit gradient-based optimization [arxiv_1808.07233]. This transition has proven consequential: differentiable NAS (DNAS) methods are faster by several orders of magnitude compared to their reinforcement learning or evolutionary counterparts, a property that has driven their rapid adoption and made DARTS one of the most influential early instantiations of the paradigm [arxiv_2304.05405].

The core principle underlying differentiable search is the relaxation of the discrete architecture selection problem into a continuous hyperparameter optimization problem solvable by gradient descent [arxiv_2109.12425]. By embedding candidate operations within a continuous and differentiable space, these methods allow architecture parameters and network weights to be jointly optimized, dramatically reducing the search cost relative to approaches that require training each candidate architecture independently. Luo, Tian, and colleagues formalized this intuition by proposing a method that maps architectures into a continuous representation, enabling efficient gradient-based traversal of the architecture space [arxiv_1808.07233]. Similarly, Guo, Wu, and colleagues developed a differentiable neural architecture learning framework specifically targeting efficient network design for resource-constrained environments such as embedded and mobile platforms, where the prohibitive cost of evaluating large numbers of candidate architectures under reinforcement learning or evolutionary schemes is particularly problematic [arxiv_2103.02126].

Despite their appeal, differentiable methods are not without limitations. Questions have been raised regarding the effectiveness and generalizability of gradient descent for solving non-convex architecture optimization problems, since the loss landscape over architecture parameters can be highly irregular and gradient signals may not reliably guide the search toward well-performing discrete architectures [arxiv_2109.12425]. This concern motivates hybrid approaches: Mills and colleagues propose L²NAS, which reframes architecture search as a continuous-action reinforcement learning problem, thereby retaining the continuous parameterization that enables efficient optimization while avoiding exclusive reliance on gradient methods that may struggle with non-convexity [arxiv_2109.12425]. This work illustrates that the boundary between differentiable and reinforcement learning-based NAS is not always sharp; continuous-action spaces can serve as a bridge between the two paradigms.

The survey by Heuillet and colleagues provides a broader perspective, situating DNAS within the historical trajectory of NAS research and cataloguing the variety of techniques that have emerged to address known weaknesses of the basic differentiable framework, including issues of search space design, performance estimation, and the gap between the relaxed continuous problem and the final discrete architecture [arxiv_2304.05405]. Taken together, the literature suggests that while differentiable and continuous search methods have substantially advanced the efficiency of automated architecture design [arxiv_1808.07233; arxiv_2103.02126], ongoing work continues to interrogate their theoretical foundations and practical reliability [arxiv_2109.12425; arxiv_2304.05405].
