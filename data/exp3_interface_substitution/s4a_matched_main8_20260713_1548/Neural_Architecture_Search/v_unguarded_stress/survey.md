# Neural Architecture Search

### Injected invalid unbalanced organization

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a transformative paradigm within the broader landscape of automated machine learning, driven by the recognition that the choice of network architecture is critically determinative of model performance across a wide range of tasks. As noted in foundational survey work, the growing interest in both automation of machine learning and deep learning has inevitably led to the development of a wide variety of automated methods for neural architecture search, with many advances in deep learning springing directly from architectural improvements [arxiv_1905.01392]. This observation situates NAS not merely as a convenience but as a central research imperative, particularly given the computational intensity of deep learning techniques and the infeasibility of exhaustive manual design at scale.

The field has historically been organized around three principal methodological families: neuro-evolutionary algorithms, reinforcement learning (RL)-based algorithms, and one-shot or weight-sharing approaches [arxiv_2108.08474]. This tripartite taxonomy, while broadly accepted, masks considerable internal diversity and ongoing methodological evolution. Reinforcement learning approaches, for instance, treat architecture design as a sequential decision-making problem in which a controller policy is rewarded based on the validation performance of sampled architectures. A dedicated survey on RL-based NAS underscores that the process of exploring optimal structures and hyperparameters often involves substantial human intervention, and that automating this process through RL represents a meaningful reduction in the burden of neural network design [arxiv_2409.18163]. However, RL-based methods have historically suffered from prohibitive computational costs, requiring thousands of GPU-hours to evaluate candidate architectures in discrete search spaces.

This computational bottleneck motivated the development of continuous relaxation strategies, most prominently differentiable NAS (DNAS). By converting the search over discrete architectures into a hyperparameter optimization problem solvable by gradient descent, DNAS methods dramatically accelerated the search process by several orders of magnitude compared to prior RL or evolutionary approaches [arxiv_2304.05405]. The popularity of DARTS, one of the first major DNAS methods, catalyzed an entire subfield, and subsequent work has sought to address its limitations. Differentiable Neural Architecture Learning (DNAL), for example, was proposed to tackle the challenge that NAS confronts when a large number of candidate architectures must be trained, as occurs under reinforcement learning or evolutionary paradigms, by instead enabling gradient-based optimization over a continuous architecture representation [arxiv_2103.02126]. Similarly, Neural Architecture Optimization (NAO) proposed encoding architectures into a continuous space and performing optimization therein, arguing that existing methods based on RL or evolutionary algorithms conduct architecture search in a discrete space that is highly inefficient [arxiv_1808.07233]. These continuous-space methods share a common motivation but differ in their specific encoding mechanisms and optimization procedures, with NAO relying on an encoder-decoder framework and performance predictor, while DARTS and its descendants rely on architectural weight relaxation within a supernet.

Despite the appeal of differentiable methods, questions have been raised regarding their effectiveness and generalizability, particularly concerning the non-convex nature of the architecture optimization landscape [arxiv_2109.12425]. L²NAS addresses this concern by proposing continuous-action reinforcement learning as an alternative to pure gradient descent for navigating the architecture search space, arguing that RL-based continuous optimization can overcome some of the pathological behaviors observed in gradient-based DNAS, such as performance collapse and architecture discretization artifacts. This represents an interesting convergence of the RL and differentiable paradigms, suggesting that the boundaries between methodological families are more porous than the canonical taxonomy implies.

A further dimension of complexity is introduced when NAS is framed as a multi-objective optimization problem. Early NAS efforts focused predominantly on maximizing predictive accuracy, but this single-objective framing has increasingly given way to approaches that simultaneously optimize for accuracy, computational efficiency, memory footprint, and hardware-specific latency constraints [arxiv_2307.09099]. Multi-objective NAS is closely related to hyperparameter optimization and the broader AutoML agenda, and its rise reflects the practical necessity of deploying neural networks on resource-constrained platforms such as mobile and embedded devices [arxiv_2103.02126]. The tension between accuracy and efficiency is a recurring theme across the literature, and different methodological families handle this trade-off in distinct ways: evolutionary algorithms naturally accommodate multi-objective Pareto optimization, while differentiable methods typically incorporate hardware proxies as regularization terms within a scalar objective [arxiv_2307.09099].

Taken together, the NAS literature reflects a field that has matured considerably from its early RL-based origins toward a diverse ecosystem of methods spanning continuous optimization, multi-objective search, and hybrid RL-gradient approaches. The acceleration of search, whether through weight sharing, continuous relaxation, or more efficient RL formulations, remains a central preoccupation [arxiv_2108.08474], as does the question of how well architectures discovered in proxy settings transfer to target tasks and hardware configurations. The interplay between search efficiency and solution quality, and between single-objective and multi-objective formulations, continues to structure ongoing methodological debate and motivates the development of increasingly sophisticated search strategies [arxiv_2304.05405, arxiv_2409.18163].
