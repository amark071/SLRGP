# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has prompted a corresponding surge in survey literature aimed at systematically cataloguing the methods developed to make inference more computationally tractable. Taken together, these overviews reveal both the breadth of the problem and the diversity of proposed solutions, while differing in scope, taxonomy, and emphasis.

A foundational concern motivating all of these surveys is the tension between model capability and deployment feasibility. As [arxiv_2402.09748] observes, the significant memory and computational costs incurred during the inference process make it challenging to deploy large models on resource-constrained devices. This framing is echoed by [arxiv_2404.14294], which similarly notes that the substantial computational and memory requirements of LLM inference pose challenges for deployment in resource-constrained scenarios. Both surveys thus position efficient inference not as a peripheral engineering concern but as a central research priority that conditions the practical utility of LLMs at scale.

Where the surveys diverge is in their organizational logic and thematic coverage. [arxiv_2402.09748] approaches the problem from an explicitly algorithmic perspective, investigating compression and efficient inference methods through a taxonomy that foregrounds model-level transformations such as quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation. [arxiv_2404.14294] adopts a comparably systematic stance but places greater emphasis on the full inference pipeline, encompassing both algorithmic techniques and the hardware-software co-design considerations that determine real-world throughput. This distinction is meaningful: an algorithmic survey risks underweighting the systems-level constraints that often govern which optimizations are practically viable, while a pipeline-oriented survey may sacrifice depth on any single technique.

[arxiv_2401.02038] situates efficient inference within a broader intellectual context by reviewing the entire arc from training to deployment. Its observation that low-cost training and deployment of LLMs represent the future development trend frames efficiency not as a remedial afterthought but as a design principle that should inform model development from the outset. This longitudinal perspective complements the more narrowly scoped inference-focused surveys by connecting architectural and training-time decisions to downstream inference costs.

[arxiv_2408.03130] occupies a somewhat different register, functioning as a practitioner-oriented literature review that attends to the effects, challenges, and practical considerations associated with inference optimization. Its emphasis on the unique challenges and opportunities arising from the sheer scale and complexity of LLMs, and on novel model training, optimization, and deployment methods, positions it as a bridge between academic taxonomy and applied guidance. This practical orientation distinguishes it from the more classification-driven approaches of [arxiv_2402.09748] and [arxiv_2404.14294].

Collectively, these surveys establish that efficient LLM inference is a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated advances across algorithms, systems, and hardware. They also signal an emerging consensus that no single technique is sufficient; rather, gains are typically achieved through the combination of complementary methods. The existence of multiple concurrent surveys further underscores the urgency and dynamism of this research area, as the field moves rapidly enough that different groups, working in parallel, have independently identified the need for systematic synthesis.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The compression of large language models has emerged as a central research priority, driven by the substantial memory and computational demands that constrain practical deployment. A broad consensus in the literature holds that LLMs contain significant redundancy, and that techniques such as weight pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation can exploit this redundancy to reduce memory footprints and accelerate inference [arxiv_2409.17372]. These approaches are not mutually exclusive, and several works have explored their combination to achieve compounding efficiency gains.

Quantization, which reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, has attracted particular attention as a post-training strategy that avoids the cost of full retraining. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is widely regarded as an effective method to accelerate LLM inference without requiring access to the original training pipeline [arxiv_2408.08554]. However, PTQ is not without limitations. Low-bit quantization introduces non-trivial accuracy degradation, and deployment of quantized models faces practical engineering challenges that complicate real-world adoption [arxiv_2408.08554]. The ABQ-LLM framework directly addresses these challenges by targeting arbitrary-bit quantized inference acceleration, reflecting a broader trend toward more flexible and hardware-aware quantization schemes [arxiv_2408.08554].

Pruning, which removes weights or structural components deemed non-essential, represents another well-established compression strategy. In the context of Transformer-based models, pruning has been combined with quantization to meet the latency and throughput constraints demanded by industrial deployment scenarios [arxiv_2211.07715]. The Fast DistilBERT work illustrates this complementarity: compression techniques including both quantization and pruning are applied together to bring Transformer models within the performance envelopes required for production use on CPU hardware [arxiv_2211.07715]. This practical framing highlights that compression research is increasingly motivated not merely by academic benchmarks but by the hard constraints of real inference infrastructure.

Knowledge distillation, in which a smaller student model is trained to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher, offers a structurally different route to efficiency. Rather than modifying an existing model's weights or precision, distillation produces a new, more compact model that approximates the capabilities of its larger counterpart. The Fast DistilBERT work situates distillation within this same family of compression techniques, treating it alongside pruning and quantization as a complementary tool for reducing inference cost [arxiv_2211.07715].

An important perspective that cuts across all three paradigms concerns the relationship between model size and the efficiency of training and inference. Li et al. [arxiv_2002.11794] argue that, under fixed compute budgets, training larger models and subsequently compressing them can be more effective than training smaller models from scratch. This "train large, then compress" strategy reframes compression not as a remedial step but as an integral part of an efficient model development pipeline, suggesting that the benefits of scale and the benefits of compression are complementary rather than competing. Taken together, the literature reviewed here underscores that no single compression technique dominates universally; rather, the choice among pruning, quantization, and distillation—or their combination—depends on deployment constraints, available compute, and the acceptable trade-off between model size and task performance [arxiv_2409.17372; arxiv_2002.11794].
