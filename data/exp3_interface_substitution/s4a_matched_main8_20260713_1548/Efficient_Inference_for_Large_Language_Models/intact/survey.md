# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid growth of large language models has prompted a substantial body of survey literature dedicated to understanding and improving the efficiency of their inference pipelines. As LLMs have become central to a wide range of natural language processing tasks, the computational and memory demands associated with deploying these models at scale have emerged as a critical bottleneck, motivating researchers to synthesize progress across multiple optimization fronts.

Two particularly comprehensive surveys address the efficiency challenge from complementary angles. Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] approach the problem from an algorithmic perspective, examining compression and efficient inference methods with a structured taxonomy that encompasses techniques such as quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation. Their work is explicitly motivated by the difficulty of deploying large models on resource-constrained devices, where the memory and computational costs incurred during inference are prohibitive. Similarly, Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] survey the landscape of efficient LLM inference techniques, emphasizing the substantial computational and memory requirements that challenge deployment in resource-limited scenarios. While both surveys share a common motivation, Zhou et al. place particular emphasis on cataloguing the breadth of community efforts directed toward inference efficiency as a distinct engineering and research objective, offering a broad mapping of the field that complements the more algorithmically focused taxonomy of Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748].

Beyond surveys focused narrowly on inference efficiency, Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] provide a broader overview that situates inference optimization within the full lifecycle of large language models, from training through deployment. Their work, prompted in part by the widespread adoption of systems such as ChatGPT, reviews the evolution of training techniques alongside inference considerations, arguing that low-cost training and deployment represent the dominant future development trend. This wider framing is valuable for understanding how inference efficiency is not an isolated concern but is deeply intertwined with decisions made during model design and training.

Complementing these high-level surveys, Donisch et al. [arxiv_2408.03130] offer a practically oriented literature review that focuses on the effects, challenges, and real-world considerations associated with inference optimization techniques. Their work acknowledges that while LLMs are valued for their ability to adapt to new tasks without retraining, their scale and complexity introduce unique deployment challenges. This practical orientation distinguishes their contribution from the more taxonomically driven surveys of Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] and Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294], as it foregrounds the trade-offs and implementation difficulties that arise when optimization methods are applied in realistic settings.

Taken together, these works establish a clear consensus that inference efficiency is one of the defining challenges of the current LLM era. They collectively cover algorithmic strategies, system-level optimizations, and practical deployment considerations, providing a multi-layered foundation upon which more targeted research into specific efficiency techniques can be grounded [arxiv_2402.09748, arxiv_2404.14294, arxiv_2401.02038, arxiv_2408.03130].

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The computational and memory demands of large language models have motivated a substantial body of research into model compression, with three principal techniques — pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation — emerging as the dominant strategies for enabling efficient inference. These approaches are often treated as complementary rather than mutually exclusive, and recent work increasingly explores their combination within unified compression pipelines.

Quantization, which reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, has attracted particular attention as a post-training technique that requires no retraining of the original model. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is widely regarded as a practical and scalable path to inference acceleration, yet its deployment at very low bit-widths introduces well-documented challenges. As [arxiv_2408.08554] identifies, two major obstacles confront low-bit PTQ: the difficulty of maintaining accuracy under aggressive precision reduction and the hardware-level complexity of supporting arbitrary bit-width configurations efficiently. The ABQ-LLM framework proposed in that work directly addresses these challenges by enabling arbitrary-bit quantized inference acceleration, illustrating how the field is moving beyond fixed-precision schemes toward more flexible quantization strategies that can be tailored to diverse deployment constraints.

Pruning, which removes redundant weights or structural components from a model, is similarly motivated by the observation that large language models contain significant parameter redundancy. This premise is made explicit in [arxiv_2409.17372], which notes that weight pruning, quantization, and distillation all implicitly exploit the redundancy present in LLMs, targeting memory reduction and inference acceleration simultaneously. Importantly, however, [arxiv_2409.17372] also raises a critical limitation shared by most compression techniques: they concentrate predominantly on weight optimization while leaving other sources of inefficiency — such as architectural search and activation patterns — comparatively underexplored. This observation motivates a broader conception of efficiency that goes beyond compressing a fixed architecture.

Knowledge distillation offers a structurally different approach, training a smaller student model to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher. When combined with quantization and pruning, distillation can partially recover accuracy lost through aggressive compression. This pipeline logic is central to [arxiv_2211.07715], which demonstrates that combining quantization and pruning with distillation yields substantial throughput improvements for transformer-based models on CPU hardware, directly addressing the latency constraints that prevent large models from being deployed in production environments. The work underscores that compression techniques are most powerful when applied jointly rather than in isolation.

A complementary perspective on the relationship between model size and efficiency is offered by [arxiv_2002.11794], which challenges the assumption that smaller models should be trained from scratch for efficient inference. Their findings suggest that training a large model and subsequently compressing it can be more compute-efficient than training a small model directly, a result with significant implications for how practitioners should think about the interplay between pretraining scale and post-hoc compression. Taken together, these works reveal a field in which quantization, pruning, and distillation are no longer treated as isolated techniques but as interdependent tools whose combined application — and whose relationship to upstream training decisions — determines the practical efficiency of large language model deployment.
