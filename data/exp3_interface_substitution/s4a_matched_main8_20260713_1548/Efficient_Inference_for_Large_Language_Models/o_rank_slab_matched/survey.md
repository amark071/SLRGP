# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-4

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary artificial intelligence research, driven by the substantial memory and computational demands that these models impose during inference. A foundational concern motivating this body of work is that, while LLMs have achieved remarkable success across a wide range of natural language processing tasks, their scale renders direct deployment on resource-constrained hardware prohibitively expensive [arxiv_2402.09748]. This tension between model capability and practical deployability has catalysed a rich literature spanning weight pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, and architectural search, all oriented toward reducing memory footprints and accelerating inference without unacceptable degradation in model quality.

Broad surveys of the field provide essential taxonomic scaffolding for understanding these diverse approaches. Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] offer a comprehensive algorithmic perspective on compression and efficient inference for transformer-based LLMs, systematically categorising methods and situating them within the broader landscape of deployment constraints. Complementing this, Shen et al. [arxiv_2409.17372] observe that the majority of model compression research has concentrated on weight optimisation—encompassing pruning, quantization, and distillation—as a means of exploiting the redundancy inherent in large models. Crucially, however, they argue that this predominant focus on weights leaves underexplored a parallel dimension of structural and architectural efficiency, suggesting that the search for efficient LLMs must extend beyond parameter-level compression alone. Together, these two surveys delineate the current state of the field while also identifying its blind spots, providing a productive point of departure for more targeted investigations.

One particularly acute manifestation of the inference efficiency problem concerns the Key-Value (KV) cache, whose memory demands grow exponentially with context length. Liu et al. [arxiv_2508.06297] address this bottleneck directly, reviewing KV cache compression strategies as a distinct and increasingly critical subproblem within LLM inference optimisation. As context windows in modern LLMs continue to expand, the KV cache can come to dominate memory consumption, constraining both scalability and throughput. This review underscores that optimising the KV cache is not merely a secondary engineering concern but a first-order challenge for enabling efficient long-context inference, a point that distinguishes it from the weight-centric framing prevalent in earlier compression literature [arxiv_2409.17372].

The practical dimension of inference optimisation is further illuminated by work on smaller transformer architectures deployed in production settings. Shen et al. [arxiv_2211.07715] examine the deployment of DistilBERT on CPU hardware, demonstrating that quantization and pruning can be combined to satisfy the latency constraints typical of industrial applications. While this work predates the era of very large generative models and focuses on encoder-based architectures, it establishes important empirical grounding for the claim that compression techniques can bridge the gap between research-grade models and production-viable systems. The challenges it identifies—maximum throughput under latency budgets, hardware-aware optimisation—anticipate concerns that have only grown more acute as model scales have increased [arxiv_2402.09748]. Collectively, these works frame efficient LLM inference as a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated advances in weight compression, architectural design, and memory management.

### Rank slab 2: positions 5-8

The efficient deployment of large language models demands solutions that address memory, computation, and parameter overhead simultaneously, and a cluster of recent contributions has approached these challenges from complementary angles.

A foundational tension in the field concerns how model size interacts with resource constraints during both training and inference. Li et al. [arxiv_2002.11794] interrogate this relationship directly, arguing that the conventional instinct to constrain model size in order to satisfy hardware budgets may be misguided. Their empirical analysis of Transformer models on self-supervised pretraining and high-resource machine translation tasks demonstrates that training larger models and subsequently compressing them can yield better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs than training smaller models from scratch under the same compute envelope. This "train large, then compress" paradigm reframes compression not as a remediation strategy but as an integral component of the training pipeline, and it provides a theoretical grounding for the proliferation of post-training compression methods that have followed in subsequent years.

Building directly on the compression theme, Muñoz et al. [arxiv_2501.16372] advance the state of the art by combining low-rank adapter techniques with neural architecture search (NAS) for LLM compression. Their retrospective work surveys how low-rank representations, originally popularized for parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), can be synergized with automated architecture search to discover compact model configurations that retain task performance. Where Li et al. [arxiv_2002.11794] treat compression as a post-training step applied to a fully trained large model, Muñoz et al. [arxiv_2501.16372] integrate structural decisions about rank and layer configuration into a more principled search process, suggesting that the two approaches are complementary rather than competing: the "train large" philosophy supplies a strong initialization, while NAS-guided low-rank decomposition identifies the most efficient final architecture.

Memory consumption during inference introduces a distinct bottleneck that compression of model weights alone cannot fully resolve. As sequence lengths grow, the key-value (KV) cache—which stores intermediate attention states to avoid redundant recomputation—scales linearly and can dominate GPU memory usage. Dai et al. [arxiv_2404.15949] address this with CORM, a cache optimization strategy that leverages recent message information to reduce KV cache size without sacrificing generation quality. Their method targets the inference phase specifically, complementing weight-level compression techniques by attacking a different source of memory pressure. Taken together, the work of Li et al. [arxiv_2002.11794], Muñoz et al. [arxiv_2501.16372], and Dai et al. [arxiv_2404.15949] illustrate that efficient inference requires a multi-front strategy: reducing parameter counts through principled compression, optimizing adapter structures through architecture search, and managing runtime memory through cache eviction policies.

Situating these contributions within the broader landscape, Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] provide a comprehensive overview of LLM training and inference techniques, explicitly noting that low-cost training and deployment represent the dominant trajectory for future development. Their survey contextualizes the individual contributions described above within an evolving ecosystem where cost efficiency is no longer a secondary concern but a primary design criterion, reinforcing the relevance of each of the targeted optimizations reviewed here.
