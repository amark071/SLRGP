# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated a corresponding body of survey literature dedicated to understanding and addressing the computational costs associated with their inference. Several comprehensive overviews have emerged that collectively map the landscape of efficient inference research, each approaching the problem from a somewhat distinct vantage point.

Among the most systematic treatments is the survey by Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294], which directly addresses the substantial computational and memory requirements that make LLM inference challenging in resource-constrained scenarios. The authors provide a structured taxonomy of techniques aimed at enhancing inference efficiency, covering the breadth of algorithmic and systems-level approaches that have been developed in response to these deployment pressures. Similarly, Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] survey model compression and efficient inference methods specifically from an algorithmic perspective, focusing on the significant memory and computational costs incurred during inference for Transformer-based models. Their work is particularly attentive to the challenge of deploying large models on devices with limited resources, and their taxonomic framing provides a useful organizational schema for comparing disparate methods.

A broader contextual treatment is offered by Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038], whose comprehensive overview spans the full lifecycle of LLMs from training to inference. Motivated in part by the widespread adoption of models such as ChatGPT, the authors situate efficient inference within the larger trend toward low-cost training and deployment, arguing that cost efficiency represents a defining direction for future LLM development. While this survey is less narrowly focused on inference optimization than those of Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] or Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748], its broader scope allows it to contextualize inference challenges within the evolution of training techniques and downstream task adaptation, offering readers a more integrated picture of the field.

Complementing these algorithmic and systems surveys, Donisch et al. [arxiv_2408.03130] adopt a practitioner-oriented perspective, reviewing optimization techniques with explicit attention to their effects, challenges, and practical considerations. Their work acknowledges that LLMs are particularly valuable precisely because they can adapt to new tasks without retraining, making inference efficiency a critical bottleneck in real-world deployment. By foregrounding practical tradeoffs alongside technical descriptions, this review serves as a useful bridge between theoretical proposals and applied implementation.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a consensus that the memory and computational demands of LLM inference constitute a central challenge for the field, while differing in their emphasis: Wang et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] and Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] prioritize algorithmic taxonomy, Liu et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] embed inference efficiency within a wider developmental narrative, and Donisch et al. [arxiv_2408.03130] foreground practical deployment considerations. This diversity of framing reflects the multidisciplinary nature of efficient inference research, which draws on algorithm design, hardware architecture, and systems engineering simultaneously.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The compression of large language models has emerged as a central research priority, driven by the substantial memory and computational demands that constrain their practical deployment. A broad consensus in the literature holds that LLMs contain significant redundancy, and that techniques such as weight pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation can exploit this redundancy to reduce memory footprints and accelerate inference [arxiv_2409.17372]. These approaches are not mutually exclusive; in practice, they are often combined to achieve compounding efficiency gains, as demonstrated in work targeting transformer-based models for production environments [arxiv_2211.07715].

Quantization has received particularly intensive attention, given its ability to reduce the numerical precision of model weights and activations without requiring architectural changes. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is widely regarded as an effective and practical method for accelerating LLM inference, since it does not necessitate retraining from scratch [arxiv_2408.08554]. However, PTQ is not without its challenges. Low-bit quantization schemes introduce accuracy degradation, and the deployment of such methods faces two major obstacles: maintaining model quality at aggressive bit-widths and ensuring that the resulting representations can be efficiently executed on real hardware [arxiv_2408.08554]. The ABQ-LLM framework directly addresses these concerns by enabling arbitrary-bit quantized inference acceleration, reflecting a broader trend toward more flexible and hardware-aware quantization strategies [arxiv_2408.08554].

Pruning, which removes redundant weights or structural components from a trained model, represents another well-established compression paradigm. Like quantization, pruning targets the inherent over-parameterization of large models, and it is frequently studied alongside quantization as a complementary technique [arxiv_2409.17372]. A notable limitation identified in the literature, however, is that most compression methods—including pruning—concentrate predominantly on weight optimization, potentially overlooking other sources of inefficiency within the model [arxiv_2409.17372]. This observation motivates more holistic approaches to efficient LLM design that move beyond weight-centric perspectives.

Knowledge distillation offers a third avenue for compression, wherein a smaller student model is trained to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher model. The Fast DistilBERT work illustrates how distillation can be combined with quantization and pruning to meet the stringent latency constraints imposed by industrial deployment, where maximum throughput is a critical requirement [arxiv_2211.07715]. This multi-technique approach underscores that no single compression method is universally sufficient for production-grade systems.

An important theoretical consideration is raised by Li et al. [arxiv_2002.11794], who argue that the conventional objective of maximizing accuracy under fixed compute budgets should be reconsidered. Their findings suggest that training larger models and subsequently compressing them can be more efficient than training smaller models directly, a strategy they term "train large, then compress." This insight reframes compression not merely as a post-hoc remediation of oversized models, but as an integral component of a deliberate training and deployment pipeline for transformer-based NLP systems [arxiv_2002.11794]. Taken together, these works illustrate that efficient inference for LLMs requires coordinated advances across pruning, quantization, and distillation, informed by a principled understanding of the trade-offs between model size, training cost, and inference efficiency.
