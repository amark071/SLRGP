# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The rapid convergence of visual and linguistic modalities within large-scale neural architectures has generated a substantial body of survey literature seeking to map the field's trajectory and consolidate its foundational concepts. An early and influential contribution to this lineage is the work on vision-language pre-training (VLP) by [arxiv_2210.09263], which organises pre-training methods into three broad categories: approaches targeting image-text tasks such as captioning, retrieval, and visual question answering; approaches oriented toward core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification; and video-language pre-training. This taxonomy provided a principled scaffold for understanding how multimodal representations were being learned prior to the emergence of instruction-following generalist models, and it established the conceptual vocabulary that later surveys would extend.

Building directly on this foundation, [arxiv_2309.10020] offers a more expansive treatment by tracing the evolution from specialist vision-language models to general-purpose multimodal assistants. The survey covers five core research topics and explicitly frames the transition as a paradigm shift, arguing that the integration of large language model backbones with visual encoders enabled a qualitatively new class of system capable of open-ended dialogue and cross-modal reasoning. Where [arxiv_2210.09263] concentrated on pre-training objectives and benchmark-specific capabilities, [arxiv_2309.10020] broadens the lens to encompass instruction tuning, alignment strategies, and the architectural decisions that underpin general-purpose assistants, reflecting the field's maturation between 2022 and 2023.

The survey by [arxiv_2402.12451] continues this trajectory into 2024, characterising the emergence of multimodal large language models as a "revolution" in generative intelligence. The authors emphasise the dialogue-based interface and instruction-following capabilities that distinguish contemporary MLLMs from their predecessors, and they situate these developments within the broader success of large language models as a driving force. This framing complements [arxiv_2309.10020] by placing greater rhetorical and analytical weight on the generative and interactive dimensions of modern systems, rather than on the specialist-to-generalist transition per se.

A notably distinct contribution is offered by [arxiv_2410.05608], which extends the scope of multimodal survey work beyond the dominant vision-language dyad to encompass audio, sensor data, and video alongside text and images. This breadth distinguishes it from the other three surveys, all of which concentrate primarily on visual and textual modalities. By foregrounding the full heterogeneity of data types that contemporary multimodal models must handle, [arxiv_2410.05608] highlights technical challenges—such as cross-modal alignment across non-visual sensory streams—that remain underexplored in vision-centric accounts. Taken together, these four surveys trace a coherent intellectual arc: from the systematic cataloguing of vision-language pre-training paradigms [arxiv_2210.09263], through the documentation of the specialist-to-generalist transition [arxiv_2309.10020], to assessments of instruction-following MLLMs as a transformative development [arxiv_2402.12451], and finally to calls for a more inclusive conception of multimodality that reaches beyond sight and language [arxiv_2410.05608].

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has emerged as a central research focus in artificial intelligence, driven by the goal of enabling systems to jointly process and reason over visual and textual inputs. A foundational contribution in this space is PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], which demonstrates that effective scaling of both vision and language components, combined with a flexible text-generation interface, allows a single model to address a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages. PaLI generates text conditioned on visual and textual inputs, and its jointly scaled architecture underscores the importance of co-training vision encoders and language models at scale rather than treating either modality as secondary.

Building on the premise that visual and linguistic representations must be tightly integrated, subsequent work has explored how to extend such architectures beyond the image-text paradigm. VALOR [ss_478472] proposes an omni-perception pretraining framework that jointly models vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. Unlike the predominantly vision-language pretraining models that preceded it, VALOR employs three separate unimodal encoders alongside a multimodal decoder, enabling richer cross-modal representations that incorporate auditory signals. This trimodal design reflects a broader recognition that real-world multimodal understanding cannot be fully captured by vision and language alone, and it positions VALOR as a meaningful architectural departure from dual-modality predecessors such as PaLI.

Despite the impressive capabilities demonstrated by large-scale MLLMs, a persistent challenge concerns the computational cost of training and adapting these models. Retraining large language model backbones to accommodate new visual inputs is expensive, and many existing approaches offer limited flexibility for task-specific fine-tuning [arxiv_2408.06610]. CROME addresses this by introducing cross-modal adapters that enable efficient multimodal adaptation without requiring full language model retraining. This adapter-based strategy represents a shift in architectural philosophy: rather than scaling up pretraining jointly across modalities, CROME prioritizes parameter-efficient adaptation, making MLLMs more accessible for downstream deployment while acknowledging that zero-shot performance improvements alone provide insufficient guidance for task-specific scenarios.

The trajectory from PaLI to CROME also reflects a broader trend in the field toward modularity and efficiency. Whereas PaLI emphasizes the benefits of joint scaling during pretraining, CROME suggests that well-designed adapter modules can bridge modalities post hoc, reducing the resource burden substantially. Complementing these architectural innovations, work such as Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] highlights the growing interest in multimodal representation learning that seamlessly integrates textual and visual information, noting that multimodal models—understood as extensions of LLMs—have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks including image captioning and visual question answering.

Taken together, these contributions illustrate the rapid evolution of MLLM architectures: from large-scale joint pretraining paradigms [arxiv_2209.06794] to trimodal omni-perception frameworks [ss_478472], and toward efficient cross-modal adaptation strategies [arxiv_2408.06610]. The field continues to grapple with the tension between model capacity and training efficiency, while progressively expanding the modalities and tasks that such systems can address [arxiv_2403.08773].
