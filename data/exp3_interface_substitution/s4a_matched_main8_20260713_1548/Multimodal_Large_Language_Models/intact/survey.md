# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The rapid convergence of large language models (LLMs) with multimodal perception has generated a substantial body of survey literature seeking to map the field's trajectory, methods, and open challenges. Collectively, these overviews trace a clear arc from early vision-language pre-training (VLP) pipelines toward increasingly general-purpose, dialogue-capable systems that operate across a wide range of modalities.

An important point of departure for understanding this evolution is the foundational work on vision-language pre-training. [arxiv_2210.09263] provides a systematic account of VLP methods developed in the years preceding the LLM era, organising approaches into those designed for image-text tasks — including image captioning, visual question answering, image-text retrieval, and visual grounding — and those targeting core computer vision tasks such as open-set image classification. This taxonomy highlights how early multimodal research was largely task-specific, with models pre-trained and fine-tuned for narrowly defined objectives rather than general-purpose interaction.

Building directly on this historical context, [arxiv_2309.10020] charts the subsequent transition from specialist models to general-purpose multimodal assistants. The survey presents a comprehensive taxonomy of multimodal foundation models demonstrating vision and vision-language capabilities, covering five core research topics divided into well-established areas and emerging directions. Crucially, the work frames this shift as a fundamental change in design philosophy: whereas earlier systems were optimised for individual benchmarks, newer foundation models aim to serve as versatile assistants capable of handling diverse instructions across tasks. This framing complements [arxiv_2210.09263] by situating VLP as a necessary precursor to, rather than a substitute for, the more flexible architectures that followed.

The emergence of models explicitly designed around LLM backbones is addressed in depth by [arxiv_2402.12451], which surveys the class of systems now commonly referred to as Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs). The survey emphasises that connecting visual and textual modalities within a dialogue-based, instruction-following interface represents a qualitative leap beyond earlier VLP approaches, enabling richer and more open-ended human-computer interaction. Where [arxiv_2309.10020] focuses on the structural evolution of foundation models, [arxiv_2402.12451] foregrounds the generative and conversational dimensions that distinguish MLLMs as a distinct research paradigm, underscoring the role of instruction tuning and dialogue interfaces as key enablers of this shift.

Extending the scope further, [arxiv_2410.05608] broadens the survey lens beyond vision and language to encompass audio, video, sensor data, and other modalities. This tutorial-oriented overview situates multimodal LLMs within a wider landscape of pretrained models capable of integrating diverse data forms, addressing foundational concepts, the historical evolution of multimodal research, and key technical challenges. Compared to [arxiv_2402.12451], which concentrates on visual-textual integration, [arxiv_2410.05608] emphasises the generality of the multimodal paradigm and the challenges that arise when scaling to heterogeneous input streams beyond the image-text pairing that has dominated the field.

Taken together, these surveys delineate a coherent progression: from task-specific VLP methods [arxiv_2210.09263], through the consolidation of vision-language capabilities into foundation models [arxiv_2309.10020], to the rise of dialogue-capable MLLMs [arxiv_2402.12451], and ultimately toward systems that aspire to process the full breadth of human-perceivable modalities [arxiv_2410.05608].

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has accelerated considerably in recent years, driven by advances in joint vision-language pretraining, scalable architectures, and efficient cross-modal adaptation strategies. A central challenge in this space is designing model architectures capable of processing both visual and textual inputs within a unified framework, while maintaining the representational power necessary for diverse downstream tasks.

One of the most prominent contributions to this area is PaLI, a jointly-scaled multilingual language-image model that extends the scaling principles of large language models to encompass vision [arxiv_2209.06794]. PaLI generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, enabling a single model to address a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages. A key architectural insight in PaLI is the use of a flexible task interface that allows the model to be applied uniformly across tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and optical character recognition. The authors demonstrate that effective scaling of both the language and vision components is essential to achieving strong performance, underscoring the importance of co-designing the two modalities rather than treating vision as a secondary add-on to a pretrained language backbone [arxiv_2209.06794].

Building on the broader trend of integrating visual and linguistic representations, subsequent work has explored how multimodal models can be made more accessible and adaptable. Chawla et al. [arxiv_2403.08773] situate MLLMs as natural extensions of large language models, emphasizing their capacity to address a diverse array of tasks ranging from image captioning to complex visual reasoning. Their work on Veagle highlights the growing interest in multimodal representation learning as a means of seamlessly integrating textual and visual information, reflecting a community-wide recognition that unimodal pretraining alone is insufficient for tasks requiring joint understanding [arxiv_2403.08773].

A complementary line of research addresses the practical limitations of full-scale multimodal pretraining by focusing on efficient cross-modal adaptation. Ebrahimi et al. [arxiv_2408.06610] propose CROME, a framework employing cross-modal adapters that enable effective image-language alignment without requiring expensive retraining of the underlying language model. This approach is particularly significant because it decouples the adaptation of visual representations from the core language model parameters, reducing computational costs while preserving strong zero-shot and task-specific performance [arxiv_2408.06610]. Compared to architectures like PaLI that require joint scaling from pretraining, CROME represents a more parameter-efficient paradigm suited to scenarios where full retraining is infeasible.

Beyond the dominant vision-language paradigm, some researchers have argued for extending multimodal pretraining to additional sensory modalities. Liu et al. [ss_478472] propose VALOR, an omni-perception pretraining model that jointly models vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations alongside a unified multimodal decoder, enabling both understanding and generation tasks [ss_478472]. This architecture contrasts with dual-encoder vision-language models by explicitly incorporating audio as a first-class modality, suggesting that richer multimodal pretraining objectives may yield more generalizable representations than those trained on vision and language alone.
