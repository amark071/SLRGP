# Multimodal Large Language Models

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, and a growing body of survey literature has sought to map this rapidly evolving landscape. An early and foundational contribution to this area is the work on vision-language pre-training (VLP) by [arxiv_2210.09263], which surveys methods developed to align visual and textual representations across a range of tasks including image captioning, image-text retrieval, visual question answering, and visual grounding, as well as open-set image classification and core computer vision tasks. This work provides a taxonomy of VLP approaches that laid important groundwork for understanding how multimodal alignment can be achieved at scale, grouping methods according to the nature of the tasks they address and the architectural choices they employ.

Building directly on this foundation, [arxiv_2309.10020] offers a more expansive treatment of multimodal foundation models, tracing their evolution from narrow specialist systems toward general-purpose assistants capable of handling a broad range of vision and vision-language tasks. The survey is organized around five core research topics and explicitly frames the trajectory of the field as a transition from task-specific pipelines to unified, instruction-following models. This framing is particularly valuable for contextualizing the architectural and training innovations that have enabled more flexible multimodal reasoning, and it situates the emergence of general-purpose assistants within a longer history of multimodal research.

More recent survey contributions have extended this perspective to account for the latest generation of MLLMs. [arxiv_2402.12451] characterizes the current moment as a "revolution" in multimodal generative intelligence, emphasizing the capacity of contemporary models to seamlessly integrate visual and textual modalities within a dialogue-based, instruction-following interface. This framing reflects a qualitative shift from the earlier VLP paradigm surveyed by [arxiv_2210.09263], in which models were largely evaluated on fixed benchmarks, toward systems that can engage in open-ended, conversational interactions grounded in visual input. The survey by [arxiv_2402.12451] thus complements [arxiv_2309.10020] by focusing more specifically on the generative and conversational capabilities that define the most recent wave of MLLM development.

A further dimension of the field is captured by [arxiv_2410.05608], which broadens the scope of multimodal modeling beyond the vision-language dyad to encompass audio, video, and sensor data. This tutorial-oriented overview highlights the foundational concepts of multimodality and traces the evolution of multimodal research across a wider range of data modalities, addressing key technical challenges such as cross-modal alignment and the integration of heterogeneous input streams. In doing so, it situates vision-language models within a more general framework of multimodal learning, suggesting that the principles underlying MLLMs are extensible to a diverse array of sensory modalities. Taken together, these four contributions collectively document the field's progression from early VLP methods [arxiv_2210.09263] through the development of general-purpose multimodal assistants [arxiv_2309.10020, arxiv_2402.12451] and toward a broader, modality-agnostic conception of multimodal intelligence [arxiv_2410.05608].

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has emerged as a central research frontier, driven by the ambition to integrate visual and linguistic understanding within unified architectures. A foundational contribution in this space is PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], which demonstrates that effective scaling and a flexible task interface can extend the successes of large language models to joint vision-language modeling. PaLI generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, enabling it to perform a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages. The model's design philosophy—scaling both the language and vision components jointly—established an influential template for subsequent multimodal architectures, underscoring that the synergy between modality-specific capacity and shared representation is critical to downstream performance.

Building on the general trajectory of multimodal integration, more recent work has explored how to make such models more efficient and adaptable. CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] addresses a practical limitation of many MLLMs: the requirement for expensive language model retraining when adapting to new visual inputs or tasks. By introducing cross-modal adapters, CROME enables cost-effective training and adaptation without wholesale retraining of the underlying language model. The authors also note that the prevailing focus on zero-shot performance in the field provides insufficient guidance for task-specific tuning, suggesting that architectural choices must be evaluated not only on general benchmarks but also on their capacity for targeted adaptation. Compared to the large-scale joint pretraining paradigm exemplified by PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] represents a shift toward modular, parameter-efficient design, reflecting growing concerns about the computational costs associated with training and deploying MLLMs at scale.

The scope of multimodal pretraining has also expanded beyond the vision-language dyad. VALOR [ss_478472] proposes an omni-perception pretraining framework that jointly models relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. Unlike the predominantly vision-language-focused architectures such as PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations alongside a unified decoder for multimodal content generation, thereby accommodating richer cross-modal interactions. This trimodal approach highlights a broader trend in the field toward models capable of perceiving and reasoning across a more comprehensive set of sensory inputs, moving beyond the binary of image and text.

Complementing these architectural innovations, Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] situates multimodal models as natural extensions of LLMs, emphasizing their remarkable capabilities across diverse tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering. The work reflects the broader research community's interest in seamlessly integrating textual and visual information, reinforcing the view that multimodal representation learning is not merely an auxiliary capability but a core dimension of next-generation language models. Taken together, these contributions illustrate a field progressing along two complementary axes: scaling and broadening the modalities addressed during pretraining [arxiv_2209.06794, ss_478472], and improving the efficiency and task-specific adaptability of the resulting models [arxiv_2408.06610, arxiv_2403.08773].
