# 3D Gaussian Splatting

### Injected invalid unbalanced organization

The field of novel view synthesis has undergone a profound transformation over the past several years, driven first by the emergence of Neural Radiance Fields and subsequently by the development of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). Understanding the trajectory of this transformation requires situating 3DGS within the broader landscape of radiance field research, examining its foundational mechanisms, and critically assessing both its advantages and the limitations that have motivated subsequent work.

Neural Radiance Fields, introduced in 2020, represented a landmark advance in implicit scene representation, encoding geometry and appearance through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks that are queried via volumetric ray marching [arxiv_2210.00379]. While NeRF-based methods achieved remarkable photorealistic quality and found applications spanning robotics, urban mapping, autonomous navigation, and augmented reality [arxiv_2210.00379], they carried a significant computational burden. Training times were lengthy, and rendering speeds were far from suitable for real-time applications, creating a persistent tension between visual fidelity and practical deployability [arxiv_2401.00834]. Various subsequent NeRF variants attempted to address these bottlenecks, yet as noted by Kerbl et al., no existing method prior to 3DGS could achieve real-time rendering at 1080p resolution for unbounded and complete scenes without sacrificing quality [arxiv_2308.04079].

The introduction of 3D Gaussian Splatting by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] marked a decisive departure from neural implicit representations. Rather than encoding a scene within the weights of a neural network queried through volumetric rendering, 3DGS represents scenes as collections of explicit, optimizable 3D Gaussian ellipsoids. These Gaussians are initialized from sparse point clouds, typically derived from Structure-from-Motion, and are subsequently refined through differentiable rasterization. The result is a method capable of real-time rendering at high resolutions with training times measured in tens of minutes rather than hours or days, while maintaining competitive or superior visual quality relative to leading NeRF-based approaches [arxiv_2308.04079]. This combination of speed and quality rapidly established 3DGS as a central reference method in the field [arxiv_2502.19318].

Despite this success, the theoretical foundations of 3DGS's rendering pipeline have attracted scrutiny. Celarek et al. [arxiv_2502.19318] observe that while 3DGS superficially resembles NeRF in its use of volumetric representations, it departs substantially from principled volumetric rendering as employed in ray-marching approaches. The approximations inherent in the splatting rasterization pipeline raise questions about whether the method's empirical success is contingent on accurate volumetric rendering or whether it arises despite these approximations. This is a non-trivial theoretical question, as the answer has implications for how future improvements to the rendering pipeline should be designed and evaluated [arxiv_2502.19318].

A further and practically significant limitation of 3DGS concerns its memory consumption. Because each Gaussian is associated with a set of per-primitive parameters — including position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients encoding view-dependent appearance — scenes represented by large numbers of Gaussians impose substantial memory overhead [arxiv_2312.04564]. This overhead affects both training and inference, limiting the deployment of 3DGS in resource-constrained environments. Girish et al. [arxiv_2312.04564] address this through the EAGLES framework, which introduces lightweight encodings and quantization strategies to reduce memory requirements while preserving rendering quality. Similarly, Sun et al. [arxiv_2405.17083] propose F-3DGS, which factorizes the coordinates and representations of Gaussians to achieve more compact scene descriptions, explicitly motivated by the observation that the high computational costs of NeRF-like methods and the memory demands of standard 3DGS both impede deployment in real-time and resource-limited settings [arxiv_2405.17083].

Wang and Xu [arxiv_2509.17083] approach the memory problem from a hybrid perspective, combining explicit 3D Gaussians with complementary neural representations to achieve memory-efficient novel view synthesis without sacrificing quality. Their work highlights that the per-Gaussian parameterization of view-dependent effects and anisotropic shapes, while expressive, is a primary driver of the memory overhead that limits 3DGS scalability [arxiv_2509.17083]. This stands in contrast to the compression-oriented approaches of EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] and F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], which seek to retain the explicit Gaussian representation while reducing its storage footprint through encoding and factorization, respectively.

Beyond memory and rendering fidelity, 3DGS has also been extended to handle degraded input data. Lee et al. [arxiv_2401.00834] demonstrate that the Gaussian splatting framework can be adapted for deblurring, addressing the common scenario in which training images are affected by motion blur. This extension illustrates the flexibility of the explicit Gaussian representation as a substrate for incorporating domain-specific priors, a flexibility that contrasts with the more opaque parameterization of neural implicit methods.

The breadth of these developments is surveyed comprehensively by Wu et al. [arxiv_2403.11134], who document how 3DGS has accelerated not only rendering speed but also the pace of research across multiple subfields of novel view synthesis. Their survey situates 3DGS as a paradigm shift relative to NeRF, emphasizing that the explicit, ellipsoid-based scene model enables efficient rendering through rasterization rather than volumetric integration, while remaining fully differentiable and thus amenable to gradient-based optimization [arxiv_2403.11134]. Taken together, the body of work reviewed here reveals a field in which the foundational contribution of Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] has catalyzed a rich program of follow-on research addressing memory efficiency, rendering correctness, input robustness, and representational compactness, each thread responding to distinct limitations of the original formulation while preserving its core computational advantages.
