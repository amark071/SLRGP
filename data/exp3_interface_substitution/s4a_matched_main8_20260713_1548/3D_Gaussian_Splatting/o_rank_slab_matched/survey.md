# 3D Gaussian Splatting

### Rank slab 1: positions 1-4

The foundational contribution to 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) was introduced by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079], who proposed representing scenes as collections of 3D Gaussian primitives that can be rendered in real time at 1080p resolution without sacrificing visual quality. Prior to this work, radiance field methods had achieved impressive photorealistic novel-view synthesis, yet they relied heavily on neural networks and volumetric rendering pipelines that imposed substantial computational costs during both training and inference. Faster alternatives existed but consistently traded rendering quality for speed. The key insight of Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] was to replace the implicit, network-driven scene representation with explicit, optimizable Gaussian ellipsoids, enabling efficient rasterization-based rendering that bypasses the expensive ray-marching procedures characteristic of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). This shift from implicit to explicit scene representation marked a significant inflection point in the field, making high-fidelity novel-view synthesis practically deployable in real-time settings for unbounded and complete scenes.

The broader significance and rapid uptake of this paradigm are documented in the survey by Wu et al. [arxiv_2403.11134], who characterize 3DGS as having "greatly accelerated the rendering speed of novel view synthesis." Their survey draws an explicit contrast between 3DGS and NeRF-based implicit representations: whereas NeRF encodes scene geometry and appearance through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks, 3DGS models the scene with a set of Gaussian ellipsoids whose parameters are directly optimized, enabling efficient rendering through explicit geometric primitives. This representational distinction is not merely technical but has practical consequences for the breadth of downstream applications that the method can support [arxiv_2403.11134].

Despite its advantages, the original 3DGS formulation is not without limitations, and subsequent work has addressed several of its shortcomings. Lee et al. [arxiv_2401.00834] observe that while 3DGS-based approaches successfully overcome the training and rendering bottlenecks associated with neural volumetric methods, practical deployment is complicated by real-world image degradation such as motion blur, which the base model does not account for. Their work on deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting extends the framework to handle blurry input images, demonstrating that the Gaussian splatting paradigm is amenable to targeted improvements that address specific capture-side artifacts [arxiv_2401.00834].

A different but equally pressing limitation concerns memory efficiency. Wang and Xu [arxiv_2509.17083] note that 3DGS incurs significant memory overhead because each Gaussian primitive must store per-Gaussian parameters to model view-dependent appearance effects and anisotropic shapes. This per-primitive parameterization, while central to the method's expressive power, scales poorly with scene complexity. Their HyRF framework proposes a hybrid radiance field approach that combines the strengths of explicit Gaussian representations with complementary strategies to reduce memory consumption while preserving rendering quality [arxiv_2509.17083]. Taken together, the works of Lee et al. [arxiv_2401.00834] and Wang and Xu [arxiv_2509.17083] illustrate a common trajectory in the literature: accepting the core representational innovation of Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079] while systematically addressing the practical constraints—whether perceptual quality under degraded inputs or computational resource demands—that limit its applicability at scale.

### Rank slab 2: positions 5-8

Research into 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has expanded rapidly across several complementary directions, addressing fundamental theoretical questions about the method's rendering formulation as well as practical challenges arising from degraded or sparse input data and the demands of high-quality surface reconstruction.

At the theoretical level, Celarek et al. [arxiv_2502.19318] interrogate a foundational assumption underlying 3DGS: whether its approximate splatting-based rasterization actually requires the kind of principled volumetric rendering accuracy that characterizes Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs). NeRFs employ a ray-marching approach grounded in volumetric rendering theory, whereas 3DGS shares a similar representational spirit but departs from strict physical accuracy in its projection and compositing steps. By systematically examining this discrepancy, the authors raise important questions about whether the empirical success of 3DGS is attributable to rendering correctness or to other factors such as the flexibility of the Gaussian primitives themselves. This theoretical inquiry is significant because it informs how future improvements to the pipeline should be prioritized—whether efforts should focus on closing the gap with physically accurate volumetric rendering or on other aspects of the representation.

Turning to practical robustness, two lines of work address scenarios in which the standard assumption of clean, densely sampled imagery breaks down. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2403.11831] tackle the problem of motion blur, which is common in real-world capture conditions such as low-light or long-exposure photography. Their BAD-Gaussians framework integrates bundle-adjusted deblurring directly into the Gaussian splatting training process, recognizing that both blurry images and inaccurate camera poses—which often co-occur—can severely degrade reconstruction quality. By jointly optimizing for sharp latent images and refined camera parameters within the 3DGS framework, the method extends the applicability of Gaussian splatting to a broader range of capture conditions that would otherwise produce unsatisfactory results. Peng et al. [arxiv_2411.03637] address a related but distinct challenge: sparse input views. Their Structure Consistent Gaussian Splatting method incorporates matching priors to enforce structural consistency across the limited available viewpoints, targeting the significant quality degradation that both NeRF- and 3DGS-based methods exhibit when training data is scarce. Notably, they highlight that the problem is particularly acute in large scenes, where sparse coverage leaves substantial regions under-constrained. Together, these two works [arxiv_2403.11831, arxiv_2411.03637] illustrate that robustness to imperfect inputs—whether degraded image quality or insufficient view coverage—represents a critical frontier for making 3DGS practically deployable.

A further challenge lies in extracting geometrically accurate surfaces from Gaussian representations. Song et al. [arxiv_2411.01853] observe that while 3DGS employs an explicit, discrete representation that enables fast training and rendering, this same discreteness complicates surface reconstruction, as the resulting geometry is effectively assembled from numerous individual Gaussian primitives rather than a continuous field. Their GVKF method introduces Gaussian voxel kernel functions to enable efficient and effective surface reconstruction in open scenes, addressing a limitation that NeRF-based methods handle differently through their implicit continuous representations, albeit at much greater computational cost. This work thus highlights a fundamental trade-off in the 3DGS paradigm: the explicit representation that grants speed and real-time capability simultaneously introduces challenges for downstream geometric tasks that implicit methods handle more naturally.
