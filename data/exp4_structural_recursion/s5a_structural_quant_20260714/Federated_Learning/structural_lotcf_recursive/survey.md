## Introduction

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling models to be trained across a multitude of decentralized devices or institutions without requiring raw data to be centralized. By keeping data local and exchanging only model updates, federated learning addresses fundamental tensions between the utility of large-scale machine learning and the privacy, regulatory, and logistical constraints that make centralized data collection impractical or undesirable. Since its early formulations, the field has grown rapidly, spawning a rich and interconnected body of literature that spans algorithmic theory, systems engineering, privacy guarantees, and real-world deployment.

This literature review surveys the federated learning landscape through a foundational-to-applied lens, tracing the intellectual lineage from core optimization frameworks and algorithmic principles through to the practical challenges and solutions that define contemporary research. The review is organized recursively, beginning with the mathematical and algorithmic foundations that underpin federated optimization, before expanding outward to examine architectural variants, empirical comparisons, and the persistent challenge of data heterogeneity—commonly referred to as the non-IID problem—which complicates convergence and model fairness in realistic deployments.

A substantial portion of this review is devoted to privacy, reflecting the centrality of privacy preservation to federated learning's original motivation. We examine differential privacy in its multiple instantiations within federated systems: from high-level surveys and taxonomies that map the design space, to central and aggregation-level mechanisms, to local differential privacy applied at the client side, and finally to the intersection of differential privacy with communication efficiency and secure aggregation. Complementing this, we survey cryptographic and protocol-level approaches to secure aggregation, which provide formal guarantees against inference from intermediate model updates, as well as decentralized architectures that eliminate the trusted central server entirely through peer-to-peer aggregation protocols.

Communication efficiency receives dedicated attention, as the bandwidth constraints of federated environments make gradient compression, quantization, coding-theoretic approaches, and straggler mitigation essential practical concerns rather than mere optimizations. The review concludes its thematic arc with broad surveys, taxonomies, and systematic reviews of federated learning as a whole, which provide integrative perspectives that contextualize the more specialized contributions examined throughout.

Together, these eleven thematic clusters form a coherent map of a field that is simultaneously theoretically deep and practically urgent. By traversing this map from foundational algorithms to applied systems, from centralized privacy mechanisms to fully decentralized protocols, this review aims to equip readers with both the conceptual vocabulary and the structural understanding needed to engage critically with federated learning research and to identify the open problems that will shape its future trajectory.

##### Core Optimization Frameworks and Algorithmic Foundations of Federated Learning

The theoretical and algorithmic foundations of federated learning rest on a recognition that the distributed, privacy-sensitive nature of federated settings introduces optimization challenges that differ fundamentally from those encountered in conventional centralized or even standard distributed machine learning. Understanding these foundations requires moving from basic problem formulations through to the nuanced performance limitations that emerge when algorithms are deployed under realistic conditions.

At the most foundational level, federated learning can be understood as a specific instantiation of distributed optimization in which the objective is to collaboratively learn a shared model from data that is partitioned across many edge devices or clients, without requiring that data to be centralized. What distinguishes federated optimization from classical distributed optimization is not merely the scale of the system but the constellation of constraints that must be simultaneously respected: communication efficiency, statistical heterogeneity across clients, compatibility with privacy mechanisms, and the practical realities of system heterogeneity such as varying computational capacity and intermittent availability [arxiv_2107.06917]. These constraints are not peripheral engineering concerns but are central to the problem formulation itself, and any algorithm designed for federated settings must be evaluated against all of them rather than optimizing for a single criterion in isolation.

Building on this foundational framing, the work of [arxiv_2107.06917] provides a systematic set of recommendations and guidelines for formulating, designing, evaluating, and analyzing federated optimization algorithms. Rather than cataloguing existing methods, this contribution is oriented toward enabling researchers and practitioners to reason carefully about what constitutes a meaningful simulation of federated conditions and how to translate simulation results into reliable inferences about real-world performance. The emphasis on concrete examples and practical implementation reflects a recognition that the gap between theoretical algorithm design and empirical behavior in federated systems is often substantial, and that closing this gap requires deliberate methodological choices at the level of problem formulation itself. This makes [arxiv_2107.06917] a particularly important reference for understanding not just what federated optimization algorithms exist, but how to think rigorously about their design and assessment.

Moving from the design of algorithms to the analysis of their limitations, [arxiv_2501.03477] examines the performance constraints that arise in federated learning systems, with particular attention to two interrelated challenges: the communication bottleneck and data non-IID-ness. These two issues are deeply connected at a theoretical level. When client data distributions diverge significantly from one another, local gradient updates computed on individual clients become increasingly poor proxies for the global gradient, and the aggregation of such updates can lead to model drift and degraded convergence. This phenomenon, sometimes referred to as client drift, is exacerbated in communication-constrained settings where clients perform many local update steps between rounds of aggregation, amplifying the divergence between local and global optima [arxiv_2501.03477]. The study situates these challenges within the broader historical development of federated learning since its introduction by Google in 2016, noting that while the field has expanded to address problems including model compression, differential privacy, robustness, and federated generative models, the foundational optimization challenges associated with heterogeneous data and limited communication remain central open problems.

Taken together, these two contributions illuminate a productive tension in the theoretical study of federated optimization. On one side, [arxiv_2107.06917] argues for principled, constraint-aware algorithm design and rigorous evaluation methodology as the path toward algorithms that perform reliably in practice. On the other, [arxiv_2501.03477] demonstrates that even well-designed algorithms face inherent performance ceilings imposed by the statistical and communication structure of federated environments. The implication is that progress in federated optimization theory requires not only the development of new algorithms but also a clearer analytical understanding of the fundamental limits imposed by data heterogeneity and communication constraints, so that algorithm designers can reason about how close any given method comes to those limits and under what conditions the gap can be reduced.

##### Architectural Variants, System Comparisons, and Empirical Assessments of Federated Learning

Federated learning has evolved considerably beyond its original formulation, giving rise to a range of architectural variants, hybrid designs, and systematic comparative evaluations that together illuminate both the capabilities and the constraints of distributed training paradigms. Understanding these developments requires moving from broad system-level comparisons toward increasingly specific architectural innovations and empirical characterizations.

At the most general level, comparative assessments between federated and centralized machine learning establish the foundational performance baseline against which all architectural variants must ultimately be judged. The non-IID nature of data distributed across participating clients introduces characteristic divergences from centralized training dynamics, affecting convergence behavior, model accuracy, and the reliability of gradient aggregation [arxiv_2202.01529]. These differences are not merely incidental but are structurally embedded in the federated setup, where locally trained model updates reflect heterogeneous data distributions that the global aggregation step must reconcile. Such comparative work makes clear that federated learning does not simply replicate centralized learning under a privacy constraint; it constitutes a qualitatively different optimization regime with its own failure modes and design requirements [arxiv_2202.01529]. This general framing motivates the search for architectural solutions that can mitigate the performance penalties introduced by data heterogeneity and communication constraints.

One prominent architectural response to these challenges is split learning, which partitions the model architecture between clients and a central server rather than replicating the full model on each client. This design offers meaningful advantages in resource-constrained environments, since clients need only maintain and compute over a portion of the network, and it provides stronger model privacy guarantees by ensuring that neither party holds the complete model [arxiv_2004.12088]. However, split learning's relay-based sequential training across clients introduces substantial latency, making it considerably slower than standard federated learning when the number of participating clients is large [arxiv_2004.12088]. This trade-off between privacy and computational efficiency represents a genuine architectural tension that neither pure federated learning nor pure split learning resolves satisfactorily on its own.

The hybrid SplitFed framework directly addresses this tension by amalgamating the two paradigms, preserving the model-partitioning benefits of split learning while recovering the parallelism that makes federated learning faster in practice [arxiv_2004.12088]. Empirical results reported for SplitFed demonstrate that the combined approach achieves training speeds comparable to federated learning while retaining the enhanced privacy properties associated with architectural splitting. The framework is further refined through the incorporation of differential privacy and PixelDP mechanisms, targeting both data privacy and model robustness simultaneously [arxiv_2004.12088]. This layered design illustrates a broader methodological trend in which system-level architectural choices and privacy-enhancing mechanisms are co-designed rather than applied independently.

Complementing these architectural studies, systematic reviews of machine learning models deployed within federated settings provide a wider empirical picture of how different model families perform under federated constraints. Drawing on PRISMA guidelines to structure a comprehensive survey of recent privacy-preserving machine learning literature, such reviews examine supervised and unsupervised approaches across diverse application domains, cataloguing performance outcomes and identifying recurring limitations [arxiv_2311.10832]. This kind of systematic synthesis is methodologically distinct from the empirical benchmarking found in architectural papers, but it serves an important function by aggregating evidence across studies and revealing patterns that no single experimental comparison could establish alone.

Taken together, these three bodies of work trace a coherent methodological arc. Comparative assessments against centralized learning establish what federated systems must overcome [arxiv_2202.01529]; architectural innovations such as SplitFed demonstrate concrete engineering strategies for doing so [arxiv_2004.12088]; and systematic model-level reviews situate these strategies within the broader empirical landscape of federated machine learning research [arxiv_2311.10832]. The progression from general system comparison to specific hybrid architecture to cross-study synthesis reflects the field's maturation from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward principled, evidence-based system design.

#### Data Heterogeneity (Non-IID) Challenges and Robustness in Federated Learning

The challenge of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data across participating clients represents one of the most extensively studied and consequential problems in federated learning research. There is broad consensus in the literature that data heterogeneity degrades model performance relative to centralized training baselines, and this shared recognition forms the foundation upon which more specific and sometimes competing solutions have been proposed.

The survey by Ye et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] provides the most comprehensive treatment of this foundational problem, offering a systematic analysis of how non-IID data distributions affect both parametric and non-parametric machine learning models across horizontal and vertical federated learning settings. The authors document in detail the mechanisms through which statistical heterogeneity undermines the convergence and generalization of globally aggregated models, establishing a taxonomy of non-IID types and reviewing the relative merits and limitations of existing mitigation strategies. This breadth of coverage positions the survey as an authoritative reference point against which more targeted interventions can be evaluated. Crucially, the authors acknowledge that no single approach has yet resolved the non-IID challenge comprehensively, and they identify several open research directions, signaling that the field remains in active development rather than settled consensus.

Building on this recognized gap, Yue et al. [arxiv_2104.06557] approach the non-IID problem from a causal inference perspective, arguing that conventional federated learning methods fail to achieve robust out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization precisely because they learn spurious correlations that are artifacts of heterogeneous local data distributions rather than stable causal mechanisms. Their proposed causal federated learning framework seeks to disentangle invariant causal features from environment-specific confounders, thereby improving robustness when the global model is deployed on data distributions not encountered during training. Importantly, this work also situates the non-IID challenge within a broader security context, noting that data heterogeneity compounds vulnerability to adversarial attacks, including backdoor insertion and model degradation by malicious clients. This dual framing—non-IID as both a statistical and a security concern—represents a conceptual extension beyond the more purely performance-oriented analysis offered in [arxiv_2106.06843], and it introduces a degree of controversy insofar as causal approaches require additional assumptions about the data-generating process that may not always be verifiable in practice.

A different line of inquiry is pursued by the work on chaotic maps-based privacy-preserving federated learning [arxiv_2402.10145], which addresses non-IID data not through representational or causal reformulation but through cryptographic and privacy-enhancing mechanisms. This study proposes a secured federated learning framework that incorporates an additional privacy layer and directly evaluates model performance under both IID and non-IID conditions. A notable contribution is the empirical comparison between differential privacy and chaotic-map-based encryption as privacy mechanisms, finding that the federated learning process improves average performance metrics even when data are non-IID. This finding is broadly consistent with the optimistic conclusions of [arxiv_2106.06843] regarding the viability of federated learning under heterogeneous conditions, yet it diverges in emphasis: whereas the survey foregrounds the performance costs of non-IID distributions, [arxiv_2402.10145] highlights the resilience of appropriately secured federated systems. The comparison between differential privacy and chaotic encryption also introduces a point of methodological controversy, as differential privacy is a well-established formal framework with known privacy-utility trade-offs, while chaotic-map-based encryption is a less standardized approach whose theoretical guarantees remain subject to ongoing scrutiny in the cryptographic community.

Taken together, these works reflect a trajectory from broad consensus—that non-IID data is a fundamental obstacle in federated learning—toward increasingly specialized and sometimes competing proposals for addressing it, spanning statistical, causal, and cryptographic dimensions. The diversity of proposed solutions underscores that data heterogeneity is not a single, well-defined problem but a multifaceted challenge whose resolution likely requires integrating insights from across these complementary research directions.

#### Surveys, Frameworks, and Taxonomies of Differentially Private Federated Learning

The intersection of federated learning (FL) and differential privacy (DP) has matured sufficiently to warrant dedicated surveys, taxonomies, and software frameworks that collectively map the landscape and guide practitioners. These foundational works range from early tool-oriented contributions to comprehensive systematic reviews, and together they establish the conceptual scaffolding upon which more specialized research is built.

An early and practically oriented contribution to this space is the Sherpa.ai Federated Learning framework [arxiv_2007.00914], which addresses a gap that was particularly acute in 2020: while several software tools had emerged to support FL and DP individually, they lacked a unified vision and a coherent methodological workflow for combining the two. The authors analyze existing software tools, identify their limitations, and present the Sherpa.ai framework as an integrated solution built upon both paradigms. Crucially, they also propose methodological guidelines intended to support practitioners in preserving data privacy throughout the federated learning pipeline. This work is notable for its dual contribution—both a concrete software artifact and a normative methodology—making it one of the earliest attempts to operationalize the FL-DP combination in a principled, reproducible manner.

As the field expanded, the need for holistic conceptual reviews became equally pressing. The survey by [arxiv_2404.18814], titled "Belt and Braces: When Federated Learning Meets Differential Privacy," responds to this need by providing a comprehensive review of current developments in DP-FL, organized around the observation that practitioners frequently lack full awareness of the field's development and categorization, and face difficult trade-offs between privacy and utility. The authors begin by introducing the primary concepts of both FL and DP, highlighting the theoretical compatibility of the two paradigms before surveying the state of the art. Their framing is explicitly practitioner-facing: the review is motivated not only by academic synthesis but by the recognition that achieving practically usable FL systems with DP guarantees remains genuinely challenging. This orientation distinguishes [arxiv_2404.18814] from more purely taxonomic efforts, as it foregrounds usability and the challenges and opportunities that arise when moving from theoretical guarantees to deployed systems.

The most taxonomically rigorous contribution in this cluster is the systematic review by [arxiv_2405.08299], which directly addresses a deficiency that the authors identify in prior literature: existing taxonomies have not adequately considered the objects and levels of privacy protection provided by various DP models within federated learning. In response, the authors propose a new taxonomy grounded in the definitions and guarantees of differential privacy as applied across different components and threat models in FL. This work is explicitly positioned as a systematic overview rather than a narrative review, suggesting a more structured methodology for literature selection and synthesis. Compared to [arxiv_2404.18814], which emphasizes practical usability and challenges, [arxiv_2405.08299] places greater weight on theoretical precision and classificatory completeness, making the two surveys complementary rather than redundant.

Taken together, these three works trace an instructive trajectory in the DP-FL literature. [arxiv_2007.00914] represents the field's early need for tooling and workflow guidance; [arxiv_2404.18814] reflects a mid-maturity phase in which holistic conceptual reviews become necessary to orient practitioners amid a proliferating literature; and [arxiv_2405.08299] signals a more advanced stage in which the field is sufficiently developed to demand rigorous, systematic taxonomization. All three works share the premise that DP has become the de facto standard for privacy protection in FL [arxiv_2405.08299, arxiv_2404.18814], and all three identify the gap between theoretical guarantees and practical deployment as a central concern. As foundational references, they provide the definitional vocabulary, categorical structure, and software context that subsequent algorithmic and application-specific research in differentially private federated learning presupposes.

##### Central and Aggregation-Level Differential Privacy Mechanisms

At the aggregation and server level of federated learning pipelines, differential privacy (DP) mechanisms must reconcile the fundamental tension between protecting client information and preserving the utility of the globally trained model. The papers reviewed in this subsection address this challenge through progressively more sophisticated approaches, moving from foundational concentrated DP formulations through communication-aware optimization frameworks to correlated quantization schemes that integrate privacy and compression simultaneously.

The earliest and most foundational contribution in this set is the work on concentrated differentially private federated learning [arxiv_2003.13761], which directly targets the scenario in which the central server cannot be fully trusted and information exchanged during each communication round constitutes a potential privacy leak. The authors observe that prior privacy-preserving mechanisms, while technically applicable to federated learning, impose substantial costs on model convergence and final utility. Their response is to adopt a concentrated differential privacy (CDP) framework, which offers tighter composition guarantees than standard DP and thereby allows the privacy budget to be spent more efficiently across many communication rounds. By applying CDP-compliant noise at the aggregation stage, the approach seeks to decouple the privacy guarantee from excessive degradation of the learned model. This work establishes the core problem framing that subsequent contributions build upon: how to inject noise into the global aggregation pipeline without undermining convergence.

Building directly on this concern for the privacy–performance trade-off, a later study [arxiv_2304.05127] approaches the problem from an optimization-theoretic angle. Rather than modifying the noise mechanism itself, the authors argue that the interaction between the number of local gradient steps performed by each client and the total number of communication rounds is a critical but underexplored lever for managing convergence under a fixed privacy budget. Their central claim is that an optimal balance exists between these two quantities, and they provide formal convergence bounds for a DP variant of the SCAFFOLD algorithm that make this trade-off explicit. This is a meaningful conceptual advance over [arxiv_2003.13761] because it shifts attention from the noise injection mechanism alone to the broader algorithmic configuration of the federated pipeline. By proving that convergence can be maximized within a given privacy budget through careful scheduling of local computation and global communication, the work demonstrates that server-level DP need not be treated as a fixed overhead but can instead be managed as part of a jointly optimized system. The implication is that practitioners can recover a substantial portion of the utility lost to DP noise simply by tuning the ratio of local steps to communication rounds.

The most technically complex contribution in this group is CorBin-FL [arxiv_2409.13133], which addresses not only privacy and convergence but also communication efficiency, a constraint that becomes binding in large-scale deployments. The mechanism introduces correlated binary stochastic quantization, wherein clients use shared common randomness—coordinated through secure multi-party computation—to quantize their local model updates in a correlated rather than independent fashion before transmission to the server. This correlation is the key innovation: whereas independent quantization introduces variance that must be compensated by additional noise or additional rounds, correlated quantization allows the aggregated updates to retain more signal, improving the mean squared error of the privacy mechanism. The authors prove that CorBin-FL achieves parameter-level local differential privacy (PLDP) and that it asymptotically optimizes the privacy–utility trade-off as measured by mean squared error. Compared to [arxiv_2003.13761] and [arxiv_2304.05127], which treat quantization and communication as separate or secondary concerns, CorBin-FL integrates all three objectives—privacy, accuracy, and communication cost—into a single mechanism design. This makes it the most comprehensive approach among the three, though also the most demanding in terms of the cryptographic infrastructure required to establish correlated randomness among clients.

Taken together, these three works trace a clear trajectory in the design of aggregation-level DP mechanisms for federated learning: from establishing that concentrated DP can preserve utility [arxiv_2003.13761], to demonstrating that algorithmic scheduling can further recover performance within a fixed budget [arxiv_2304.05127], to proposing a unified mechanism that simultaneously addresses privacy, convergence, and communication through correlated quantization [arxiv_2409.13133].

##### Local Differential Privacy and Client-Side Privacy Mechanisms

Local differential privacy (LDP) represents a particularly stringent instantiation of privacy protection within federated learning, as it requires that each client perturb or otherwise protect its data before any information leaves the local device, thereby eliminating reliance on a trusted central aggregator. The two works examined in this subsection approach LDP from complementary angles: one addresses the integration of LDP with gradient-based optimization in a general federated setting, while the other applies client-side privacy mechanisms specifically to tree-based ensemble models, together illustrating both the foundational principles and applied extensions of local privacy guarantees.

At the foundational level, the work by [arxiv_2209.14086] motivates LDP as a response to a well-recognized limitation of centralized differential privacy, namely the assumption that a trusted third-party data curator exists. In practice, this assumption frequently fails to hold, and the authors argue that local differential privacy offers a stronger and more realistic privacy model because each participant retains full control over what is disclosed before any transmission occurs. Building on this premise, [arxiv_2209.14086] proposes integrating federated learning with LDP through a momentum gradient descent mechanism. The incorporation of momentum is significant: standard LDP-based gradient perturbation introduces noise that can substantially degrade convergence, and momentum-based updates are intended to stabilize the optimization trajectory by accumulating gradient history, thereby partially compensating for the variance injected by the privacy mechanism. This combination addresses two simultaneous concerns—privacy leakage and communication cost—by ensuring that the noisy gradients transmitted to the server are both locally privatized and more informative per round than those produced by naive stochastic updates. The work thus establishes a methodological template for thinking about LDP not merely as a noise-injection step appended to an existing algorithm, but as a component that must be co-designed with the underlying optimizer.

Moving from gradient-based models to tree-based models, [arxiv_2106.10662] demonstrates that the challenge of client-side privacy extends well beyond neural network training. XGBoost and related gradient boosting frameworks are widely used in practice, yet their adaptation to federated learning has been constrained by the high overhead of conventional cryptographic privacy methods. The authors of [arxiv_2106.10662] propose two distinct protocols under the FedXGBoost umbrella. The first, FedXGBoost-SMM, employs an enhanced secure matrix multiplication method that achieves lossless accuracy with lower computational overhead than encryption-based alternatives, operating at the level of matrix operations rather than individual data values. The second, FedXGBoost-LDP, applies noise perturbation directly at the client side, aligning it conceptually with the local differential privacy paradigm explored in [arxiv_2209.14086], though in the structurally different context of histogram-based tree splitting rather than continuous gradient updates.

Comparing the two works reveals an important tension in the LDP literature. [arxiv_2209.14086] treats LDP as the primary privacy mechanism and focuses on recovering optimization quality through algorithmic design choices such as momentum. [arxiv_2106.10662], by contrast, presents LDP as one of two parallel solutions, juxtaposing it against a secure computation alternative and acknowledging that the LDP variant is heuristically designed and empirically evaluated rather than formally optimized for the tree-boosting setting. This contrast highlights a recurring theme in applied federated learning research: LDP is attractive for its strong, assumption-free guarantees and low communication overhead, but it often requires domain-specific adaptation—whether through momentum scheduling for gradient descent or through careful noise calibration for discrete histogram statistics—to remain practically viable. Together, these papers illustrate that local differential privacy is not a monolithic technique but a design principle whose concrete realization must be tailored to the computational structure of the learning algorithm it protects.

#### DP-FL under System Constraints: Communication Efficiency and Secure Aggregation

The intersection of differential privacy (DP) and federated learning (FL) under real-world system constraints represents one of the most practically consequential research frontiers in the field. While early theoretical treatments of DP-FL tended to isolate privacy analysis from deployment realities, a growing body of work has begun to address the fundamental tensions that arise when privacy guarantees must coexist with communication overhead, partial device participation, and secure aggregation protocols. The papers reviewed in this subsection trace a progression from identifying these tensions to proposing concrete mechanisms that resolve them.

A foundational contribution to this area is the work of [arxiv_2101.12240], which directly confronts the compounding difficulties that emerge when communication-reduction techniques are applied in privacy-sensitive federated settings. The authors observe that the dominant strategies for reducing communication overhead in FL — model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation — each introduce additional training variance, a problem that is already exacerbated in FL by data heterogeneity, since devices sample from potentially non-identical distributions. Variance-reduction algorithms designed to address heterogeneity typically require the transmission of auxiliary control information, thereby consuming the very communication budget that practitioners are trying to conserve. The paper thus frames the problem as a tripartite tension: communication efficiency, statistical variance, and differential privacy cannot be optimized independently. By bringing DP explicitly into this system-level analysis, [arxiv_2101.12240] establishes that privacy noise interacts non-trivially with the variance already introduced by compression and sparse participation, making naive combinations of these techniques potentially harmful to both utility and formal privacy guarantees. This work is significant precisely because it refuses to treat DP as an add-on layer and instead positions it as a first-class constraint that reshapes the design space for communication-efficient FL.

Building on this understanding of system-level interactions, more recent work has turned to the specific challenge of reconciling secure aggregation with the multi-step local training that is standard in practical FL deployments. Secure aggregation protocols are attractive because they allow a server to compute an aggregate of client updates without observing individual contributions, which in principle strengthens the privacy guarantees achievable under DP. However, as [arxiv_2407.19286] demonstrates, the combination of secure aggregation with sample-level differential privacy has historically imposed a severe constraint: to fully benefit from the privacy amplification that secure aggregation enables, parties have generally been required to communicate after every local optimization step, effectively eliminating the communication savings that make FL practical in the first place. This creates a direct conflict with the communication-efficiency goals identified by [arxiv_2101.12240], since forcing per-step communication negates the variance-reduction and bandwidth benefits of periodic aggregation.

The contribution of [arxiv_2407.19286] is to show that a refined privacy analysis dissolves this apparent incompatibility. By introducing a new analytical framework, the authors demonstrate that clients can perform multiple local optimization steps while still obtaining the formal privacy benefits of secure aggregation, yielding higher-utility models without sacrificing the privacy guarantees that motivate the use of secure aggregation in the first place. This represents a meaningful methodological advance over the state of affairs described in [arxiv_2101.12240], where the interaction between privacy mechanisms and communication strategies was characterized primarily as a source of tension rather than as a problem admitting clean resolution.

Taken together, these two works illustrate a clear trajectory in the literature. [arxiv_2101.12240] maps the problem space by showing that communication constraints, data heterogeneity, and differential privacy form an interdependent system that cannot be addressed piecemeal. [arxiv_2407.19286] then advances the frontier by providing a tighter analysis that allows secure aggregation and multi-step local training to coexist without sacrificing privacy guarantees, directly addressing one of the most practically limiting constraints identified in earlier work. The progression from problem characterization to algorithmic resolution reflects the maturation of DP-FL research as it moves from idealized settings toward deployment under realistic system constraints.

### Secure Aggregation, Decentralized Architectures, and Communication Efficiency

## Secure Aggregation, Decentralized Architectures, and Communication Efficiency

The three child sections gathered here share a common structural concern: how to reduce or eliminate dependence on a trusted, always-available central server without sacrificing the correctness, privacy, or scalability of the learning process. Each section attacks a distinct dimension of that problem, yet the dimensions are deeply interdependent, and reading them in sequence from simpler to more complex reveals a coherent engineering logic.

The first section, on cryptographic and protocol-level secure aggregation, establishes the foundational vocabulary. It asks what guarantees can be provided when a central aggregator must still exist but cannot be fully trusted, answering with progressively stronger cryptographic constructions. This is the appropriate starting point because the threat models and privacy definitions introduced here carry forward into every subsequent discussion.

The second section, on decentralized and peer-to-peer architectures, extends those concerns by asking what happens when the central aggregator is removed entirely. The privacy and consensus challenges that arise in serverless topologies presuppose familiarity with the aggregation protocols examined in the first section, making the sequencing natural rather than arbitrary.

The third section, on communication efficiency, addresses the practical cost of operating either a cryptographically secured or a fully decentralized system at scale. Compression, coding, and straggler-mitigation strategies are meaningless in isolation; their importance becomes clear only once the reader understands the protocol overhead introduced by the earlier two sections.

Together, the three sections trace a path from cryptographic correctness through architectural decentralization to operational feasibility, collectively defining the design space for privacy-preserving, server-light federated learning.

#### Cryptographic and Protocol-Level Secure Aggregation

Cryptographic and protocol-level approaches to secure aggregation in federated learning span a spectrum from foundational survey frameworks to increasingly sophisticated constructions designed to resist stronger adversarial models. Understanding this progression requires beginning with the conceptual landscape before examining specific protocol designs.

A natural entry point is the comprehensive survey offered by [arxiv_2203.17005], which maps the terrain of privacy-preserving aggregation (PPAgg) protocols across federated learning systems. The authors observe that while federated learning was originally conceived to reduce privacy risk by keeping raw data local, the exchange of model gradients or parameters during aggregation introduces its own leakage surface. Their survey organises PPAgg mechanisms according to the cryptographic primitives they employ—including secret sharing, homomorphic encryption, and multi-party computation (MPC)—and identifies the trade-offs each approach makes among communication cost, computational overhead, and the strength of the privacy guarantee. This taxonomic work establishes the vocabulary and evaluation criteria against which individual protocol proposals can be assessed.

Building directly on the MPC tradition, [arxiv_2201.07730] presents SCOTCH, a decentralised m-party secure computation framework that deploys secret sharing as its core primitive. The authors motivate their design by noting that prior cryptographic approaches, while theoretically sound, frequently incur large communication overheads or degrade model accuracy. SCOTCH addresses this by distributing the aggregation role across multiple parties rather than centralising it in a single server, thereby eliminating the trusted-aggregator assumption. The protocol is described as simple and efficient, offering strict privacy guarantees against both curious aggregators and colluding data owners while keeping communication costs competitive with state-of-the-art alternatives. In this sense, SCOTCH represents a relatively direct application of classical MPC and secret-sharing ideas to the federated setting, prioritising practicality and low overhead.

The complexity of the threat model increases substantially in [arxiv_2501.05053], which introduces TAPFed. Where SCOTCH assumes that a sufficient majority of parties behave honestly, TAPFed is explicitly designed for environments in which some aggregators may be actively malicious. The authors note that existing secure aggregation solutions remain vulnerable to recently demonstrated inference attacks, including disaggregation attacks that can isolate individual contributions even when aggregation appears to be privacy-preserving. To counter this, TAPFed employs a threshold functional encryption scheme distributed across multiple decentralised aggregators, tolerating a bounded number of malicious participants while preserving both security and utility. The use of threshold functional encryption represents a more complex cryptographic construction than the secret-sharing primitives central to SCOTCH, reflecting the need to harden the protocol against a strictly stronger adversary. Comparing the two, SCOTCH optimises for efficiency under an honest-but-curious model, whereas TAPFed accepts greater cryptographic complexity in exchange for robustness against malicious aggregators—a trade-off that mirrors a broader pattern identified in [arxiv_2203.17005] between lightweight constructions and stronger security guarantees.

Complementing these protocol-level contributions, [arxiv_2002.08423] introduces PrivacyFL, a configurable simulator for privacy-preserving and secure federated learning environments. Rather than proposing a new cryptographic primitive, PrivacyFL provides an experimental infrastructure that allows researchers and practitioners to evaluate the feasibility of collaborative training under various security and privacy configurations before committing to a real deployment. Its extensible architecture supports the integration of different aggregation mechanisms, making it a useful empirical counterpart to the theoretical constructions described above. In the context of this subsection, PrivacyFL occupies a supporting role: it does not advance the cryptographic frontier but enables systematic comparison of protocols such as those in SCOTCH and TAPFed under controlled conditions.

Taken together, these works trace a clear methodological arc. The survey in [arxiv_2203.17005] establishes the problem space and primitive taxonomy; SCOTCH [arxiv_2201.07730] demonstrates that MPC-based secret sharing can achieve efficient aggregation under standard adversarial assumptions; TAPFed [arxiv_2501.05053] extends the construction to threshold functional encryption to address more powerful attacks; and PrivacyFL [arxiv_2002.08423] provides the simulation tooling needed to evaluate such protocols empirically. The progression reflects the field's movement from conceptually straightforward cryptographic deployments toward increasingly sophisticated schemes capable of withstanding realistic, multi-party adversarial conditions.

#### Decentralized Architectures and Privacy-Preserving Peer-to-Peer Aggregation

The evolution of federated learning toward fully decentralized, serverless architectures represents a significant departure from the canonical coordinator-dependent paradigm, addressing fundamental vulnerabilities associated with central aggregation points while introducing new challenges around consensus, privacy, and communication topology. Understanding this trajectory requires moving from foundational theoretical frameworks through to applied protocol design.

A systematic treatment of the conceptual foundations is offered by [arxiv_2306.02570], which examines the intersection of decentralized optimization and federated learning as a coherent research program. The authors argue that while federated learning has been extensively studied, the dominant centralized setting remains vulnerable to single-point failures, and that decentralized communication topologies offer a principled alternative. Their analysis organizes the challenges and opportunities along three axes—model, data, and communication—providing a taxonomy that situates subsequent protocol-level contributions within a broader theoretical landscape. This framing is important because it clarifies that decentralized federated learning is not merely a topological variant of the standard paradigm but requires rethinking optimization assumptions at each of these levels.

Building on this conceptual groundwork, [arxiv_2012.07183] presents one of the earlier concrete protocol proposals for privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation. The authors formulate the aggregation problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), a well-established tool in distributed optimization, and critically examine the privacy weaknesses that arise even when data never leaves local nodes. Their key contribution is a communication-control mechanism that restricts which participants exchange information in each aggregation round, thereby minimizing privacy leakage without resorting to computationally expensive techniques such as homomorphic encryption or the utility-degrading noise injection of differential privacy. Privacy guarantees are established against an honest-but-curious adversary model, and an efficient algorithm for constructing the requisite communication pattern is proposed. This work is notable for demonstrating that structural constraints on the communication graph itself can serve as a privacy mechanism, a conceptual move that anticipates later work on dynamic topologies.

The assumption of a static communication graph, however, is relaxed in [arxiv_2210.00325], which addresses the more realistic scenario of high-mobility environments where peer-to-peer connectivity varies between successive aggregation rounds. The authors propose the first consensus-based algorithm explicitly designed for time-varying communication graphs in a fully decentralized, coordinator-free setting. Their approach integrates the Metropolis-Hastings method to dynamically recompute the weighted adjacency matrix at each round, ensuring that consensus can still be reached despite topological instability. Privacy is enforced through Shamir's secret sharing scheme, which distributes model information across peers such that no individual node can reconstruct another's contribution. Compared to [arxiv_2012.07183], which achieves privacy through communication restriction on a presumably stable graph, this work addresses a harder problem by combining cryptographic secret sharing with adaptive consensus under graph variability, representing a meaningful advance in robustness.

Complementing these protocol-oriented contributions, [arxiv_2311.16008] takes an applied experimental perspective, deploying a peer-to-peer federated learning environment augmented with differential privacy and evaluating the privacy-utility trade-off on benchmark datasets. The authors highlight that while local training in federated learning provides baseline privacy, the system remains susceptible to membership inference attacks, motivating the addition of differential privacy mechanisms. Their findings underscore that parameter selection for differential privacy is central to managing the trade-off between model utility and privacy protection, a practical concern that connects back to the theoretical critique raised in [arxiv_2012.07183] regarding the costs of noise-based approaches. Taken together, these four contributions trace a coherent arc from abstract decentralized optimization theory through ADMM-based and consensus-based protocol design to empirical evaluation, collectively establishing the current state of decentralized, privacy-preserving federated learning research.

#### Communication Efficiency: Compression, Coding, and Straggler Mitigation

Communication efficiency has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in federated learning (FL), prompting a substantial body of research that spans broad taxonomic surveys as well as targeted algorithmic frameworks. Understanding this landscape requires moving from general characterizations of the problem space toward the specific mechanisms that have been proposed to address it.

At the most general level, the communication bottleneck in FL arises because iterative model training requires repeated exchange of high-dimensional parameter vectors between potentially large numbers of clients and a central server. The survey by [arxiv_2208.01200] provides a systematic account of this challenge, organizing the field around three interconnected perspectives: communication efficiency, communication environment, and communication resource allocation. By mapping the relationships among these dimensions, the authors reveal that communication problems in FL are not isolated technical inconveniences but are structurally intertwined with the broader goals of privacy preservation and collaborative model convergence. The survey's framing is particularly useful because it situates compression and bandwidth-reduction strategies within a wider ecosystem that includes network heterogeneity and resource constraints, thereby establishing the conceptual scaffolding within which more specific contributions can be evaluated. Crucially, [arxiv_2208.01200] underscores that the transition from traditional distributed machine learning—where raw data traverses the network—to FL introduces new communication pathologies precisely because the privacy-preserving substitution of model updates for data does not eliminate, and may in some respects exacerbate, the volume and sensitivity of transmitted information.

Moving from this general survey perspective toward a specific methodological contribution, [arxiv_2201.10092] addresses two interrelated failure modes that the broader literature identifies as central: the straggler problem and unstable convergence under client heterogeneity. The authors propose Stochastic Coded Federated Learning (SCFL), a framework in which each client constructs a privacy-preserving coded dataset by adding calibrated additive noise to a random linear combination of its local data samples. These coded datasets are uploaded to the parameter server, which aggregates them into a composite dataset capable of compensating for the contributions of straggling clients—those whose heterogeneous computational resources or fluctuating communication rates prevent timely participation. This coded redundancy strategy is conceptually distinct from the gradient compression and quantization techniques surveyed in [arxiv_2208.01200], though it addresses the same underlying communication bottleneck from a different angle: rather than reducing the bit-width or sparsity of transmitted updates, SCFL redistributes informational content across clients so that the server can proceed without waiting for the slowest participants.

The relationship between these two works is therefore one of complementarity rather than competition. [arxiv_2208.01200] supplies the taxonomic context that allows one to appreciate where coded approaches fit within the broader landscape of communication-reduction strategies, while [arxiv_2201.10092] demonstrates that coding theory offers a principled path to simultaneously satisfying convergence guarantees, straggler mitigation, and differential privacy constraints—objectives that purely compression-based methods do not always address jointly. The SCFL framework is notable for providing formal convergence analysis alongside its privacy guarantees, a combination that is not universally present in the compression-oriented methods catalogued by the survey.

Taken together, these contributions illustrate a general-to-specific trajectory in the literature on communication efficiency in FL. Broad surveys such as [arxiv_2208.01200] establish that no single technique suffices to resolve the multifaceted communication challenge, while targeted frameworks such as SCFL [arxiv_2201.10092] demonstrate how stochastic and coded methods can be engineered to address several of these challenges simultaneously. The field continues to require both kinds of contribution: comprehensive mappings of the problem space to guide research priorities, and rigorous algorithmic proposals that translate those priorities into deployable solutions with provable guarantees.

### Surveys, Taxonomies, and Systematic Reviews of Federated Learning

The federated learning (FL) literature has generated a substantial body of integrative scholarship aimed at mapping the field's conceptual boundaries, tracing its intellectual origins, and cataloguing its principal technical challenges. Taken together, these surveys and systematic reviews serve as essential reference points for researchers seeking orientation within a rapidly evolving domain.

An early and foundational contribution to this integrative tradition is the work situating FL explicitly within the broader history of distributed machine learning [arxiv_2010.09258]. Written in 2020, this piece frames FL as an improved iteration of distributed machine learning (DML), arguing that the central server in FL is repositioned from a micro-managing coordinator to a more passive aggregator, thereby affording substantially stronger privacy and security guarantees. The architecture described relies on smart devices—smartphones and IoT sensors—that retain raw data locally, transmitting only encrypted model updates for aggregation into a global model. This framing proved influential in establishing the conceptual vocabulary through which subsequent surveys would characterise FL's relationship to its distributed computing antecedents.

Building on this lineage, a more architecturally detailed survey published in 2022 similarly traces FL's origins in distributed machine learning while offering a functional decomposition of FL systems [arxiv_2104.14362]. Where the earlier work emphasised the privacy-security axis as FL's defining departure from DML, this survey broadens the analytical lens to encompass distributed training protocols, data communication strategies, and system-level security considerations. The authors propose an explicit taxonomy of related techniques, providing a structured vocabulary that subsequent researchers could use to position novel contributions. Crucially, both works acknowledge that the regulatory and legal constraints governing data sharing across organisations and jurisdictions are not merely incidental concerns but constitutive drivers of FL's emergence as a paradigm—a point that would become increasingly prominent in later surveys.

The 2023 systematic survey [arxiv_2301.01299] represents a methodological step forward in the integrative literature by explicitly acknowledging the limitations of prior surveys, noting that most existing reviews focus on a specific perspective or fail to capture the latest research progress. In response, the authors adopt a more comprehensive and structured approach, reviewing recent advances across multiple dimensions of FL methodology and application. This self-reflexive positioning—situating the survey against the accumulated body of prior reviews—signals the field's growing maturity and the increasing difficulty of achieving genuinely comprehensive coverage. The paper's four major contributions, while not fully enumerated in the available abstract, are framed as addressing this gap through systematic breadth.

The most recent survey in this group [arxiv_2504.17703], dated 2026, reflects the field's continued expansion and the consolidation of certain recurring themes. Like its predecessors, it begins with core FL architecture and the standard lifecycle of local training, model aggregation, and global updates, but it places particular emphasis on the persistent technical challenges of non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) data and system heterogeneity. The survey also foregrounds application domains—healthcare, finance, and smart IoT systems—as primary motivating contexts, a framing that reflects the field's maturation from theoretical proposal to practical deployment. Compared with the 2020 and 2022 surveys, which were largely concerned with establishing FL's conceptual identity relative to DML, this later work assumes that identity as settled and turns attention toward the operational and regulatory complexities of real-world implementation.

Across this chronological arc, several consistent themes emerge: the centrality of privacy and data sovereignty as motivating concerns; the tension between communication efficiency and model quality; and the challenge of heterogeneous data distributions. What shifts over time is the level of analytical granularity and the degree to which surveys engage self-consciously with the accumulated literature they are synthesising. Collectively, these works provide the integrative scaffolding upon which more specialised FL research is built.

## Conclusion

Federated learning has matured from a conceptually elegant proposal into a multifaceted research discipline whose challenges and solutions span optimization theory, distributed systems, cryptography, and statistical privacy. This review has traced that maturation along a foundational-to-applied axis, revealing both the coherence of the field's intellectual architecture and the productive tensions that continue to drive it forward.

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this survey. First, the tension between model utility and privacy is not resolved but managed: differential privacy mechanisms, whether applied centrally, at the aggregation layer, or locally at the client, each occupy distinct points on the utility-privacy frontier, and no single approach dominates across all deployment contexts. Second, the non-IID nature of federated data is not merely a technical inconvenience but a fundamental characteristic of the federated setting that propagates through optimization convergence, fairness, and robustness to adversarial manipulation. Third, communication efficiency and privacy are deeply intertwined; compression and secure aggregation protocols must be co-designed rather than treated as independent modules if federated systems are to scale to realistic deployments.

Decentralized architectures represent perhaps the most structurally ambitious response to these challenges, removing the central server as both a bottleneck and a trust assumption, yet they introduce their own complexities in consensus, topology sensitivity, and privacy accounting. The cryptographic approaches to secure aggregation similarly offer strong formal guarantees at the cost of computational and communication overhead that remains an active area of optimization.

Looking forward, the field's most pressing open problems lie at the intersections of these themes: achieving meaningful differential privacy guarantees under severe communication constraints, designing robust aggregation protocols that are simultaneously efficient and resistant to Byzantine participants, and developing optimization algorithms that provably converge under realistic heterogeneity without sacrificing privacy. The surveys and taxonomies reviewed here provide essential scaffolding for this ongoing work, but the field's dynamism ensures that any such map requires continual revision. Federated learning remains a domain where foundational questions and applied imperatives are productively inseparable.
