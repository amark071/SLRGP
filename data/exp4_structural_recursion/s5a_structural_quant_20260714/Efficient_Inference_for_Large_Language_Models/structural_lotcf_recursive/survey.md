## Introduction

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) across research and industry has brought with it an equally pressing challenge: making these models fast, affordable, and practical to deploy at scale. While the expressive power of modern LLMs has transformed natural language processing, their computational and memory demands impose severe constraints on real-world inference—constraints that span hardware budgets, latency requirements, energy consumption, and accessibility. Efficient inference for LLMs has consequently emerged as one of the most active and consequential research frontiers in artificial intelligence, drawing contributions from algorithm design, systems engineering, hardware architecture, and applied machine learning.

This literature review surveys the methods developed to address these challenges, organized along a foundational-to-applied axis that reflects how the field has matured. The review begins with broad surveys that situate inference efficiency within the full lifecycle of LLM development, from training-time decisions through deployment, before narrowing to frameworks specifically concerned with inference evaluation and benchmarking. From this foundation, the review proceeds through the principal families of model compression: post-training quantization, quantization-aware parameter-efficient fine-tuning, pruning, knowledge distillation, and neural architecture search. These techniques reduce the intrinsic cost of a model before it ever receives a query.

The review then turns to a cluster of methods targeting the key-value (KV) cache, which has emerged as a central bottleneck in autoregressive generation. Strategies here range from token selection and attention sparsity to low-bit compression of cached entries and architectural redesigns that structurally reduce cache footprint. Systems-level surveys that integrate these KV cache strategies into coherent deployment pipelines bridge the gap between individual algorithmic contributions and practical infrastructure.

Finally, the review examines the frontier of inference serving, speculative decoding, and hardware-software co-optimization—approaches that treat the inference stack holistically, coordinating model behavior, scheduling, and hardware utilization to extract maximum throughput and minimum latency.

Throughout, the organizing principle is methodological: each section addresses a distinct lever by which inference cost can be reduced, and the progression from foundational to applied reflects both logical dependency and historical development. Compression methods that reshape a model's parameters precede runtime strategies that manage how those parameters are accessed, which in turn precede systems-level orchestration that governs how requests are served. By following this structure, the review aims to give readers both a principled understanding of the design space and a practical map of the state of the art in efficient LLM inference.

#### Quantization Methods for LLM Compression and Inference

## Quantization Methods for LLM Compression and Inference

Quantization has become one of the most consequential families of techniques for making large language models tractable at inference time, operating by reducing the numerical precision of weights, activations, or both, thereby shrinking memory footprints and accelerating computation without redesigning model architectures. The two child sections collected here represent the natural progression of quantization research as it has matured from a standalone compression tool into a component deeply integrated with model adaptation workflows.

Readers should begin with the post-training quantization section, which establishes the foundational vocabulary and core trade-offs of the field. PTQ methods require no retraining and can be applied to already-deployed models, making them the entry point through which most practitioners first encounter quantization. Understanding the constraints PTQ operates under—particularly the tension between aggressive bit-width reduction and preserved model quality—is essential context for appreciating why the field did not stop there.

The second section then shows what happens when quantization is no longer treated as a terminal, read-only operation but is instead coupled with parameter-efficient fine-tuning. This pairing introduces new degrees of freedom: rather than simply tolerating quantization error, models can be adapted to partially recover from it, or fine-tuned objectives can be pursued at reduced precision from the outset. The progression from PTQ to quantization-aware PEFT thus mirrors a broader intellectual movement from compression-as-post-processing toward compression-as-co-design, and reading the sections in this order makes that conceptual shift legible.

##### Post-Training Quantization for LLM Inference Acceleration

Post-training quantization (PTQ) has emerged as one of the most practically viable strategies for reducing the memory footprint and computational cost of large language models (LLMs) without requiring the expensive retraining that characterises quantization-aware training approaches. By compressing model weights and activations after training is complete, PTQ methods can be applied to already-deployed models, making them attractive for both research and production settings. The literature in this area progresses from relatively straightforward weight-only quantization schemes targeting specific hardware to more sophisticated frameworks capable of handling arbitrary numerical precisions across heterogeneous computing environments.

At the simpler end of the methodological spectrum, the work presented in [arxiv_2311.00502] addresses the practical challenge of deploying LLMs on central processing units (CPUs), which remain the most widely available compute substrate in enterprise and edge environments. The authors observe that the primary bottleneck for LLM inference is not raw arithmetic throughput but rather memory capacity and memory bandwidth, since the sheer volume of model parameters demands continuous data movement between storage and compute units. To mitigate this, they propose an automatic INT4 weight-only quantization flow that reduces the precision of stored weights to four-bit integers while preserving higher-precision arithmetic during the actual matrix computations. Crucially, the contribution extends beyond the quantization algorithm itself: the authors design a specialised LLM runtime equipped with highly optimised kernels tailored to CPU instruction sets, demonstrating that hardware-aware software engineering is as important as the numerical compression scheme. Validation across popular model families including Llama, Llama 2, and GPT-NeoX confirms the general applicability of the approach, and the publicly released implementation through Intel's extension for Transformers lowers the barrier to adoption. This work thus establishes a clear baseline for CPU-centric PTQ: a single fixed bit-width (INT4), applied only to weights, with inference acceleration achieved primarily through kernel optimisation rather than algorithmic novelty in the quantization itself.

Moving toward greater complexity, [arxiv_2408.08554] identifies two fundamental limitations that constrain PTQ at scale. The first is the well-documented accuracy degradation that accompanies aggressive low-bit quantization, where the coarse numerical grid introduces approximation errors that compound across the many layers of a deep transformer. The second, and arguably more architecturally significant, limitation concerns hardware utilisation: conventional GPUs expose only a restricted set of integer computing unit types, which means that when different layers or operations are quantized to different precisions, the resulting mixed-precision matrix multiplications cannot be efficiently mapped onto available hardware primitives. This mismatch between algorithmic flexibility and hardware rigidity has historically forced practitioners to choose a single uniform bit-width across an entire model, sacrificing the potential gains of per-layer or per-tensor precision assignment.

To resolve both issues simultaneously, [arxiv_2408.08554] introduces ABQ-LLM, a framework that supports arbitrary-bit quantization and provides an inference engine capable of executing mixed-precision operations efficiently on GPU hardware. The framework introduces novel algorithmic components that maintain accuracy across a wide range of quantization configurations while the accompanying runtime ensures that the theoretical compute savings of lower-bit representations are realised in practice rather than negated by hardware inefficiency. Compared with the CPU-focused INT4 approach of [arxiv_2311.00502], ABQ-LLM represents a more general and algorithmically richer solution: whereas the former fixes precision and optimises the execution environment, the latter treats bit-width itself as a flexible design variable and addresses the hardware mapping problem as a first-class concern.

Together, these two works illustrate the dual axes along which PTQ research for LLMs advances: hardware-specific deployment optimisation, exemplified by efficient CPU kernels for fixed-precision quantization [arxiv_2311.00502], and generalised algorithmic frameworks that decouple precision selection from hardware constraints to enable fine-grained, accuracy-preserving compression at scale [arxiv_2408.08554]. Both directions reflect the broader imperative to make LLM inference tractable across diverse deployment scenarios without sacrificing model quality.

##### Quantization-Aware Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (QA-PEFT) for LLMs

The intersection of quantization and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has emerged as a productive research direction aimed at simultaneously reducing the memory and computational costs of large language models (LLMs) during both adaptation and deployment. The methods surveyed here address this dual objective through progressively more sophisticated joint optimization strategies, and their chronological development reveals a clear trajectory from post-training quantization combined with selective parameter updates toward fully integrated quantization-aware training frameworks.

An early and influential contribution to this space is AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858], proposed in 2022, which frames the problem as a two-stage pipeline: first applying post-training quantization (PTQ) to a large pre-trained language model, and then fine-tuning only a carefully chosen subset of the resulting quantized parameters for a downstream task. The technical mechanism underlying AlphaTuning is binary-coding quantization, which factorizes full-precision weights into binary parameters and a corresponding set of real-valued scaling factors. During adaptation, the binary values are frozen and only the scaling factors are updated, yielding a parameter-efficient fine-tuning regime that operates entirely within the quantized representation. This design is notable because it avoids the need to maintain full-precision copies of the model weights during adaptation, thereby reducing memory footprints and enabling low-precision computation at inference time. AlphaTuning thus established a foundational paradigm: quantize first, then adapt within the compressed space, rather than treating compression and adaptation as independent sequential concerns.

Building on this paradigm, PEQA (Parameter-Efficient and Quantization-aware Adaptation) [arxiv_2305.14152], introduced in 2023, addresses a closely related but distinct challenge. While AlphaTuning introduces a custom quantization scheme designed to expose trainable scaling factors, PEQA is designed to operate directly on LLMs that have already been quantized using existing sub-4-bit integer quantization techniques, making it more broadly applicable to the growing ecosystem of compressed models. PEQA's core insight is that updating solely the quantization scales—rather than any of the quantized weight values themselves—is sufficient to achieve meaningful task-specific adaptation. This approach is particularly memory-efficient because the optimizer state is confined to a very small set of scalar parameters per layer, and it ensures seamless task transitions without requiring dequantization or the storage of auxiliary full-precision parameters. Compared to AlphaTuning, PEQA places greater emphasis on compatibility with deployment-phase quantization pipelines and explicitly targets the sub-4-bit regime, where accuracy degradation from PTQ is most severe and the need for post-quantization recovery is most acute. Both methods, however, share the limitation of relying on PTQ as their quantization strategy, which inherently constrains the degree to which accuracy loss can be recovered during the adaptation phase.

This limitation is directly addressed by L4Q [arxiv_2402.04902], proposed in 2025, which argues that PTQ-then-PEFT pipelines are fundamentally constrained in their ability to recover accuracy because the quantization itself is not optimized jointly with the fine-tuning objective. L4Q instead integrates Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), allowing the model to learn quantization-friendly weight representations during the adaptation process itself. To make QAT tractable at the scale of LLMs, L4Q introduces a memory-optimized layer design that keeps the overall training cost competitive with PEFT-only baselines. This represents a meaningful methodological departure from both AlphaTuning and PEQA: rather than accepting the accuracy penalty of PTQ and attempting to compensate through selective parameter updates, L4Q treats quantization as a differentiable component of the training graph. The result is a tighter coupling between the compression objective and the task-adaptation objective, which the authors argue yields superior accuracy under equivalent memory constraints.

Taken together, these three works trace a coherent methodological evolution. AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858] established the principle of adapting within a quantized parameter space; PEQA [arxiv_2305.14152] extended this to sub-4-bit regimes with broader compatibility; and L4Q [arxiv_2402.04902] overcame the shared reliance on PTQ by embedding quantization awareness directly into the fine-tuning loop. Each successive method responds to identifiable limitations in its predecessors, reflecting the field's growing recognition that efficient inference and low-cost adaptation must be co-designed rather than treated as separable problems.

Reading the sections in this order—from weight-level compression to structure-level redesign—mirrors the logical dependency between the two approaches: one must understand what can be achieved by compressing a fixed architecture before appreciating why searching over architectures themselves becomes a meaningful next step.

##### Pruning and Distillation for LLM Compression

The compression of large language models through pruning and knowledge distillation represents a methodological continuum that spans from relatively straightforward structural interventions to sophisticated, multi-stage pipelines integrating hardware-aware optimization. Understanding this progression requires situating each approach within the broader tension between model capacity and deployment feasibility.

A foundational insight motivating the entire compression agenda is articulated by [arxiv_2002.11794], which challenges the intuition that smaller models are inherently more efficient. The authors demonstrate that wider and deeper Transformer models converge in substantially fewer training steps, such that the computational savings from faster per-iteration execution of smaller models are often outweighed by the greater number of iterations required to reach comparable accuracy. This leads to the counterintuitive prescription of training extremely large models but halting early, a strategy they term "train large, then compress." The implication is significant: compression is not merely a post-hoc convenience but a necessary component of a rational training and deployment strategy. By reframing model size as a variable to be optimized across the full training-plus-inference lifecycle, [arxiv_2002.11794] establishes the conceptual groundwork upon which more targeted compression methods are built.

Building on this premise, a natural first class of methods involves identifying and removing structural redundancy within already-trained models. [arxiv_2404.05741] investigates a particularly simple form of this approach: skipping latter attention sublayers in Transformer-based large language models. The rationale is that later attention layers tend to be redundant in their representational contribution while remaining computationally expensive. Empirically, the authors find that applying this layer-skipping strategy to Llama 2 7B yields a 21% increase in single-token generation speed, and, notably, performance on several standard benchmarks improves rather than degrades. This unexpected result suggests that certain architectural components may not only be dispensable but may actively introduce noise or interference in the inference pass. The simplicity of the method is a key virtue: it requires no retraining, no specialized hardware support, and no auxiliary model, making it accessible as a baseline compression technique. However, its scope is limited to a specific structural redundancy and does not address weight-level sparsity or the transfer of representational knowledge.

More complex pipelines address these limitations by combining multiple compression techniques in a coordinated fashion. [arxiv_2211.07715] presents a comprehensive production-oriented pipeline for deploying compressed Transformer models on CPUs, integrating hardware-aware pruning, knowledge distillation, quantization, and a custom inference runtime with optimized kernels for sparse and quantized operators. The inclusion of knowledge distillation is particularly important: rather than simply removing weights or layers and accepting the resulting accuracy loss, distillation allows the compressed model to recover performance by learning from the outputs of the original, larger model. This makes the pipeline qualitatively more complex than layer-skipping approaches, as it requires a teacher model, a distillation training phase, and careful coordination between the pruning mask and the distillation objective. The hardware-aware component further distinguishes this work by ensuring that the sparsity patterns introduced by pruning are aligned with the capabilities of the target CPU architecture, a consideration absent from architecture-agnostic methods. The result is a Fast DistilBERT model that achieves high throughput with minimal accuracy degradation, demonstrating that the combination of these techniques is greater than the sum of its parts.

Taken together, these works trace a clear methodological trajectory. [arxiv_2002.11794] establishes the strategic rationale for compression as an integral part of model development. [arxiv_2404.05741] demonstrates that even the simplest structural interventions, requiring no retraining, can yield meaningful efficiency gains by exploiting architectural redundancy. [arxiv_2211.07715] then shows that production-grade deployment demands a more elaborate orchestration of pruning, distillation, and hardware-specific optimization. The progression from conceptual reframing, through lightweight structural pruning, to full multi-stage compression pipelines reflects the increasing engineering sophistication required as deployment constraints become more stringent.

##### Neural Architecture Search and Architecture-Aware Optimization for LLMs

Neural Architecture Search and Architecture-Aware Optimization for LLMs

The dominant paradigm in large language model (LLM) compression has long centered on weight-level interventions—pruning redundant parameters, quantizing numerical representations, and distilling knowledge into smaller surrogates. While these techniques have yielded meaningful reductions in memory footprint and latency, they share a common limitation: they operate on a fixed architectural blueprint, leaving the question of whether that blueprint is itself optimal entirely unaddressed. A growing body of work has begun to reframe efficient inference as an architectural discovery problem, arguing that structural configuration is at least as consequential as weight optimization. The two works examined in this subsection represent complementary methodological responses to that reframing, one grounded in training-free search over inherited weight subnetworks and the other in the integration of low-rank adaptation with weight-sharing super-networks.

The foundational observation motivating both lines of inquiry is that LLMs are architecturally redundant, not merely parametrically so. Standard compression methods, as noted by [arxiv_2409.17372], concentrate on weight optimization while overlooking the exploration of optimal architectures. This is a non-trivial omission: even a well-pruned or quantized model may retain structural inefficiencies—unnecessary layers, suboptimal attention head configurations, or poorly sized feed-forward blocks—that impose avoidable computational costs at inference time. Traditional Neural Architecture Search (NAS) methods, however, were not designed with the scale of modern LLMs in mind; their complexity grows prohibitively with parameter count, rendering them largely ineffective in this regime without significant adaptation [arxiv_2409.17372].

To address this scalability barrier, [arxiv_2409.17372] proposes a training-free architecture search framework specifically designed for LLMs. Rather than training candidate architectures from scratch or relying on expensive gradient-based search, the framework identifies optimal subnets that inherit specific weights directly from the original pretrained model. This inheritance strategy is critical: it allows the search process to bypass the enormous cost of retraining while still producing subnets that preserve the fundamental capabilities of the source model. The result is inference acceleration achieved through structural selection rather than weight modification alone, positioning the approach as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional compression techniques. By decoupling architectural exploration from weight learning, [arxiv_2409.17372] makes NAS tractable at LLM scale without sacrificing the representational quality accumulated during pretraining.

Where [arxiv_2409.17372] prioritizes search efficiency through training-free subnet extraction, [arxiv_2501.16372] pursues a more integrated strategy that combines the expressive flexibility of NAS with the parameter efficiency of low-rank adaptation. The central insight of [arxiv_2501.16372] is that low-rank adapters and weight-sharing super-networks are mutually reinforcing: super-networks provide a unified search space over structural configurations, while low-rank representations constrain the adapter parameters to a compact subspace, reducing the memory and computational overhead of jointly optimizing architecture and fine-tuning weights. This synergy is particularly valuable in resource-constrained deployment scenarios, where both the search process and the resulting model must remain lightweight. The models produced through this combined methodology exhibit reduced memory footprints and faster inference times, and the authors argue that such approaches have the potential to democratize LLM deployment by making capable models accessible outside of high-resource environments [arxiv_2501.16372].

Taken together, these two works trace a methodological arc from training-free structural search toward architecture-and-adaptation co-optimization. [arxiv_2409.17372] establishes that meaningful architectural gains are achievable without any training overhead, providing a practical lower bound on the cost of architecture-aware compression. [arxiv_2501.16372] then extends the frontier by showing that when some training budget is available, integrating low-rank adapters into the NAS pipeline yields models that are simultaneously more compact and more readily fine-tuned. Both contributions underscore a broader shift in the efficient inference literature: the recognition that discovering the right structure is as important as compressing the weights within it, and that these two objectives are most powerfully pursued in concert.

#### Integrated and Systems-Level Compression Frameworks for LLM Deployment

The deployment of large language models (LLMs) in real-world, resource-constrained environments has motivated a growing body of research that moves beyond isolated compression techniques toward integrated, systems-level frameworks capable of addressing multiple efficiency bottlenecks simultaneously. Rather than treating quantization, distillation, or hardware design as independent concerns, recent work increasingly frames efficient inference as a pipeline problem requiring coordinated solutions across algorithmic, architectural, and hardware dimensions.

A foundational observation motivating this direction is that the computational and memory demands of LLMs are multifaceted: they arise from model size, memory bandwidth constraints, training overhead, and the mismatch between general-purpose hardware and the specific access patterns of transformer-based inference. [arxiv_2405.11704] provides a systematic theoretical grounding for this perspective, analyzing how transformer architectures inherently restrict computing efficiency when capturing long-range dependencies, and evaluating how adaptive optimization algorithms such as AdamW, massively parallel computing, and mixed-precision training strategies collectively reduce memory footprint and accelerate convergence. By examining the mathematical principles underlying these techniques, this work establishes that no single optimization strategy is sufficient; rather, their combined application is necessary to address the efficiency bottleneck across both training and inference phases. The paper further reviews compression strategies including quantization and pruning as complementary inference-time tools, situating them within a broader systems-level understanding of model deployment.

Building directly on this multi-technique philosophy, [arxiv_2601.09865] proposes a tightly integrated deployment pipeline that combines GPTQ-based quantization, low-rank adaptation (LoRA), and a specialized data distillation process within a single framework targeting edge devices. The pipeline leverages knowledge distillation via Kullback-Leibler divergence, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, and the Muon optimizer to preserve or enhance task-specific performance while achieving up to 2× memory compression—reducing a 6 GB model to approximately 3 GB. What distinguishes this contribution from prior work that applies these techniques in isolation is the explicit co-design of the distillation and compression stages: task-specific data acquisition, fine-tuning, and model compression are treated as interdependent steps rather than sequential post-hoc operations. This integration is particularly significant for edge deployment scenarios where acquiring labeled data, managing memory budgets, and maintaining inference quality must all be addressed within a unified workflow.

While [arxiv_2601.09865] addresses compression at the model and training-pipeline level, [arxiv_2503.18869] approaches the systems-level challenge from the hardware side, targeting the memory controller in AI accelerators as a locus of optimization. The paper argues that existing techniques such as pruning, quantization, and mixture-of-experts reduce memory capacity and bandwidth consumption but do so at the cost of inference quality degradation. In contrast, the proposed compression-aware memory controller achieves lossless block compression of model weights and key-value (KV) caches using algorithms such as LZ4 and ZSTD, thereby reducing memory capacity and bandwidth usage without compromising output fidelity. Additionally, the controller enables memory bandwidth and energy consumption to scale proportionally with context-dependent dynamic quantization. This hardware-software co-design perspective complements the algorithmic pipeline of [arxiv_2601.09865]: whereas the latter compresses the model prior to deployment, the former optimizes how compressed or uncompressed weights are accessed and managed at runtime. Together, these two works illustrate that efficient inference requires intervention at multiple levels of the deployment stack.

Taken collectively, these studies reflect a maturing understanding that LLM deployment efficiency is an integrated systems problem. [arxiv_2405.11704] provides the theoretical scaffolding by demonstrating how mixed-precision training and adaptive optimization interact to reduce overhead across the full model lifecycle. [arxiv_2601.09865] operationalizes this insight into a practical edge-deployment pipeline combining quantization, adaptation, and distillation. [arxiv_2503.18869] extends the scope further by embedding compression awareness into the hardware memory subsystem itself. The convergence of these approaches—spanning training algorithms, model compression pipelines, and hardware controller design—signals that future progress in efficient LLM inference will increasingly depend on unified frameworks that treat algorithmic and systems-level concerns as inseparable.

### KV Cache Optimization for Memory-Efficient Inference

## Why These Sections Belong Together and How to Read Them

The three child sections collected under this node share a unifying premise: the key-value cache, as it exists in standard transformer inference, is neither memory-efficient nor architecturally inevitable. Each section attacks that premise from a different angle, and together they constitute a coherent progression from the least structurally invasive interventions to the most radical reconceptions of how caching should work.

The recommended reading order follows a deliberate simple-to-complex logic. Token selection and attention sparsity methods form the natural entry point because they operate entirely at runtime, requiring no changes to model weights or architecture—only a principled decision about which cached entries to retain or discard. This makes their assumptions transparent and their failure modes easy to reason about, providing a conceptual baseline against which subsequent approaches can be measured.

Quantization and low-bit compression methods introduce a second axis of reduction: rather than asking which tokens to keep, they ask how precisely each retained token needs to be represented. This layer of complexity builds directly on the eviction literature, since the two strategies are often complementary and share the same motivating observation about memory as the binding constraint.

Architectural redesign and systems-level joint optimization then arrive as the most structurally ambitious tier, questioning whether the cache architecture itself should be fixed at all. Having understood what can be achieved by pruning and compressing within a given architecture, the reader is well positioned to appreciate why some researchers concluded that deeper structural changes were necessary and what trade-offs those changes introduce.

Read in sequence, the three sections trace a coherent escalation from runtime heuristics to representation compression to fundamental architectural rethinking.

#### Token Selection and Attention Sparsity for KV Cache Eviction

Token selection and attention sparsity represent a family of KV cache eviction strategies united by a common principle: not all tokens stored in the cache contribute equally to subsequent attention computations, and identifying the dispensable majority allows the cache to be pruned at runtime without meaningful loss in generation quality. The methods surveyed here progress from relatively straightforward eviction heuristics toward more sophisticated predictive and system-aware frameworks.

The simplest conceptual entry point into this family is the observation that attention weights are naturally sparse — at any given decoding step, only a small subset of previously cached tokens receives substantial attention mass. ALISA [arxiv_2403.17312] builds directly on this insight. Targeting resource-constrained single-GPU deployments where the KV cache can exhaust available memory and cause I/O bottlenecks, ALISA exploits the sparsity inherent in Transformer attention layers to decide which key-value pairs to retain. Because autoregressive inference already replaces quadratic-complexity attention with linear-complexity memory accesses through caching, ALISA frames the problem as managing that linear growth: by keeping only the tokens that attract high attention scores and evicting the rest, the system sustains throughput even as sequence lengths increase. The approach is thus grounded in a direct reading of the attention distribution as a proxy for token importance, making it a natural baseline against which more elaborate methods can be compared.

CORM [arxiv_2404.15949] advances the design by introducing two empirical observations that motivate a more dynamic eviction policy. First, adjacent query vectors in most Transformer models exhibit striking similarity, meaning that the attention pattern computed for one token is a reliable approximation of the pattern for its immediate neighbor. Second, and more consequentially, the attention calculation for the current query can be adequately served by consulting only a small fraction of preceding queries rather than the full history. Together these findings justify a policy in which CORM retains essential key-value pairs by consulting recent message history as a lightweight proxy for the full cache, dynamically deciding at inference time which entries to preserve. Compared with ALISA's relatively static sparsity exploitation, CORM introduces temporal locality as an additional signal: recency and inter-query similarity jointly govern eviction, rather than instantaneous attention magnitude alone. This makes the policy adaptive to the evolving context window without requiring access to future information.

InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] represents the most architecturally ambitious approach among the three. Rather than reacting to observed attention scores or exploiting query similarity post hoc, InfiniGen speculatively predicts which tokens will be important for the next attention layer before that layer is computed. The mechanism performs a minimal rehearsal using the inputs of the current layer together with a partial subset of the query weight matrix and the key cache of the subsequent layer, thereby generating a low-cost approximation of the forthcoming attention distribution. Only the tokens identified as essential by this speculative pass are prefetched from an offloaded KV cache into GPU memory, allowing InfiniGen to integrate tightly with offloading-based inference systems designed for long-text generation. This predictive prefetching strategy addresses a limitation shared by both ALISA and CORM: eviction decisions made on the basis of past or present attention patterns may lag behind the model's actual informational needs at the next step. By looking one layer ahead, InfiniGen converts a reactive pruning problem into a proactive retrieval problem, substantially reducing unnecessary data movement between host and device memory.

Taken together, these three systems trace a clear trajectory of increasing sophistication. ALISA establishes the foundational premise that attention sparsity can be exploited for cache compression [arxiv_2403.17312]. CORM enriches the eviction signal by incorporating inter-query similarity and recency, enabling dynamic retention without architectural modification [arxiv_2404.15949]. InfiniGen then closes the loop by making token selection anticipatory rather than retrospective, coupling sparsity-aware eviction with speculative prefetching to handle the extreme sequence lengths characteristic of long-form generation [arxiv_2406.19707]. The progression reflects a broader recognition that effective KV cache management requires not only identifying unimportant tokens but doing so at the right moment in the inference pipeline.

#### Quantization and Low-Bit Compression of KV Cache Entries

Quantization of key-value (KV) cache entries has emerged as one of the most actively investigated strategies for alleviating the memory bottleneck that constrains large language model (LLM) inference at scale. As sequence lengths, batch sizes, and model sizes continue to grow, the KV cache transitions from a secondary concern into the dominant consumer of GPU memory, making compression of its stored tensors a practical necessity rather than a theoretical curiosity. The literature in this area progresses from relatively straightforward uniform quantization baselines toward increasingly sophisticated schemes that account for the statistical structure of key and value activations, and two representative contributions illustrate this trajectory clearly.

The simplest conceptual starting point is uniform quantization, in which every entry of the KV cache is mapped to the same reduced bit-width representation without regard for the heterogeneous magnitude distribution of the underlying tensors. While computationally convenient, this approach treats all entries as equally important and equally well-behaved, an assumption that proves empirically problematic. As noted in the GEAR framework [arxiv_2403.05527], uniform quantization of all entries frequently incurs high approximation errors, and because autoregressive decoding compounds these errors across successive generation steps, even modest per-step inaccuracies can accumulate into critical deviations in model output. This observation motivates moving beyond flat, entry-wise schemes toward methods that are sensitive to the internal structure of the tensors being compressed.

GEAR [arxiv_2403.05527] represents an intermediate step in this progression by introducing a hybrid compression strategy that differentiates among entries rather than treating them uniformly. The core idea is to apply ultra-low-precision quantization to the majority of entries, specifically those whose magnitudes cluster together and are therefore relatively well-suited to coarse numerical representation, while reserving a complementary error-correction mechanism for the residual approximation error that quantization inevitably introduces. By combining quantization of the bulk of the cache with a lightweight correction for the remaining high-error components, GEAR aims to achieve near-lossless compression at high compression ratios. This two-stage design acknowledges that the damage done by aggressive quantization is not uniformly distributed across the tensor and that targeted remediation of outlier errors can substantially recover generation quality. The approach thus occupies a middle ground between naive uniform quantization and more structurally informed methods, offering a practical recipe that is more robust than simple baselines without requiring a fundamental rethinking of how activations are encoded.

A more structurally motivated departure from entry-wise thinking is offered by Coupled Quantization (CQ) [arxiv_2405.03917], which shifts the unit of analysis from individual scalar entries to groups of channels considered jointly. The key empirical insight driving CQ is that distinct channels within a single key or value activation embedding are not statistically independent; rather, they exhibit strong inter-dependencies such that their joint entropy grows more slowly than the sum of their individual marginal entropies. Treating channels in isolation, as all entry-wise quantization schemes implicitly do, therefore wastes representational capacity by ignoring exploitable redundancy. By coupling multiple channels together and encoding their joint distribution, CQ extracts more information per bit than independent per-channel or per-entry quantization would permit. Crucially, this advantage becomes especially pronounced at very low bit widths, a regime in which existing methods, including more straightforward quantization approaches, are reported to fail [arxiv_2405.03917]. The coupled formulation thus extends the practical frontier of KV cache compression to bit-widths that were previously inaccessible without unacceptable quality degradation.

Taken together, these two contributions trace a clear methodological arc. GEAR [arxiv_2403.05527] addresses the limitations of uniform quantization through a hybrid error-correction strategy that remains entry-centric but adds a corrective layer for high-error residuals. CQ [arxiv_2405.03917] pursues a more fundamental redesign by exploiting inter-channel statistical dependencies to encode activations in a genuinely more information-efficient manner. Both works confirm that naive uniform quantization is insufficient for aggressive compression, but they diagnose the problem differently and propose solutions at different levels of structural sophistication, reflecting the broader trend in this subfield toward increasingly principled exploitation of the internal geometry of KV cache tensors.

#### Architectural Redesign, Layer-Level Sharing, and Systems-Level Joint Optimization

## Architectural Redesign, Layer-Level Sharing, and Systems-Level Joint Optimization

The two child sections collected here represent the most structurally ambitious tier of KV cache research, and they belong together precisely because both reject the assumption that a fixed, uniform cache architecture is the appropriate starting point for optimization. Where earlier nodes in this review address what to store or how precisely to store it, the work synthesized here asks a more fundamental question: whether the architecture generating and consuming the cache should itself be reconsidered.

Reading these sections in sequence reflects a natural movement from design to deployment. The first section, on architectural and layer-level redesign, establishes the structural vocabulary—layer consolidation, hard capacity constraints, and principled redistribution of KV states—that makes the subsequent systems-level discussion legible. Understanding why certain layers can share or shed representational burden is a prerequisite for appreciating why joint optimization across quantization, chunking, and model compression is both necessary and tractable. The second section then scales that insight outward, showing how isolated interventions interact in practice and why comprehensive surveys have become an indispensable genre for mapping a landscape too complex for any single technique to navigate alone.

Together, the sections mark the transition from method-level innovation to infrastructure-level thinking. They should be read as a pair rather than independently: the architectural work supplies the mechanistic grounding, while the systems-level and survey work supplies the integrative frame needed to evaluate trade-offs across the full deployment stack.

##### Architectural and Layer-Level Redesign for KV Cache Reduction

Architectural and layer-level redesign represents a principled approach to reducing the memory burden imposed by the key-value (KV) cache during transformer inference. Rather than pruning or compressing an already-trained model post hoc, the methods reviewed here intervene at the structural level—either by reorganizing how KV states are distributed across layers or by imposing hard capacity constraints on the cache from the outset. Taken together, they form a progression from relatively straightforward layer-consolidation strategies to more elaborate bounded-capacity architectures.

The simpler end of this design spectrum is occupied by the Layer-Condensed KV Cache [arxiv_2405.10637], which addresses a fundamental observation about deep transformer models: as the number of layers grows, the cumulative memory consumed by per-layer KV caches becomes a dominant bottleneck for high-throughput deployment. The core insight of this work is that it is not necessary to compute and store KV representations for every layer independently. Instead, the method proposes computing and caching KVs for only a small subset of layers, allowing the remaining layers to reuse those representations. This condensation directly reduces the total number of KV entries that must be held in memory, yielding up to 26× higher inference throughput compared to standard transformer baselines [arxiv_2405.10637]. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that this gain does not come at the cost of prohibitive quality degradation; competitive performance is retained on both language modeling benchmarks and downstream tasks. An additional practical virtue of the approach is its orthogonality to other memory-saving techniques—because it operates at the layer-allocation level rather than within the attention mechanism itself, it can be combined straightforwardly with complementary methods such as quantization or sparse attention [arxiv_2405.10637]. This composability makes it an attractive building block for inference systems that must satisfy multiple efficiency constraints simultaneously.

Moving toward a more structurally constrained design, the Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) [arxiv_2411.15785] takes a different but complementary angle. Whereas the layer-condensed approach reduces the number of layers that contribute to the cache, the BCT imposes a predefined capacity limit on the KV cache itself, treating that limit as a first-class architectural parameter rather than an afterthought. The BCT is specifically tailored to decode-only transformer architectures and achieves its bounded footprint through dynamic updating of key-value vector sequences within the fixed capacity window [arxiv_2411.15785]. This dynamic update mechanism distinguishes the BCT from simpler truncation or eviction strategies: rather than merely discarding older entries, the model is designed and trained with the expectation that its cache will always remain within the predefined bound, allowing the architecture to adapt its representations accordingly. Experimental results confirm that this design significantly reduces memory usage while preserving inference quality and system throughput [arxiv_2411.15785].

Comparing the two approaches reveals a meaningful methodological distinction. The layer-condensed method [arxiv_2405.10637] achieves memory savings by reducing the spatial extent of the cache across the depth dimension of the network—fewer layers contribute KV entries—while leaving the per-layer cache length unconstrained. The BCT [arxiv_2411.15785], by contrast, bounds the temporal or sequence-length dimension of the cache, ensuring that no matter how long the generated sequence grows, memory consumption remains within a predefined envelope. These two axes of reduction are largely orthogonal, suggesting that a unified architecture incorporating both principles could yield compounded savings. Both works share the broader motivation of making high-throughput LLM inference feasible under real-world memory constraints, and both demonstrate that architectural commitment to efficiency—rather than post-hoc compression—can preserve model quality while delivering substantial resource reductions. Together, they illustrate that the transformer architecture itself remains a productive site of redesign for inference efficiency, with structural choices about layer-level and capacity-level organization having first-order consequences for deployment viability.

##### Systems-Level Joint Optimization and Comprehensive Surveys of KV Cache Strategies

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently at scale has motivated a growing body of work that moves beyond isolated optimization techniques toward holistic, systems-level perspectives on key-value (KV) cache management. Rather than treating quantization, token selection, or compression as independent interventions, researchers have increasingly recognized that these strategies interact in complex ways, and that understanding their joint behavior is essential for practical deployment.

Comprehensive surveys have played an important role in consolidating this fragmented landscape. The survey by [arxiv_2412.19442] provides one of the most structured overviews of KV cache management for LLM acceleration, organizing the field into token-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations. Token-level strategies encompass KV cache selection, budget allocation, merging, quantization, and low-rank decomposition, while system-level considerations address how these techniques interact with hardware constraints and serving infrastructure. This taxonomic framing is valuable precisely because it foregrounds the multi-dimensional nature of the optimization problem: no single technique operates in isolation, and the interactions between them determine real-world performance. Similarly, [arxiv_2508.06297] offers a systematic review of KV cache compression techniques, evaluating selective token strategies, quantization, and attention compression with explicit attention to their trade-offs and application scenarios. Crucially, [arxiv_2508.06297] identifies compatibility issues across different methods as a significant practical challenge, underscoring that combining techniques naively can introduce conflicts that undermine the benefits of each individual approach. Together, these two surveys establish that the field has matured to a point where the interactions among methods, rather than the methods themselves in isolation, constitute the central research frontier.

Empirical systems-level frameworks have begun to operationalize this insight. KV Pareto [arxiv_2512.01953] represents a particularly direct attempt to map the joint optimization landscape, introducing a framework that systematically characterizes the Pareto frontier between total memory consumption and task accuracy across three complementary techniques: KV cache quantization, chunked prefill, and model weight quantization via AWQ. By evaluating multiple LLM architectures—Qwen, Llama, and Mistral—under varying KV quantization schemes including int2, int4, and int8 precisions, as well as different granularities such as per-token, per-tensor, and per-block, [arxiv_2512.01953] reveals that optimal configurations are model-specific and cannot be determined by optimizing each dimension independently. This finding directly corroborates the concern raised by [arxiv_2508.06297] regarding compatibility issues, providing empirical evidence that the interaction effects are not merely theoretical but materially affect deployment outcomes, particularly at the edge where memory budgets are tightly constrained.

Comparing the methodological orientations of these works reveals a productive division of labor. The surveys [arxiv_2412.19442] and [arxiv_2508.06297] provide breadth, cataloguing the diversity of existing approaches and identifying open challenges such as scalability, generalization across model families, and the difficulty of evaluating methods under realistic serving conditions. KV Pareto [arxiv_2512.01953] provides depth, offering a concrete optimization framework that operationalizes the multi-technique perspective advocated by the surveys. Where the surveys identify the need for joint analysis, [arxiv_2512.01953] delivers a methodology for conducting it, demonstrating that Pareto-optimal configurations can be identified systematically rather than through exhaustive manual search.

Taken together, these works argue compellingly that efficient LLM inference cannot be achieved by optimizing KV cache strategies in isolation. The memory bottleneck introduced by the linear growth of KV cache with sequence length [arxiv_2512.01953] demands coordinated solutions, and the field is converging on frameworks—both conceptual [arxiv_2412.19442], [arxiv_2508.06297] and empirical [arxiv_2512.01953]—that treat systems-level joint optimization as the appropriate unit of analysis for long-context, real-world deployment.

### Inference Serving, Speculative Decoding, and Hardware-Software Co-optimization

The acceleration of large language model inference at runtime has attracted substantial research attention, with efforts progressing from relatively straightforward draft-model training strategies through more sophisticated speculative decoding systems and, ultimately, to comprehensive hardware-software co-simulation frameworks. This trajectory from simpler to more complex methods reflects the field's growing recognition that inference efficiency cannot be addressed by any single technique in isolation.

A foundational concern in speculative decoding is the availability and quality of the draft model that proposes candidate tokens for verification by the larger target model. Without a well-aligned draft model, the acceptance rate of speculated tokens remains low, negating the latency benefits of the approach. Addressing this gap, [arxiv_2403.00858] proposes a straightforward training framework for directly aligning a compact draft model to a chat-fine-tuned target LLM. The framework consists of three stages—pretraining, distillation-based dataset generation, and supervised fine-tuning—and produces Llama 2 Chat Drafter 115M, a model representing only 1.64% of the parameter count of Llama 2 Chat 7B. By focusing on alignment to the target's conversational distribution rather than general-purpose language modeling, the approach ensures that speculated tokens are stylistically and semantically consistent with what the larger model would generate, thereby improving acceptance rates without requiring access to proprietary training pipelines. This work establishes a practical baseline: given a capable draft model, speculative decoding can meaningfully reduce the memory-bandwidth bottleneck inherent in autoregressive generation.

However, deploying speculative decoding in a production serving environment introduces challenges that a single, statically trained draft model cannot adequately address. Real inference workloads are heterogeneous—requests vary in complexity, domain, and generation length—and batching multiple requests simultaneously creates contention between the speculation and verification phases. [arxiv_2503.15921] introduces SPIN, a serving system that confronts these limitations through three coordinated innovations. Rather than relying on a single homogeneous small speculative model, SPIN maintains a pool of heterogeneous draft models and employs a learning-based selection algorithm to dynamically assign the most appropriate draft model to each incoming request without requiring prior knowledge of request characteristics. This adaptive selection mechanism directly addresses the mismatch that arises when a single draft model is applied uniformly across requests of varying difficulty. Beyond draft selection, SPIN also introduces mechanisms for robust batch processing and holistic optimization spanning both the speculation and verification phases. Compared to [arxiv_2403.00858], which focuses on the offline problem of producing a high-quality draft model, SPIN operates at the system level and treats draft model selection as an online decision problem, representing a meaningful increase in architectural complexity and runtime adaptability.

Yet even a well-engineered serving system like SPIN operates within hardware constraints that are difficult to reason about analytically. Understanding how scheduling policies, memory management strategies, and hardware configurations interact under realistic workloads requires empirical experimentation at scale, which is costly and time-consuming. [arxiv_2503.08415] addresses this need by introducing TokenSim, a simulation framework designed specifically for exploring the hardware and software design space of LLM inference systems. TokenSim supports extensible modeling of system-level optimizations including scheduling and memory management, and its accuracy is validated against real-world deployments with an error rate below 1%. By enabling rapid, low-cost exploration of diverse configurations, TokenSim complements both the draft-model training perspective of [arxiv_2403.00858] and the runtime serving innovations of [arxiv_2503.15921]: it provides the analytical infrastructure needed to evaluate and compare such approaches across different hardware substrates before committing to deployment.

Taken together, these three works illustrate a coherent progression in the inference acceleration literature. Simple draft model alignment provides the prerequisite token quality; heterogeneous speculative serving systems exploit that quality dynamically across diverse workloads; and hardware-software co-simulation frameworks supply the exploratory tooling necessary to reason about system behavior at scale. Each layer of complexity builds upon and presupposes the contributions of the preceding one, suggesting that continued progress in efficient LLM inference will require tighter integration across all three dimensions.

## Conclusion

The body of work surveyed here reveals a field that has advanced with remarkable speed and breadth. What began as a collection of largely independent techniques—quantization borrowed from classical model compression, pruning adapted from earlier neural network research, caching strategies drawn from systems engineering—has evolved into a rich, interconnected ecosystem in which algorithmic, architectural, and systems-level innovations reinforce one another.

Several themes emerge from this synthesis. First, no single method dominates across all deployment scenarios. Post-training quantization offers low overhead but may sacrifice accuracy at aggressive bit-widths; KV cache eviction reduces memory pressure but requires careful calibration to avoid degrading long-context coherence; speculative decoding accelerates generation but depends on the availability of well-matched draft models. Effective deployment increasingly demands the combination of multiple techniques, a reality reflected in the growing prominence of integrated, systems-level compression frameworks.

Second, the KV cache has crystallized as a defining bottleneck of the autoregressive inference paradigm. The volume of research dedicated to its management—spanning eviction policies, quantization, architectural redesign, and joint optimization—signals that this is not a peripheral concern but a structural feature of how current LLMs generate text. Future architectural departures from dense attention may eventually dissolve this bottleneck, but for the foreseeable deployment horizon it remains central.

Third, the boundary between training-time and inference-time optimization is blurring. Quantization-aware fine-tuning, architecture search informed by inference cost, and distillation pipelines designed around serving constraints all illustrate that efficiency is increasingly a first-class objective during model development, not merely an afterthought applied to a finished model.

Looking forward, the most consequential open challenges lie at the intersection of these themes: developing compression and serving strategies that generalize robustly across model families and hardware targets, establishing evaluation frameworks that capture real-world efficiency trade-offs rather than isolated benchmarks, and ensuring that efficiency gains translate into broader accessibility rather than merely lower costs for existing deployments. The literature reviewed here provides a strong foundation for that work.
