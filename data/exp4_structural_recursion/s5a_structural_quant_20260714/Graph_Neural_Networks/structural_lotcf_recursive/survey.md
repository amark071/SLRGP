## Introduction

Graph-structured data is ubiquitous across science and engineering, appearing in domains as diverse as molecular biology, social network analysis, computer vision, and knowledge representation. Unlike grid-structured data amenable to classical convolutional neural networks, graphs exhibit irregular topology, variable node degrees, and rich relational semantics that demand specialized learning architectures. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged over the past decade as the dominant paradigm for learning over such data, enabling end-to-end differentiable computation that respects the underlying graph structure. Yet the field has grown with remarkable speed and in multiple directions simultaneously, making it increasingly difficult to trace the conceptual lineage from foundational mathematical principles to state-of-the-art applied systems.

This literature review organizes the GNN landscape along a foundational-to-applied axis, beginning with the mathematical and representational underpinnings of the field and progressing through increasingly specialized architectural and theoretical contributions. The review is structured recursively: each section builds upon the concepts established in prior sections, reflecting the genuine dependency structure of the research literature rather than imposing an artificial taxonomy.

The review opens with broad surveys and overviews that situate GNNs within the wider context of representation learning on graphs. It then descends into the spectral signal-processing foundations that gave rise to the first principled graph convolution operators. From there, attention turns to the layer-level architectural components — convolutional layers, pooling layers, and structural design choices — that constitute the building blocks of modern graph convolutional networks. Multi-scale, multi-path, and heterogeneous architectures are examined next, capturing the diversity of design strategies that have been proposed to handle complex graph distributions.

The review then addresses one of the most consequential and technically nuanced problems in GNN design: graph-level pooling. Progressing from basic global aggregation strategies through advanced second-order and attention-based methods, and culminating in hierarchical pooling and theoretically expressive approaches, this section traces how the community has grappled with the challenge of producing fixed-size graph representations from variable-size inputs. The final sections broaden the scope once more, examining how node and edge feature representations can be enriched, how structural and geometric inductive biases can be incorporated, and how alternative propagation frameworks — including sheaf Laplacians and graph kernels — extend or challenge the standard message-passing paradigm.

Together, these threads constitute a comprehensive map of the GNN methodological landscape. By following the foundational-to-applied ordering, this review aims to serve both newcomers seeking a principled entry point and experienced researchers looking to situate their work within the broader intellectual context of the field.

#### Surveys and Overviews of GNN Foundations and Representation Learning

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a foundational paradigm for learning from graph-structured data, and a growing body of survey literature has sought to map the landscape of architectures, tasks, and representation learning strategies that define the field. These overviews collectively serve as entry points for researchers and provide taxonomic frameworks that organize an otherwise rapidly expanding literature.

The most comprehensive and widely cited of these foundational surveys is [arxiv_1901.00596], which situates GNNs within the broader trajectory of deep learning by observing that while methods such as convolutional neural networks have achieved remarkable success on data residing in Euclidean space—including images, video, and speech—a substantial and growing class of real-world problems involves data generated from non-Euclidean domains. In such settings, entities and their relationships are naturally represented as graphs, and the complexity of these structures poses significant challenges for conventional machine learning algorithms. To address this, [arxiv_1901.00596] proposes a taxonomy that partitions the state-of-the-art into four principal categories of GNN architectures, offering a structured vocabulary for understanding how different model families relate to one another. This taxonomic contribution is particularly valuable as an organizing principle for newcomers to the field, as it clarifies the distinctions and connections among recurrent, convolutional, graph autoencoder-based, and spatial-temporal approaches.

Building on this architectural framing, [arxiv_2102.02026] approaches the subject from the perspective of representation learning, emphasizing that the central objective of GNNs is to encode graph-structured knowledge into lower-dimensional representations that preserve as many characteristics of the original data as possible. The paper highlights the growing importance of social and information networks, including large-scale knowledge graphs that capture relationships among billions of entities, as motivating application domains. Like [arxiv_1901.00596], it discusses graph convolutional networks and graph autoencoders, but it also gives explicit attention to spatio-temporal GNNs, reflecting the field's expansion into dynamic graph settings. Where [arxiv_1901.00596] is primarily concerned with providing a comprehensive architectural taxonomy, [arxiv_2102.02026] foregrounds the representational goals that motivate these architectures, framing the learned embeddings as tools for downstream tasks such as node classification, graph classification, and link prediction.

This task-oriented perspective is carried further by [arxiv_2307.00865], which offers a more focused review centered specifically on graph classification and link prediction. The paper opens with a critique of traditional convolutional neural networks, noting their limitation to Euclidean data and their inability to natively handle the graph-structured representations that characterize transportation networks, social networks, and citation networks, among others. The survey identifies the construction of graph convolutional operators and graph pooling operators as the pivotal technical challenge in adapting convolutional approaches to graph data, and it reviews models that incorporate attention mechanisms and autoencoder architectures as solutions to this challenge. Compared to [arxiv_1901.00596] and [arxiv_2102.02026], which aim for breadth across the full spectrum of GNN research, [arxiv_2307.00865] is more selective in scope, drilling into specific task categories and the associated benchmark datasets, making it a useful complement rather than a substitute for the broader surveys.

Taken together, these three works trace a coherent arc from general architectural taxonomy to representation learning principles to task-specific evaluation. They share a common recognition that the transition from Euclidean to non-Euclidean data is the defining challenge that motivates GNN research, and they collectively establish the conceptual vocabulary—message passing, graph convolution, pooling, autoencoding, spatio-temporal modeling—that structures subsequent, more specialized inquiry in the field. For any researcher seeking to orient themselves within the GNN literature, these surveys provide indispensable scaffolding.

#### Spectral Graph Convolution and Signal-Processing Foundations

The mathematical foundations of graph neural networks are deeply rooted in spectral graph theory and graph signal processing (GSP), a lineage that has progressively shaped how convolutional operations are defined, analyzed, and extended on irregular graph-structured data. Understanding this progression from theoretical grounding to applied filter design is essential for appreciating the broader landscape of graph neural network architectures.

A natural starting point is the signal-processing perspective, which frames graph convolution as a generalization of classical convolutional operations to non-Euclidean domains. This framing is articulated systematically in [arxiv_2008.01247], which examines how GSP principles can be leveraged to extend core components of convolutional neural networks—including convolution itself and pooling—to graphs whose topology varies across datasets and domains. The paper argues that conventional deep learning architectures perform poorly when data possess an underlying graph structure, as is common in social and biological networks, and proposes that the design of graph CNN architectures should be explicitly guided by the topological properties of the data graph. This work thus establishes a conceptual bridge between classical signal processing and modern deep learning on graphs, situating spectral convolution within a principled engineering framework.

Building on this spectral foundation, a significant body of work has focused on the specific class of polynomial spectral filters, which approximate graph convolution through truncated polynomial expansions of the graph Laplacian. While computationally tractable and theoretically well-understood, polynomial filters carry inherent limitations. Their frequency responses are constrained by the degree of the polynomial, which restricts their capacity to model complex spectral patterns and renders them potentially sensitive to noise in the graph signal. These limitations motivate the search for richer filter families. Both [arxiv_1901.01343] and [ss_476188] address this gap by proposing graph convolutional layers inspired by the auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) filter, a classical signal-processing construct that combines both moving-average (polynomial) and auto-regressive components. The two sources present substantively identical contributions—a graph neural network implementation of the ARMA filter through a recursive and distributed formulation—and together constitute a coherent theoretical and empirical argument for ARMA-based convolution. Compared to polynomial filters, the ARMA layer is shown to offer a more flexible frequency response, greater robustness to noise, and an enhanced ability to capture global graph structure. Crucially, the recursive formulation ensures that the layer remains localized in node space and can be transferred to new graphs at test time, preserving the inductive properties valued in practical deployments. A spectral analysis reported in these works further illuminates the filtering behavior of the ARMA layer, grounding its empirical advantages in a rigorous theoretical account.

The question of expressive power and the role of multi-scale spectral information is taken up in [arxiv_1906.02174], which provides a theoretical analysis of the limitations imposed on existing graph convolutional networks by their architectural choices and activation functions. The authors generalize spectral graph convolution by casting deep GCN architectures in block Krylov subspace forms, a formulation that unifies polynomial filter design with the capacity for multi-scale feature aggregation. This perspective reveals that standard GCN designs effectively constrain the expressive power of spectral convolution, and that deeper, multi-scale architectures can in principle overcome these ceilings. The Krylov subspace framing is particularly notable because it connects graph convolution to a well-studied class of iterative methods in numerical linear algebra, further enriching the theoretical vocabulary available for analyzing graph neural networks.

Taken together, these works trace a coherent arc from the foundational signal-processing rationale for graph convolution [arxiv_2008.01247], through the design and analysis of expressive spectral filters [arxiv_1901.01343, ss_476188], to theoretical investigations of architectural expressivity and multi-scale spectral representation [arxiv_1906.02174]. Each contribution deepens the mathematical basis upon which convolutional operations on graphs are constructed, and collectively they demonstrate that advances in graph neural network design are inseparable from advances in spectral theory and filter design.

##### Layer-Level Architectural Components: Convolutional, Pooling, and Structural Graph CNN Layers

The architectural design of individual layers within graph convolutional networks represents a foundational concern in the broader project of adapting CNN-style computation to non-Euclidean data. A central challenge is that standard convolutional operations presuppose a regular grid structure, such as the pixel lattice of an image, which graphs do not possess. The papers reviewed here address this challenge at progressively finer levels of specificity, moving from general frameworks for locally connected region extraction, through image-like graph representations, to explicit multi-component layer reformulations and path-integral-based convolution and pooling mechanisms.

The most general contribution in this space is the framework proposed by Niepert and colleagues [arxiv_1605.05273], which establishes a principled method for learning convolutional neural networks over arbitrary graphs, including undirected, directed, and attributed variants. The core insight is that the locally connected receptive fields that make CNNs powerful for images can be approximated on graphs by defining a procedure for extracting and normalizing local neighborhoods around each node. By assembling these neighborhoods into fixed-size, ordered sequences, the approach renders graph-structured data amenable to standard one-dimensional convolutional operations. Evaluated against established graph kernel benchmarks, the learned representations prove competitive while remaining computationally efficient, establishing a template for subsequent layer-level design work.

Where [arxiv_1605.05273] adapts the convolutional metaphor to graphs, [arxiv_1708.02218] pursues a complementary strategy: rather than modifying the CNN architecture to accommodate graphs, it transforms graphs into multi-channel image-like structures so that vanilla two-dimensional CNNs can be applied without modification. This reversal of the usual problem formulation is methodologically distinctive. By encoding graph topology and node attributes into a structured image representation, the method sidesteps the need for bespoke graph convolution operators entirely. Empirically, this approach outperforms state-of-the-art graph kernels and graph CNNs on four of six real-world datasets, with competitive performance elsewhere, and compares favorably in terms of time complexity. Taken together, [arxiv_1605.05273] and [arxiv_1708.02218] illustrate two philosophically different responses to the same structural incompatibility: the former brings CNN operations to graphs, while the latter brings graphs to CNN operations.

A more granular architectural perspective is offered by [arxiv_1811.00052], which identifies a systematic gap in prior graph CNN designs: the predominant focus on vertex features to the neglect of edge features. The paper proposes new formulations for convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers that explicitly incorporate multi-dimensional edge information alongside node information. This tripartite reformulation is notable because it treats the full complement of CNN layer types as objects requiring redesign for the graph setting, rather than focusing solely on the convolutional layer. The resulting architecture achieves improved classification accuracy over prior state-of-the-art methods on benchmark datasets, suggesting that edge-aware layer design constitutes a meaningful and previously underexploited dimension of architectural choice.

The most structurally specific contribution is the path-integral-based framework described in [arxiv_2302.13399], which grounds both convolution and pooling operations in a principled probabilistic formalism. The convolution operation aggregates information over all paths connecting message sender and receiver, with learnable weights that depend on path length, corresponding to a maximal entropy random walk. This yields a transition matrix—the maximal entropy transition matrix—whose diagonal entries relate directly to subgraph centrality, thereby providing a natural and theoretically motivated criterion for pooling. Crucially, [arxiv_2302.13399] extends this framework to heterogeneous graphs, broadening its applicability beyond the homogeneous settings addressed by [arxiv_1605.05273] and [arxiv_1811.00052]. The path-integral formulation thus represents a deepening of the layer-design question: rather than asking how to approximate local receptive fields or encode edge attributes, it asks what mathematical structure should govern information flow across multi-hop neighborhoods, and derives both convolution and pooling as consequences of a single underlying principle.

Collectively, these works trace a trajectory from general mechanisms for locally connected region extraction [arxiv_1605.05273], through structural re-encoding strategies [arxiv_1708.02218] and comprehensive multi-component layer redesign [arxiv_1811.00052], to theoretically grounded path-based formulations [arxiv_2302.13399], reflecting the field's progressive refinement of what it means to instantiate a CNN-style layer on graph-structured data.

##### Multi-scale, Multi-path, and Heterogeneous Graph CNN Architectures

The architectural evolution of graph neural networks has progressed well beyond single-layer, homogeneous designs toward richer frameworks capable of handling typed relationships, multi-path information flow, and dynamically constructed graph topologies. This progression reflects a recognition that real-world relational data rarely conforms to the uniform, fixed-structure assumptions embedded in foundational GNN formulations.

A foundational contribution to heterogeneous graph learning is the framework of Typed Graph Networks [arxiv_1901.07984], which addresses the limitation that standard message-passing architectures treat all nodes and edges as belonging to a single undifferentiated class. By instantiating distinct trainable neural modules for different node and edge types and assembling them in varied configurations, Typed Graph Networks enforce type-aware parameter sharing across the graph. This approach generalizes the weight-tying intuition borrowed from Convolutional Neural Networks on grid topologies to the more expressive setting of typed relational structures, where vertices are projected into a high-dimensional real space and iteratively refined through multiple message-passing steps in an end-to-end differentiable manner [arxiv_1901.07984]. The key insight is that the identity of a node or edge type should modulate how information is aggregated, rather than applying a single universal kernel indiscriminately across all relational instances.

While typed architectures address the heterogeneity problem, a separate but equally pressing challenge concerns the depth of graph networks. Naively stacking multiple graph convolutional layers—by analogy with deep CNNs—introduces well-documented pathologies including vanishing gradients, overfitting, and, most distinctively, over-smoothing, whereby node representations converge to indistinguishable vectors as the number of layers increases [arxiv_2105.01510]. Multipath Graph Convolutional Neural Networks [arxiv_2105.01510] tackle this problem through a structurally different strategy: rather than deepening a single sequential stack, the architecture aggregates the outputs of multiple distinct shallow networks operating in parallel. This multi-path design preserves the expressive diversity that depth is intended to provide while avoiding the degradation effects that accompany it. Empirical evaluations on node property prediction benchmarks demonstrate that the multipath approach achieves higher test accuracy with reduced training overhead compared to single-path deep alternatives [arxiv_2105.01510]. The contrast with Typed Graph Networks is instructive: whereas the latter extends the representational vocabulary of individual message-passing steps by differentiating node and edge types, the multipath framework addresses the macro-level topology of how those steps are composed and combined.

Graph Transformer Networks (GTNs) [arxiv_1911.06455] represent a further architectural escalation that subsumes concerns from both preceding directions. GTNs are explicitly motivated by the inadequacy of existing GNNs when applied to misspecified or heterogeneous graphs—graphs whose fixed connectivity structure may not align with the relational patterns most informative for a given task. The Graph Transformer layer, the core computational unit of GTNs, learns a soft selection over edge types and computes composite relations, thereby generating entirely new graph structures in which previously unconnected nodes may be linked if intermediate paths support such a connection [arxiv_1911.06455]. This dynamic graph construction is performed end-to-end, meaning the network simultaneously learns which structural transformations are useful and how to exploit the resulting topology for node representation. In this respect, GTNs extend the typed-graph intuition of [arxiv_1901.07984] by not merely conditioning computation on pre-specified types but actively synthesizing new relational structure from heterogeneous edge-type distributions. They also implicitly address the over-smoothing concern raised in [arxiv_2105.01510] by allowing the model to selectively route information through task-relevant paths rather than uniformly diffusing signals across all edges.

Taken together, these three lines of work trace a coherent trajectory from type-aware message passing, through multi-path aggregation strategies that mitigate depth-related degradation, to transformer-style architectures that treat graph structure itself as a learnable variable. Each successive framework presupposes the basic layer designs established in earlier GNN literature and extends them to increasingly demanding structural settings, reflecting the broader maturation of the field toward architectures capable of operating on the full complexity of heterogeneous, large-scale relational data.

Despite their shared target, the sections approach this problem from meaningfully different angles, and reading them in the order presented—flat and global methods first, hierarchical and attention-based methods second, theoretically expressive methods third—traces a coherent progression from architectural simplicity toward both structural sophistication and formal rigor.

By grounding the discussion in formal frameworks such as the Weisfeiler-Lehman hierarchy, it provides theoretical vocabulary that applies retroactively to both preceding sections, allowing the reader to evaluate the expressive ceiling of the methods already encountered.

Together, the three sections constitute a layered account of graph pooling: what it does in its simplest form, how it can be enriched structurally, and what theoretical limits ultimately constrain it.

## Flat and Global Pooling Methods

The two sections gathered here share a common architectural commitment: both treat graph-level readout as a single, non-hierarchical compression step that maps a set of node representations directly onto one fixed-size vector. This shared premise distinguishes them from hierarchical pooling approaches, which iteratively coarsen the graph topology, and it justifies reading them as a unified body of work concerned with the expressiveness and design of the readout function itself.

The sections are ordered from foundational to applied. ##### Basic Global Aggregation Pooling: Sum, Mean, Max, and Empirical Comparisons

Global readout operations — mechanisms that aggregate node-level representations into a single graph-level embedding — represent one of the most foundational design decisions in graph neural network (GNN) architectures for graph classification. The simplest and most widely studied of these are flat, non-hierarchical strategies: summing, averaging, or taking the element-wise maximum across all node feature vectors. Despite their apparent simplicity, these operations carry distinct inductive biases. Sum pooling preserves information about graph size and is sensitive to the total magnitude of node activations, making it theoretically expressive under certain conditions. Mean pooling normalizes for graph size and is more robust to variation in node count, while max pooling selects the most prominent feature signal regardless of how many nodes exhibit it. Understanding when each of these strategies succeeds or fails is a prerequisite for evaluating more complex hierarchical alternatives, and a growing body of empirical work has sought to establish these baselines rigorously.

A central contribution to this empirical grounding comes from work that systematically evaluates pooling methods across multiple GNN backbone architectures [arxiv_2004.03519]. This study examines combinations of pooling strategies — including flat global aggregation as well as the hierarchical DiffPool method — with three architectures: GCN, TAGCN, and GraphSAGE. The findings confirm that pooling choice meaningfully affects classification accuracy on standard graph classification benchmarks, and that hierarchical pooling via DiffPool generally improves over simpler alternatives. Crucially, however, the study also reveals that architectural choice interacts with pooling strategy in non-trivial ways: TAGCN, which incorporates multi-hop neighborhood information through polynomial filters, achieves comparable or superior accuracy to GCN and GraphSAGE, particularly on datasets characterized by larger and sparser graph structures. This interaction effect underscores that flat pooling methods cannot be evaluated in isolation from the node-embedding architecture they follow; a simple global sum or mean applied after a more expressive convolutional operator may outperform a hierarchical pooling scheme applied after a weaker one. The work thus situates basic aggregation strategies within a broader empirical landscape, providing practitioners with actionable guidance while also highlighting the difficulty of drawing universal conclusions about pooling in the absence of architectural context.

Complementing this empirical perspective, other work has approached the limitations of flat pooling from a representational standpoint, arguing that mapping a set of node embeddings to a fixed-dimensional vector — whether by sum, mean, or max — necessarily discards structural information about the distribution of those embeddings [arxiv_1905.06259]. This observation motivates a function space pooling approach, in which the set of node representations is mapped not to a vector but to an element of a function space, thereby preserving richer information about the collective geometry of node features. Experimental results reported in that work demonstrate that the proposed method generally outperforms most baseline pooling methods, including standard flat aggregations, and in some cases achieves best-in-class performance on graph classification tasks. Taken together with the findings of [arxiv_2004.03519], this suggests a consistent pattern: flat global pooling provides a competitive and computationally inexpensive baseline, but its representational ceiling is real and measurable. The gap between simple aggregation and more sophisticated readout strategies tends to widen as graph datasets become more structurally complex or as the downstream task demands sensitivity to distributional properties of node features that sum, mean, or max operations inherently collapse.

Collectively, these studies establish the empirical and conceptual foundation upon which more advanced pooling methods are built. They demonstrate that sum, mean, and max pooling are not interchangeable, that their relative performance is conditioned on architectural and dataset characteristics, and that their limitations motivate principled extensions into hierarchical and function-space readout paradigms. For researchers designing GNN systems for graph classification, this body of work makes clear that the choice of global aggregation — even in its simplest form — deserves careful empirical scrutiny rather than treatment as a default implementation detail.

##### Advanced Global Pooling: Second-Order Statistics and Attention-Based Multiset Pooling

Global pooling in graph neural networks presents a fundamental challenge: how to compress a variable-length set of node representations into a single, fixed-size graph-level embedding without losing discriminative information. Simple aggregation strategies such as sum or mean pooling treat all nodes as equally informative and capture only first-order statistics, motivating the development of richer pooling mechanisms. Two complementary lines of work address this limitation by introducing second-order statistical pooling and attention-based multiset pooling, respectively, each progressively increasing the expressive capacity of the readout function.

The first line of work, represented by [ss_477785] and [arxiv_2007.10467], proposes leveraging second-order pooling as the graph-level readout operation. Both papers share the same core argument: whereas conventional global pooling methods aggregate node features through simple summation or averaging, second-order pooling computes covariance-style statistics across the full set of node representations, thereby capturing pairwise feature interactions that first-order methods necessarily discard. The authors argue that this approach naturally handles the two principal difficulties of graph pooling—variable graph sizes and isomorphic graph structures—because the covariance matrix is permutation-invariant and of fixed dimensionality regardless of the number of nodes. Crucially, both works acknowledge that naively applying second-order pooling within a GNN pipeline introduces practical difficulties, including high-dimensional output representations and numerical instability during matrix operations. To address these issues, the authors develop two novel global pooling methods grounded in second-order statistics: a bilinear mapping variant and a related formulation that regularises the covariance structure. By doing so, they retain the statistical richness of second-order information while remaining computationally tractable and compatible with standard gradient-based training. The two papers [ss_477785] and [arxiv_2007.10467] appear to present the same research contribution across a conference and an arXiv venue, respectively, and together provide both the theoretical motivation and the empirical validation for second-order global pooling as a more powerful alternative to mean or sum readouts.

Building on the recognition that simple aggregations are insufficient, [arxiv_2102.11533] approaches the pooling problem from a different but complementary angle, framing it explicitly as a multiset encoding problem. The authors observe that sum and average pooling ignore task-relevant differences among nodes and fail to model structural dependencies between them. They further critique recently proposed hierarchical pooling methods, noting that such approaches can assign identical representations to graphs that are in fact distinguishable by the Weisfeiler-Lehman isomorphism test, thereby discarding information that is recoverable in principle. To overcome both shortcomings simultaneously, [arxiv_2102.11533] introduces the Graph Multiset Transformer, which treats the collection of node representations as a multiset and employs a self-attention mechanism augmented with auxiliary structural information to compute the pooled graph representation. Unlike second-order pooling, which enriches the statistical summary through covariance computation, the multiset transformer enriches it through learned, context-sensitive weighting of nodes, allowing the model to selectively emphasise nodes that are most relevant to the downstream task while also encoding inter-node dependencies.

Taken together, these contributions trace a clear progression from simple to complex pooling strategies. First-order aggregations provide a computationally cheap but statistically impoverished baseline. Second-order pooling [ss_477785, arxiv_2007.10467] extends this by capturing feature covariances across all nodes, adding statistical depth without introducing explicit attention. Attention-based multiset pooling [arxiv_2102.11533] goes further still, incorporating both task-adaptive node weighting and structural context, yielding a readout mechanism that is sensitive to both the content and the relational arrangement of node features. Each step in this progression sacrifices some computational simplicity in exchange for greater representational fidelity, reflecting a broader trend in the field toward pooling operations that are expressive enough to distinguish graphs that simpler methods conflate.

#### Hierarchical and Attention-Based Pooling Methods

Hierarchical and attention-based pooling methods represent a significant strand of research within graph neural networks, addressing the fundamental challenge of generating meaningful graph-level representations from node-level features. The approaches surveyed here progress from relatively straightforward structural coarsening strategies toward increasingly sophisticated mechanisms that integrate attention and multi-scale aggregation.

An early and conceptually grounded contribution to hierarchical pooling is EigenPooling [arxiv_1904.13107], which motivates the need for pooling by observing that globally combining node representations, while common, discards rich structural information embedded in the graph topology. EigenPooling addresses this limitation by leveraging spectral properties of subgraph structures to coarsen the graph in a principled manner. The method draws an explicit analogy to the pooling step in conventional convolutional neural networks, proposing a hierarchical procedure that progressively reduces the graph while preserving structural signals at each level of granularity. This spectral foundation distinguishes EigenPooling from purely feature-driven approaches, as the pooling decisions are informed by the eigendecomposition of local subgraph Laplacians, thereby encoding connectivity patterns that would otherwise be lost in flat aggregation schemes. The work situates itself within a broader recognition that graph classification demands more than node-level representation learning, and that the transition from node representations to a single graph embedding requires careful architectural design.

Building on the general motivation for improved downsampling in graph neural networks, Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool) [arxiv_1904.08082] proposes an alternative paradigm that replaces spectral coarsening with a learned, attention-driven node selection mechanism. Rather than partitioning the graph according to structural criteria derived from eigenvalues, SAGPool employs graph convolution to compute self-attention scores for each node, enabling the pooling operation to jointly consider both node features and graph topology. Nodes with higher attention scores are retained, while others are dropped, producing a coarsened graph that reflects the model's learned assessment of node importance. Compared to EigenPooling [arxiv_1904.13107], SAGPool is more directly integrated into the end-to-end training pipeline, as the attention scores are differentiable and updated through backpropagation. The authors emphasize methodological fairness in their evaluation, controlling for architecture and training procedure across competing pooling methods, which strengthens the empirical credibility of their reported improvements. SAGPool thus represents a step toward pooling methods that are simultaneously topology-aware and feature-sensitive, though it still operates within a single-level selection paradigm at each pooling stage.

The most complex approach among those reviewed is Multi-Level Attention Pooling (MLAP) [arxiv_2103.01488], which explicitly addresses a tension that simpler hierarchical methods do not fully resolve: the trade-off between local and global information in deep graph neural networks. MLAP observes that the message-passing procedure underlying most GNNs propagates information over a limited neighborhood per layer, so that lower layers capture local structure while higher layers capture global structure. Deep models that rely solely on final-layer representations risk losing local information through repeated aggregation, a phenomenon associated with performance degradation. MLAP responds to this challenge by pooling representations from multiple layers simultaneously, using attention mechanisms to weight and unify graph-level summaries across different levels of locality. This multi-scale design contrasts with both EigenPooling [arxiv_1904.13107] and SAGPool [arxiv_1904.08082], which perform pooling at discrete stages without explicitly reconciling representations from different depths of the network. By unifying multiple localities into a single graph representation, MLAP offers a more flexible and expressive framework for graph-level classification, capable of adapting to tasks where the relevant structural scale is not known in advance.

Taken together, these three works trace a clear trajectory from structure-driven coarsening, through attention-based node selection, to multi-level representation unification, reflecting the field's growing recognition that effective graph pooling must balance topological fidelity, feature relevance, and multi-scale expressiveness.

#### Theoretically Expressive and Representation-Power-Oriented Pooling

The question of how much representational power a graph neural network can achieve when producing graph-level embeddings has attracted sustained theoretical attention, with researchers drawing on combinatorial, algebraic, and probabilistic frameworks to establish formal upper and lower bounds on what pooling mechanisms can express. A particularly influential line of inquiry situates the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test as a canonical benchmark against which pooling architectures are measured, asking whether a given design can distinguish graph structures that WL cannot.

A foundational contribution to this programme is Relational Pooling [arxiv_1903.02541], which explicitly frames the problem of graph-level representation in terms of maximal expressive power. The authors observe that conventional GNN pooling strategies, including those grounded in the WL algorithm, graph Laplacians, and diffusion operators, share a common structural limitation: because they aggregate information in a permutation-invariant manner at each layer, they inherit the discriminative ceiling imposed by the WL hierarchy. To transcend this ceiling, [arxiv_1903.02541] draws on the theory of finite partial exchangeability, a probabilistic framework that characterises the conditions under which a function defined over sequences can be treated as equivalent to one defined over sets. By leveraging this theory, Relational Pooling constructs a framework in which node orderings are explicitly considered rather than immediately collapsed, allowing the model to access relational information that permutation-invariant aggregation would otherwise destroy.

A conceptually important consequence of this design is that Relational Pooling can, somewhat counterintuitively, render existing GNN architectures strictly more powerful than the original WL isomorphism test [arxiv_1903.02541]. This is a non-trivial theoretical result: it demonstrates that the expressive bottleneck in standard GNNs lies not in the underlying message-passing computation per se, but in the manner in which node-level representations are pooled into a graph-level summary. By decoupling the representational capacity of the node-update function from the pooling step, [arxiv_1903.02541] shows that architectures not originally designed for graph-structured data—including Recurrent Neural Networks and Convolutional Neural Networks—can be incorporated into a theoretically sound graph classification pipeline. This is significant because it broadens the design space for expressive pooling beyond the family of message-passing networks, suggesting that the choice of aggregation mechanism is at least as consequential as the choice of node-level computation.

The grounding of Relational Pooling in exchangeability theory is methodologically notable because it connects graph representation learning to a well-developed probabilistic literature, providing a principled basis for claims about maximality of representation power rather than relying solely on empirical demonstration [arxiv_1903.02541]. The framework establishes that a function over graphs achieves maximal representational power if and only if it can be expressed as a function over all possible node orderings, with the resulting representations then aggregated in a manner consistent with the exchangeability conditions. This formal characterisation provides a theoretical target against which other pooling designs can be evaluated, and it implies that any pooling method that discards ordering information before aggregation must necessarily sacrifice some discriminative capacity.

Empirically, [arxiv_1903.02541] reports improved performance over state-of-the-art methods across several graph classification benchmarks, lending practical credibility to the theoretical claims. Taken together, the contributions of Relational Pooling establish a foundational reference point for expressiveness-oriented pooling research: they clarify what the theoretical maximum of representational power looks like, identify the structural reasons why standard pooling falls short of that maximum, and propose a concrete mechanism—rooted in exchangeability—for approaching it. Subsequent work in this space must either build on, refine, or argue against the formal framework that [arxiv_1903.02541] puts forward.

### Structural Extensions: Node Features, Graph Geometry, and Beyond Message Passing

## Structural Extensions: Node Features, Graph Geometry, and Beyond Message Passing

The three subsections collected here form a coherent arc that begins with the most immediate representational gaps in vanilla message passing and ends with proposals that question whether message passing is the right computational primitive at all. Reading them in sequence traces a natural escalation of theoretical ambition.

The first subsection establishes the baseline problem: standard GNNs treat edges as passive conduits and assume a fixed topology, leaving relational semantics underspecified. Enriching node and edge feature representations is therefore the most direct corrective move, and it sets the vocabulary—co-learned edge attributes, adaptive adjacency—that later subsections will take for granted.

The second subsection shifts attention from what is represented to how structural and geometric priors shape what a network can express. Degree-aware convolution, multi-relational modeling, and geometric reformulations of aggregation each target a specific failure mode of uniform neighborhood aggregation. These contributions accept the message-passing skeleton but surgically modify its inductive biases, making them a natural bridge between incremental feature enrichment and more radical departures.

The third subsection completes the progression by asking whether the message-passing framework itself is the binding constraint. Sheaf-theoretic Laplacians replace scalar node signals with richer stalks governed by restriction maps, while graph kernel integration imports classical combinatorial expressivity directly into the learning pipeline. Both moves are motivated by the theoretical ceilings identified in the preceding subsections, giving the reader a clear sense of why these alternatives became necessary rather than merely novel.

Together, the three subsections move from representation, through geometry, to foundations—each step made legible by the one before it.

#### Enriching Node and Edge Feature Representations in GNNs

Early work in graph neural networks largely treated node features as the primary objects of learning, with edge information either ignored or encoded only implicitly through adjacency structure. A foundational limitation of this paradigm is that it fails to capture the rich relational semantics that edges themselves may carry, and it assumes that the graph topology is fixed and given rather than something to be adapted or co-learned alongside feature representations. The papers reviewed in this subsection address these limitations through two complementary strategies: the joint co-embedding of node and edge features within a unified message-passing framework, and the dynamic learning of graph structure in conjunction with node feature representations.

The co-embedding approach is developed in [arxiv_2010.13242] and its published counterpart [ss_477653], which together propose a graph neural network architecture that simultaneously learns representations for both nodes and edges rather than treating edges as mere conduits for node-to-node message passing. The authors motivate this design by observing that most existing graph convolutional network (GCN) methods can only embed node features, thereby discarding potentially informative edge attributes that are present in many real-world graphs, from biological interaction networks to social platforms. Their framework constructs an end-to-end learning pipeline in which node and edge embeddings are updated in a mutually dependent fashion: node representations are informed by the features of incident edges, and edge representations are in turn updated based on the features of their endpoint nodes. This bidirectional coupling allows the model to capture higher-order relational structure that would be invisible to node-only embedding schemes. The approach situates itself squarely within the standard message-passing neural network (MPNN) pipeline but enriches the representational vocabulary of that pipeline by elevating edges to first-class citizens of the learning process. Empirical evaluations reported in both [arxiv_2010.13242] and [ss_477653] demonstrate that co-embedding yields consistent improvements over node-only baselines across graph classification and regression benchmarks, underscoring the practical value of representing relational information explicitly.

Where the co-embedding work enriches what is represented within a fixed graph, [arxiv_1909.04931] addresses a complementary question: whether the graph structure itself should be treated as a learnable quantity that evolves alongside node features across the layers of a graph convolutional neural network (GCNN). The authors argue that existing methods, which either manually construct a graph or learn a single fixed graph shared across all layers, are ill-suited to capture the fact that the relevant pairwise relationships among nodes may differ at different levels of abstraction. Their solution is to cast graph learning as a distance metric learning problem, specifically employing a Mahalanobis distance metric to model pairwise feature similarities, and then decomposing the metric matrix into a low-dimensional factor to keep the optimization tractable. Crucially, the graph and the node features are optimized jointly at each layer, so that the topology adapts dynamically to the evolving feature representations rather than remaining static. Experiments on point cloud data and citation networks demonstrate that this dynamic joint learning outperforms approaches that fix the graph structure, highlighting the importance of allowing topology and features to co-evolve.

Taken together, these works represent a progression from foundational enrichment of the MPNN representational scheme to more applied structural adaptation. [arxiv_2010.13242] and [ss_477653] establish the principle that edges deserve their own learned representations within the standard pipeline, while [arxiv_1909.04931] extends the notion of enrichment beyond feature space to include the graph topology itself. Both directions share the underlying insight that the standard GNN assumption of fixed, node-centric representations is unnecessarily restrictive, and that richer, more flexible representations of both relational structure and node attributes lead to more expressive and better-performing models.

#### Structural and Geometric Inductive Biases: Degree, Dimensionality, and Graph Geometry

The structural and geometric inductive biases embedded in graph neural network architectures represent a critical methodological frontier, as standard message-passing frameworks exhibit well-documented limitations in capturing the full complexity of neighborhood topology and long-range relational dependencies. A coherent line of work has emerged that addresses these shortcomings through increasingly targeted interventions: geometric reformulations of aggregation, degree-aware convolution, multi-relational graph modeling, and two-dimensional convolution over both node links and attribute spaces.

A foundational critique of conventional message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) is articulated in Geom-GCN [arxiv_2002.05287], which identifies two structural weaknesses shared across standard aggregators: the loss of positional and structural information about nodes within their neighborhoods, and an inability to capture long-range dependencies in disassortative graphs, where connected nodes tend to belong to different classes. To address both limitations simultaneously, the authors propose a geometric aggregation scheme that grounds graph convolution in a continuous latent space underlying the discrete graph structure. By embedding nodes into this geometric space and defining structural neighborhoods within it, the method preserves relative positional relationships that permutation-invariant aggregators would otherwise discard. The scheme is decomposed into three modules—node embedding, structural neighborhood construction, and bi-level aggregation—enabling the network to exploit geometric proximity as a proxy for structural similarity. This geometric perspective represents a principled departure from purely topological aggregation and directly targets the representational bottlenecks that limit standard MPNNs on heterophilic benchmarks.

Complementing this geometric approach, DEMO-Net [arxiv_1906.02319] addresses a different but related structural limitation: the failure of most graph neural networks to account for node degree when performing convolution. The authors observe that existing methods lack seed-oriented, degree-aware, and order-free properties in a unified framework, and that the degree-specific neighborhood structure of individual nodes is not explicitly encoded during aggregation. Motivated by the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, DEMO-Net introduces degree-specific convolutional filters that distinguish nodes by their local structural context, enabling the model to differentiate neighborhoods that would otherwise be conflated by a shared aggregation function. This degree-aware design provides a theoretically grounded mechanism for structure-sensitive representation learning, addressing a gap that geometric methods like Geom-GCN approach from a spatial rather than combinatorial angle.

Whereas both Geom-GCN and DEMO-Net operate on single-relational graphs, real-world networks frequently encode multiple types of relationships between the same pair of nodes. Multi-dimensional Graph Convolutional Networks [arxiv_1808.06099] extend the convolutional paradigm to multi-dimensional graphs, where each relation type constitutes a distinct dimension. The authors argue that single-dimensional GCN methods fail to exploit the richer inter-dimensional interactions present in such data, and propose a framework that performs convolution across relational dimensions jointly. This multi-relational inductive bias is orthogonal to the geometric and degree-specific strategies discussed above, targeting the expressiveness of the input graph representation rather than the aggregation mechanism per se.

A further methodological extension is proposed in the dimensionwise separable 2-D graph convolution framework (DSGC) [arxiv_1909.12038], which critiques the exclusive reliance of existing GCN variants on one-dimensional convolution operating solely over the object-link graph. By jointly modeling object links and attribute relations through a 2-D convolution, DSGC captures informative relational structure among node features that 1-D methods systematically ignore. The authors demonstrate that this approach reduces intra-class variation in learned representations and is particularly beneficial for noisy and sparse real-world networks. Compared to the multi-dimensional approach of [arxiv_1808.06099], which extends the relational graph structure, DSGC extends the convolution operator itself into the attribute dimension, representing a complementary axis of structural enrichment.

Taken together, these four methods illustrate a methodological progression from geometric re-embedding of neighborhoods [arxiv_2002.05287], through degree-specific structural discrimination [arxiv_1906.02319], to multi-relational [arxiv_1808.06099] and attribute-relational [arxiv_1909.12038] extensions of the convolution operator, each targeting a distinct structural or geometric limitation of standard graph neural network aggregation.

#### Beyond Message Passing: Sheaf Laplacians, Graph Kernels, and Alternative Propagation Frameworks

The standard message-passing neural network (MPNN) paradigm, while powerful, operates within well-documented theoretical constraints. Two complementary research directions have emerged to transcend these limitations: grounding propagation in sheaf-theoretic structures and integrating classical graph kernels directly into the learning process. Together, these approaches reframe what it means for a GNN to "understand" relational structure, moving from heuristic aggregation toward mathematically principled alternatives.

The foundational motivation for both directions stems from the recognised expressivity ceiling of MPNNs. As established in the literature, standard message-passing schemes cannot exceed the discriminative power of the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test [arxiv_2201.00491]. This ceiling is not merely a theoretical curiosity; it has practical consequences for tasks requiring fine-grained structural discrimination, asymmetric relational reasoning, or interpretable feature construction. Addressing this ceiling requires either enriching the algebraic substrate on which diffusion operates or augmenting the aggregation mechanism with richer combinatorial information.

Sheaf Neural Networks [arxiv_2012.06333] pursue the former strategy by replacing the standard graph Laplacian with the sheaf Laplacian, a construction drawn from algebraic topology and sheaf theory. In the classical GCN formulation, the diffusion operator implicitly assumes that the "signal" at each node lives in a common, homogeneous space and that inter-node relations are symmetric and constant. The sheaf Laplacian relaxes all three assumptions simultaneously: it encodes additional relational structure parameterised by the underlying graph, permitting relations between nodes to be non-constant, asymmetric, and varying in dimension [arxiv_2012.06333]. This generalisation is theoretically significant because it provides a proper extension of the diffusion operation rather than a post-hoc modification of aggregation rules. Empirically, the resulting Sheaf Neural Networks outperform graph convolutional networks in domains where node relations are asymmetric and signed [arxiv_2012.06333], suggesting that the expressivity gains are not merely formal but translate into measurable performance improvements on structurally heterogeneous graphs.

Where sheaf theory enriches the propagation operator itself, KerGNNs [arxiv_2201.00491] enrich the information that flows through an otherwise conventional message-passing process by integrating graph kernels directly into it. Graph kernels represent the historically dominant paradigm for graph classification, offering theoretically grounded, interpretable similarity measures over graph structures. Their principal weakness has been reliance on hand-crafted combinatorial features, which limits their representational capacity relative to learned methods [arxiv_2201.00491]. Conversely, MPNNs learn flexible representations but sacrifice interpretability and are bounded by WL expressivity. KerGNNs resolve this tension by drawing inspiration from convolutional filters in CNNs: rather than treating kernel computation and neural message passing as separate pipelines, the framework embeds kernel evaluations within the message-passing process itself, allowing the model to learn richer local structural features while retaining the interpretability associated with kernel methods [arxiv_2201.00491]. This integration directly addresses the dual limitations of both predecessor paradigms.

Comparing the two approaches reveals a productive theoretical complementarity. Sheaf Neural Networks operate at the level of the propagation operator, modifying the mathematical object that governs how information diffuses across the graph; their theoretical grounding lies in topology and linear algebra over stalks. KerGNNs, by contrast, operate at the level of feature construction within the aggregation step, enriching what is communicated rather than how it is diffused; their grounding lies in kernel theory and the classical graph comparison literature. Both frameworks share the conviction that the standard MPNN/WL ceiling is a structural artefact of overly restrictive mathematical assumptions, and both demonstrate that relaxing those assumptions—whether through sheaf-theoretic diffusion [arxiv_2012.06333] or kernel-augmented aggregation [arxiv_2201.00491]—yields models with strictly greater expressive and practical capacity. Taken together, they illustrate that progress beyond the WL frontier is achievable through multiple, mathematically distinct routes, each offering different trade-offs between theoretical elegance, interpretability, and empirical performance.

## Conclusion

This review has traced the development of Graph Neural Networks from their mathematical roots in spectral graph theory and signal processing through to the sophisticated architectural and theoretical innovations that define the current frontier of the field. Several overarching themes emerge from this traversal.

First, the field has been shaped by a productive tension between expressiveness and efficiency. Spectral methods offered theoretical elegance but computational cost; spatial and message-passing methods traded some theoretical grounding for scalability. This tension recurs at every level of the architectural hierarchy, from individual convolutional layers to global pooling strategies, and continues to motivate new work.

Second, the problem of pooling — how to aggregate node-level representations into graph-level summaries — has proven far richer than early sum or mean baselines suggested. The progression from simple global aggregation through second-order statistics, attention mechanisms, and hierarchical methods reflects a deepening understanding of what information must be preserved for downstream tasks, and how the choice of pooling interacts with the expressive power of the overall architecture.

Third, the incorporation of structural and geometric inductive biases represents a maturing of the field beyond purely data-driven approaches. Recognizing that graphs carry implicit geometric and topological information — in node degrees, dimensionality, curvature, and connectivity patterns — has enabled architectures that generalize more reliably across diverse graph distributions.

Finally, the emergence of alternative propagation frameworks, including sheaf-theoretic and kernel-based approaches, signals that the message-passing paradigm, while powerful, is not the terminus of GNN research. These alternatives open new theoretical vistas and may prove essential for graphs where standard homophily assumptions break down.

Future work will likely continue to push along all of these axes simultaneously, seeking architectures that are expressive, scalable, geometrically informed, and theoretically well-understood. This review provides a structured foundation from which such advances can be contextualized and evaluated.
