## Introduction

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in artificial intelligence research over the past several years. By bridging the long-standing divide between visual perception and linguistic reasoning, these systems have fundamentally expanded what machine intelligence can perceive, represent, and communicate. Where early natural language processing models operated exclusively within the symbolic domain of text, and early computer vision systems remained confined to pixel-level pattern recognition, modern MLLMs integrate these modalities into unified architectures capable of understanding images, generating descriptions, answering visual questions, following complex instructions, and reasoning across heterogeneous inputs with remarkable fluency.

This literature review traces the intellectual lineage and technical evolution of multimodal large language models from their foundational underpinnings to their most applied instantiations. The review is organized along a foundational-to-applied axis, reflecting the layered nature of progress in this field: advances at each level of abstraction have enabled and shaped the possibilities at the next. We begin with the theoretical and representational foundations of the field, examining how cross-modal grounding, semantic alignment, and joint embedding spaces provide the conceptual substrate upon which all subsequent architectures depend. From there, we move through the pretraining paradigms and architectural innovations that gave rise to vision-language and omni-modal models, before considering the scaling dynamics that transformed specialized multimodal systems into general-purpose foundation models.

The review then turns to the practical and methodological dimensions of building capable MLLMs, including the construction and curation of instruction-tuning datasets, the structural design choices that govern cross-modal alignment in deployed visual language models, and the growing body of work on efficient adaptation, lightweight architectures, and knowledge distillation that makes these systems accessible beyond large-scale compute environments. We subsequently examine how these general capabilities have been refined and redirected toward domain-specific and task-specific applications, where instruction tuning has proven a powerful lever for specialization.

The final sections of the review address the broader ecosystem of evaluation and understanding that has developed alongside these models. We survey the benchmarks and assessment frameworks that the community has constructed to measure multimodal competence, as well as emerging work on interpretability and in-context learning that seeks to illuminate the internal mechanisms driving model behavior. Taken together, these ten thematic areas encompass a total of thirty-two documents and collectively map the current state of a rapidly maturing field.

By organizing this review from foundational principles to applied systems, we aim to provide readers with both a coherent narrative of how the field arrived at its present state and a structured reference for navigating its considerable breadth. The goal is not exhaustiveness but synthesis: to identify the key ideas, recurring tensions, and open questions that define multimodal large language model research today.

## Foundations: Multimodal Pretraining, Architectures, and Representation Learning

Each section isolates a distinct methodological layer of that problem, and together they trace a coherent arc from theoretical grounding through architectural design to large-scale realization.

The progression from grounding theory to architectural method to scaled foundation models reflects the actual intellectual dependencies in the field and provides the structural vocabulary needed to evaluate the downstream capabilities and limitations discussed elsewhere in this review.

#### Representation Learning: Grounding, Alignment, and Cross-Modal Semantics

The theoretical question of what multimodal pretraining actually learns—and whether it produces genuinely grounded, semantically rich representations—sits at the heart of research into multimodal large language models. A productive way to approach this body of work is to move from foundational concerns about grounding and its limits toward the broader structural and architectural questions that have shaped how cross-modal representations are understood and built.

A natural starting point is the grounding problem itself. Language models trained exclusively on text have long been criticized for learning statistical associations among symbols without connecting those symbols to perceptual reality. Vision-and-language (VL) pretraining was proposed as a principled remedy: by jointly training on image or video data alongside text, models might acquire representations that are anchored in the physical world rather than derived purely from linguistic co-occurrence. Yet the empirical evidence for this intuition is more ambiguous than the theoretical motivation suggests. [arxiv_2109.10246] directly interrogates this assumption by comparing the internal linguistic representations of VL-pretrained models against their text-only counterparts, employing a suite of analyses including clustering, probing classifiers, and performance on commonsense question answering tasks, all evaluated in a language-only setting. The findings are sobering: multimodal models fail to significantly outperform text-only variants on these measures, indicating that the benefits of VL pretraining do not straightforwardly transfer into improved lexical grounding within the linguistic modality itself. This result is theoretically important because it decouples task-level performance—where VL models do show gains on benchmarks such as visual question answering—from representational quality at the level of word meaning. The implication is that a model can succeed at multimodal tasks through mechanisms that do not require the kind of deep semantic grounding that critics of text-only learning have demanded, and it signals that future architectural and training innovations must be more deliberately designed if genuine grounding is the goal.

Moving from this specific diagnostic concern to a broader structural perspective, the evolution of multimodal representation learning as a field reveals how researchers have progressively developed more sophisticated frameworks for capturing cross-modal interaction. [arxiv_2302.00389] provides a comprehensive survey of this trajectory, tracing the development of deep learning architectures—particularly transformer-based models—that operate across textual, visual, and audio modalities. The survey emphasizes that cross-modal interaction and the exploitation of complementary information across modalities are not incidental features of these systems but are theoretically central to their capacity to perform tasks such as visual question answering, natural language visual reasoning, and vision-language retrieval. The diversity of architectural variants catalogued in [arxiv_2302.00389] reflects an ongoing theoretical negotiation over how modalities should be fused, whether through early fusion at the input level, late fusion at the decision level, or intermediate cross-attention mechanisms that allow representations from one modality to condition those of another.

Taken together, these two contributions illuminate a productive tension in the field. On one hand, [arxiv_2109.10246] demonstrates that the mere fact of multimodal training does not guarantee that representations become grounded in any theoretically meaningful sense; the linguistic component of a VL model may remain largely insulated from the visual signal at the level of semantic structure. On the other hand, [arxiv_2302.00389] documents the remarkable empirical success of multimodal architectures across a wide range of tasks, suggesting that cross-modal alignment—even if it does not produce grounding in the strict philosophical sense—yields representations that are functionally powerful. The gap between these two observations points toward an important open question: what theoretical account of representation learning would explain why cross-modal pretraining improves task performance without necessarily improving the internal semantic structure of linguistic representations? Answering this question will require both finer-grained probing methodologies and more explicit theoretical frameworks for distinguishing alignment, grounding, and task-relevant feature extraction as distinct objectives within multimodal learning.

#### Pretraining Architectures and Objectives: Vision-Language and Omni-Modal Models

The design of pretraining architectures and objectives for multimodal large language models spans a broad methodological continuum, ranging from relatively constrained dual-encoder systems that align image and text representations in a shared embedding space to more elaborate encoder-decoder frameworks capable of conditional generation, and ultimately to omni-modal systems that incorporate audio alongside vision and language. Understanding this progression requires situating individual architectural choices within the wider landscape of vision-language pre-training (VLP), before examining how specific design decisions—concerning encoders, decoders, alignment objectives, and dataset construction—differentiate one family of models from another.

At the most general level, VLP methods can be organized according to the downstream task families they are designed to serve. [arxiv_2210.09263] provides a comprehensive taxonomy that partitions the field into three broad categories: VLP for image-text tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and image-text retrieval; VLP for core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation; and VLP for video-text tasks such as video captioning and video question answering. This tripartite organization is useful because it foregrounds the fact that architectural choices are not made in a vacuum—the pretraining objective and the modality pairing are jointly constrained by the intended application domain. Models optimized for retrieval, for instance, tend to favor dual-encoder contrastive objectives that produce comparable representations for paired image and text inputs, whereas models targeting generation tasks require autoregressive or sequence-to-sequence decoder components that can produce fluent natural language conditioned on visual context [arxiv_2210.09263].

A complementary perspective is offered by [arxiv_2306.07198], which surveys VLP through the lens of multimodal machine translation. This framing is instructive because translation is a generation task that demands fine-grained cross-modal grounding rather than coarse-grained alignment, thereby placing pressure on architectural components that many contrastive VLP models underspecify. The survey observes that while models such as CLIP have demonstrated strong performance on retrieval and classification benchmarks, their dual-encoder design provides limited support for tasks requiring the model to condition text generation on detailed visual content. This observation motivates encoder-decoder architectures in which a visual encoder feeds into a language decoder, enabling the model to produce outputs that are sensitive to specific visual regions or temporal segments [arxiv_2306.07198]. The contrast between dual-encoder and encoder-decoder designs thus maps onto a fundamental tension between representational efficiency and generative expressiveness.

Moving from the bimodal to the trimodal setting, [arxiv_2304.08345] and [ss_478472] introduce the Vision-Audio-Language Omni-peRception (VALOR) pretraining model, which extends the encoder-decoder paradigm to jointly model vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end framework. VALOR employs three separate unimodal encoders—one for each modality—feeding into a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation. This architectural decision preserves modality-specific representation learning while enabling cross-modal fusion at the generation stage. The model is pretrained with two complementary objectives: Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA), which projects all three modalities into a common embedding space and simultaneously builds vision-language, audio-language, and audiovisual-language alignment; and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC), which trains the decoder to generate text conditioned on vision, audio, or their combination [arxiv_2304.08345][ss_478472]. The dual-objective design is notable because MGA and MGC address different aspects of cross-modal learning—discriminative alignment and generative conditioning, respectively—mirroring the broader field-level tension identified in bimodal VLP between contrastive and generative pretraining strategies.

Taken together, these works illustrate a clear methodological trajectory: from unimodal pretraining adapted to bimodal tasks, through purpose-built dual-encoder and encoder-decoder VLP architectures, to omni-modal systems that scale both the number of modalities and the complexity of pretraining objectives. Each step in this progression introduces new design choices regarding how modality-specific encoders are coupled, how alignment is operationalized across heterogeneous input spaces, and how generation objectives are formulated to exploit the richer multimodal context available when audio is included alongside vision and language.

#### Scaling to General-Purpose Multimodal Foundation Models and LLM Architectures

The trajectory from narrow, task-specific pretrained models toward large-scale, general-purpose multimodal systems represents one of the most consequential shifts in contemporary artificial intelligence research. Understanding this transition requires examining both the architectural foundations that make such systems possible and the scaling principles that determine their capabilities.

A pivotal contribution to this trajectory is PaLI (Pathways Language and Image model), which demonstrates that the joint scaling of vision and language components is essential for achieving strong performance across a broad range of multimodal tasks [arxiv_2209.06794]. Rather than treating visual and linguistic processing as separate pipelines that are loosely coupled at inference time, PaLI integrates large pre-trained encoder-decoder language models with Vision Transformers (ViTs), capitalizing on the existing capabilities of each component while enabling them to be scaled together. Notably, the authors identify an asymmetry in prior work: Transformer architectures for language had grown considerably larger than their vision counterparts, motivating the training of a 4-billion parameter ViT (ViT-e) to quantify the benefits of increased visual capacity [arxiv_2209.06794]. By generating text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs through a unified interface, PaLI is able to address vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages within a single model, exemplifying the general-purpose design philosophy that distinguishes this generation of systems from earlier specialist architectures.

The broader landscape within which PaLI sits is systematically mapped by a comprehensive survey that traces the evolution of multimodal foundation models from specialist systems to general-purpose assistants [arxiv_2309.10020]. This survey organizes the field into two broad classes: well-established research areas, including vision backbone pretraining for visual understanding and text-to-image generation, and more exploratory open research areas, encompassing unified vision models inspired by LLMs, end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs, and the chaining of multimodal tools with LLMs [arxiv_2309.10020]. This taxonomy is valuable precisely because it situates models like PaLI within a continuum, clarifying how the field has progressively moved away from purpose-built models toward systems designed to serve as flexible, instruction-following assistants capable of handling diverse tasks without retraining.

The architectural underpinnings of this shift are examined in detail by a survey of LLM architectures, which contextualizes multimodal large language models (MLLMs) as an advanced subset of the broader LLM family [arxiv_2412.03220]. This work highlights that contemporary LLMs, built on transformer blocks and trained on vast datasets with billions to trillions of parameters, have become multi-functional systems capable of text generation, translation, question answering, and code analysis. MLLMs extend these capabilities by incorporating additional modalities—images, audio, and video—thereby substantially expanding the scope of tasks that a single model can address [arxiv_2412.03220]. The survey's attention to benchmarks and challenges provides a useful complement to the more capability-focused framing found in PaLI and the multimodal foundation model survey, grounding architectural trends in empirical evaluation.

Instruction tuning emerges as a particularly important mechanism for operationalizing general-purpose multimodal capabilities in practice. A tutorial-oriented treatment of multimodal large models emphasizes that instruction tuning strategies are central to optimizing performance for specific tasks, and that the field has expanded well beyond vision and language to encompass sensors, audio, and other modalities [arxiv_2410.05608]. This work also underscores the importance of multimodal datasets and the evolution of pretraining paradigms, framing the current state of the field as the product of cumulative advances in data, architecture, and fine-tuning methodology [arxiv_2410.05608]. Taken together, these contributions collectively illustrate how the field has moved from isolated specialist models toward integrated, scalable, and instruction-responsive multimodal foundation models, with joint scaling, architectural innovation, and tuning strategies each playing indispensable roles in enabling this transition.

##### Instruction-Tuning Data Construction: Generation, Scaling, and Quality

The construction of high-quality instruction-tuning data for multimodal large language models has evolved through a series of increasingly sophisticated methodologies, progressing from foundational generation strategies to large-scale expansion and, ultimately, to self-correcting iterative paradigms.

The seminal contribution in this space is LLaVA, which introduced the first systematic attempt to leverage a language-only GPT-4 model for generating multimodal instruction-following data [arxiv_2304.08485]. The core insight of this approach was that rich textual descriptions of images—such as captions and bounding box annotations—could serve as a proxy for visual content, enabling a text-only model to produce diverse and contextually grounded instruction-response pairs. By training an end-to-end multimodal model on this synthetically generated data, LLaVA demonstrated that machine-generated instruction data could meaningfully transfer conversational and reasoning capabilities to the vision-language domain, achieving 85.1% of GPT-4's relative score on a synthetic multimodal benchmark and strong performance on Science QA [arxiv_2304.08485]. This work established the foundational pipeline: annotate images with symbolic metadata, prompt a powerful language model to generate instruction pairs, and fine-tune a multimodal architecture on the resulting corpus.

While LLaVA demonstrated the viability of this generation paradigm, its dataset remained relatively modest in scale, leaving the upper limits of multimodal capability largely unexplored. SVIT directly addressed this limitation by scaling the construction process to 4.2 million visual instruction-tuning examples, encompassing 1.6 million conversational question-answer pairs, 1.6 million complex reasoning pairs, 1.0 million referring question-answer pairs, and 106,000 detailed image descriptions [arxiv_2307.04087]. Like LLaVA, SVIT relies on GPT-4 prompted with manual image annotations to generate data, preserving the core generation strategy while dramatically expanding both volume and diversity. The authors argue that existing multimodal models are constrained not merely by architectural choices but by the scarcity of high-quality tuning data, and that scaling along dimensions of quantity, quality, and task diversity simultaneously is necessary to push model capabilities further [arxiv_2307.04087]. SVIT thus represents a quantitative scaling of the LLaVA paradigm rather than a departure from it, reinforcing the value of GPT-4-driven generation while demonstrating that richer and more varied prompting of the same underlying model can yield substantially broader coverage.

However, both LLaVA and SVIT share a structural limitation: by relying on language-only GPT-4 conditioned on textual image surrogates, they are inherently constrained in their ability to capture fine-grained visual details that resist verbalization. VIGC identifies this as a fundamental weakness of the prevailing paradigm and proposes a qualitatively different approach [arxiv_2308.12714]. Rather than delegating generation entirely to a text-only oracle, VIGC employs an available multimodal large language model to generate instruction-tuning data directly from images. The challenge this introduces is that existing MLLMs, while capable of processing visual inputs, are prone to producing inadequate responses and hallucinated content—a problem that text-only GPT-4, operating on curated annotations, largely avoids. To address this, VIGC introduces an iterative generation-and-correction mechanism in which the model's outputs are progressively refined, mitigating the tendency toward false information and improving response quality over successive iterations [arxiv_2308.12714]. This represents a methodological shift from a single-pass, annotation-dependent generation strategy toward a closed-loop, self-improving data construction process.

Taken together, these three works trace a coherent trajectory in multimodal instruction-tuning data construction. LLaVA established the foundational method of GPT-4-based generation from symbolic image annotations [arxiv_2304.08485]; SVIT demonstrated that this method could be scaled substantially in volume and diversity without sacrificing quality [arxiv_2307.04087]; and VIGC challenged the reliance on text-only generation by introducing a multimodal, iterative correction paradigm capable of leveraging visual inputs directly while compensating for the imperfections of current MLLMs [arxiv_2308.12714]. Each successive approach addresses residual limitations of its predecessor, reflecting the field's growing recognition that both scale and fidelity to visual content are essential properties of effective instruction-tuning datasets.

##### Core Visual LLM Architectures: Cross-Modal Alignment and Structural Design

The architectural design of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has evolved rapidly, with researchers approaching the problem at multiple levels of abstraction—from foundational design principles to fine-grained alignment mechanisms and parameter-efficient adaptation strategies. Understanding this progression requires moving from general frameworks to increasingly specialized structural innovations.

At the most general level, [arxiv_2306.14895] provides a comprehensive orientation to the field through tutorial notes accompanying the CVPR 2023 presentation on building and surpassing multimodal GPT-4. The work situates MLLMs within the broader trajectory of GPT-like large models for vision-and-language tasks, establishing instruction tuning as the central methodological paradigm for extending language model capabilities into the multimodal domain. Crucially, [arxiv_2306.14895] frames the construction of a minimum viable multimodal GPT-4-like prototype as an achievable goal using open-source resources, thereby democratizing access to the architectural principles underlying such systems. This tutorial-level treatment serves as an important conceptual anchor, identifying the core challenge as one of cross-modal alignment: how to bridge the representational gap between visual inputs and the text-oriented processing machinery of large language models (LLMs).

Building on this general framing, a more specific architectural concern emerges around the granularity of visual representations fed into the language model. Standard visual instruction tuning approaches, as noted by [arxiv_2308.13437], rely exclusively on image-level features aligned with language through instruction data, leaving a significant gap in fine-grained cross-modal correspondence. To address this limitation, [arxiv_2308.13437] introduces Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT), which augments the conventional single-encoder pipeline by integrating an additional region-level vision encoder alongside the standard image-level encoder. This dual-encoder design enables the model to ground language not merely at the holistic image level but at the level of specific spatial regions, promoting more detailed visual comprehension. Complementing the architectural innovation, [arxiv_2308.13437] also develops multiple data generation strategies to construct an image-region-language instruction dataset, recognizing that architectural capacity for fine-grained alignment is only realizable when paired with appropriately structured training supervision. In this respect, PVIT represents a targeted refinement of the general instruction-tuning paradigm described in [arxiv_2306.14895], moving from coarse image-language alignment toward spatially grounded, region-aware cross-modal correspondence.

A distinct but equally important architectural challenge concerns the preservation of pre-existing NLP capabilities during multimodal training. The dominant practice of unfreezing the language model backbone during vision-language fine-tuning, while effective for acquiring multimodal competencies, frequently degrades the model's natural language processing performance—a phenomenon identified as a central motivation in [arxiv_2408.12902]. The Inner-Adaptor Architecture (IAA) proposed in [arxiv_2408.12902] addresses this tension by keeping the language model entirely frozen and instead inserting multiple multimodal adaptors at varying depths within the transformer stack. Unlike approaches that interact with the LLM only at its input boundary, IAA enables direct interaction between visual information and the inherently text-oriented transformer layers at multiple levels of representation, facilitating richer cross-modal integration without modifying the underlying language model weights. This stands in instructive contrast to the approach implied by [arxiv_2306.14895], where the prototype construction does not foreground the NLP degradation problem, and to [arxiv_2308.13437], which focuses on encoder-side enrichment rather than on how visual signals propagate through the frozen language model's depth. The IAA design thus addresses a complementary failure mode: whereas PVIT enriches the visual signal before it enters the LLM, IAA restructures how that signal is absorbed across the LLM's internal layers while protecting its linguistic competence.

Taken together, these three works trace a coherent architectural trajectory: from general principles of multimodal instruction tuning [arxiv_2306.14895], through encoder-level innovations for spatially fine-grained alignment [arxiv_2308.13437], to depth-wise adaptor designs that reconcile multimodal learning with the preservation of language model integrity [arxiv_2408.12902]. Each contribution refines a different structural bottleneck in the cross-modal alignment pipeline.

#### Efficient Adaptation, Lightweight Architectures, and Knowledge Distillation

Efforts to reduce the computational burden of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) while preserving their language capabilities have produced a coherent body of work spanning parameter-efficient adapters, frozen language model strategies, and structured knowledge distillation pipelines. Examining these contributions in order of increasing methodological complexity reveals a clear progression from targeted architectural interventions to multi-stage training regimes designed to transfer rich representational knowledge across model scales.

The most architecturally straightforward approach involves inserting lightweight adapter modules between a vision encoder and a frozen large language model, thereby avoiding the prohibitive cost of retraining the language backbone entirely. CROME exemplifies this strategy by introducing a gated cross-modal adapter that fuses visual and textual representations before they are passed into a frozen LLM [arxiv_2408.06610]. Because only the adapter parameters are updated during training, the total number of trainable weights remains small, yet the gating mechanism provides a principled means of controlling how much visual information is injected at each forward pass. The authors report that this design achieves strong zero-shot performance on visual question answering and instruction-following benchmarks while also supporting task-specific fine-tuning with competitive results [arxiv_2408.06610]. The frozen-LLM constraint is central to the efficiency argument: by treating the language model as a fixed feature extractor, CROME sidesteps catastrophic forgetting and reduces memory requirements substantially compared with full-model fine-tuning approaches.

Veagle pursues a related but slightly more elaborate direction by focusing on the quality of multimodal representation learning itself rather than solely on adapter design [arxiv_2403.08773]. The work acknowledges that existing vision-language models frequently struggle to accurately interpret images in the context of open-ended questions, a limitation that lightweight adapters alone may not fully resolve. Veagle therefore proposes enhancements to how visual and textual features are aligned and integrated, aiming to improve the fidelity of cross-modal understanding across tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and visual grounding [arxiv_2403.08773]. Compared with CROME, Veagle places greater emphasis on representation quality as a prerequisite for efficient adaptation, suggesting that the two approaches are complementary: one optimises the adapter mechanism, the other the representational substrate on which that mechanism operates.

Moving to a higher level of methodological complexity, knowledge distillation frameworks address the scenario in which a large-scale MLLM already exists and the goal is to compress its capabilities into a smaller model without retraining from scratch. LLaVA-KD formalises this objective through two distillation objectives and a three-stage training scheme [arxiv_2410.16236]. The first objective, Multimodal Distillation (MDist), transfers the teacher model's representations across both visual and linguistic modalities, ensuring that the student small-scale MLLM inherits robust cross-modal features. The second objective, Relation Distillation (RDist), specifically targets the teacher's ability to capture relationships among visual tokens, a capability that is easily lost when model capacity is reduced [arxiv_2410.16236]. The three-stage training scheme is designed to exploit these distillation signals progressively, first establishing foundational alignment before refining finer-grained relational knowledge. This staged approach is notably more complex than the single-phase adapter training employed by CROME [arxiv_2408.06610], but it addresses a different problem: rather than adapting a frozen large model efficiently, LLaVA-KD seeks to endow a genuinely smaller model with performance approaching that of its larger teacher [arxiv_2410.16236].

Taken together, these works illustrate a spectrum of solutions to the efficiency challenge in MLLMs. Adapter-based methods such as CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] and representation-focused approaches such as Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] operate within the constraint of a fixed or lightly modified LLM, prioritising training efficiency and adaptability. Knowledge distillation frameworks such as LLaVA-KD [arxiv_2410.16236] accept greater training complexity in exchange for genuinely compact deployable models. Rigorous evaluation across standardised benchmarks remains essential for comparing these strategies fairly, as the diversity of tasks spanning perception, reasoning, and domain-specific capabilities means that gains on one axis do not automatically generalise to others [arxiv_2408.08632].

#### Domain-Specific and Task-Specific Instruction Tuning Applications

Instruction tuning has emerged as a powerful mechanism for adapting multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to specialized domains and tasks, moving beyond general-purpose visual question answering toward applications that demand fine-grained, domain-aware, or modality-specific competencies. The papers reviewed here trace a trajectory from broad methodological innovations in domain adaptation to increasingly targeted deployments, illustrating both the versatility and the remaining limitations of instruction-tuned MLLMs.

A foundational challenge motivating much of this work is that standard visually-tuned models, despite their general image-understanding capabilities, frequently fail to process textual content embedded within images. TRINS [arxiv_2406.06730] addresses this gap directly by constructing a large-scale Text-Rich image INStruction dataset built upon LAION through hybrid machine-assisted and human-assisted annotation. Comprising 39,153 text-rich images and over 102,000 questions, TRINS is notable for the density of its annotations—words per annotation substantially exceed those of comparable datasets—thereby posing harder reading comprehension challenges. Alongside the dataset, the authors introduce a Language-vision Reading Assistant (LaRA) architecture designed to exploit this richer textual signal. The work demonstrates that targeted data construction, rather than architectural novelty alone, can meaningfully improve an MLLM's ability to read and reason about text embedded in natural images, a capability increasingly important as documents, signage, and infographics proliferate in real-world deployments.

Moving from general text-rich imagery to a highly specialized industrial domain, [arxiv_2409.07463] presents a framework for analyzing electron microscopy images in semiconductor manufacturing. The central methodological contribution is a teacher-student pipeline in which a large pretrained model such as GPT-4 generates instruction-following data for zero-shot visual question answering and classification tasks, which are then used to fine-tune smaller multimodal models (SMMs). This approach merges knowledge engineering with machine learning, effectively distilling domain expertise from a large, general model into a compact, deployable assistant without requiring extensive human labeling. Compared with TRINS [arxiv_2406.06730], which relies on hybrid human-machine annotation to build a general reading corpus, [arxiv_2409.07463] prioritizes security, cost-efficiency, and customizability within a closed industrial setting—concerns that are less salient in open academic benchmarks but critical in semiconductor manufacturing contexts where proprietary data cannot be shared with external APIs at scale.

A complementary perspective on domain specialization is offered by [arxiv_2402.08360], which proposes Visual Question Answering Instruction (VQA-IN), a method for converting heterogeneous domain-specific visual and vision-language datasets into a unified question-answering format. By reformulating tasks such as detection, segmentation, and classification as structured QA pairs, VQA-IN enables smaller LLM variants (sLLMs) to be trained across multiple MLLM architectures while preserving performance on domain-specific visual tasks. Where [arxiv_2409.07463] focuses on a single industrial domain and leverages a large teacher model for data generation, [arxiv_2402.08360] pursues a more generalizable unification strategy applicable across diverse domain datasets. Together, these two works suggest that the bottleneck for domain-specific instruction tuning lies less in model architecture than in the principled construction and formatting of training data.

Finally, [arxiv_2406.15768] addresses a structural limitation shared by all the above approaches: MLLMs typically excel at high-level image-text interpretation but struggle with fine-grained visual perception such as detection and segmentation. The Mutually Reinforced MLLM (MR-MLLM) proposes a shared query fusion mechanism that harmonizes detailed visual inputs from dedicated perception models with the linguistic depth of language models, enabling the two modalities to reinforce each other bidirectionally. This mutual reinforcement framing distinguishes MR-MLLM from the other works reviewed, which treat instruction tuning primarily as a data-curation or format-conversion problem. By instead modifying the interaction between perception and comprehension modules at the architectural level, [arxiv_2406.15768] points toward a more integrated solution to the fine-grained understanding deficits that domain-specific applications—whether text-rich image reading [arxiv_2406.06730], microscopy analysis [arxiv_2409.07463], or multi-task visual reasoning [arxiv_2402.08360]—consistently expose.

#### Surveys, Benchmarks, and Broad Overviews of MLLMs

The rapid proliferation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has generated a corresponding body of survey literature aimed at systematically cataloguing architectural innovations, training methodologies, and evaluation practices across this fast-moving field. At the broadest level, such surveys serve a dual purpose: they orient newcomers to the conceptual landscape while providing experienced researchers with structured comparisons of design choices that might otherwise remain scattered across hundreds of individual papers.

One of the most comprehensive contributions to this genre is the survey by [arxiv_2402.12451], which frames the emergence of MLLMs as nothing less than a revolution in generative intelligence, driven by the foundational success of large language models and extended through the integration of visual modalities. The authors argue that the capacity to seamlessly connect text and visual information, while simultaneously supporting dialogue-based interaction and instruction-following behaviour, represents a qualitative shift in what machine learning systems can accomplish. Their review is notable for the breadth of its analytical scope: rather than restricting attention to a single capability or model family, it examines architectural choices, multimodal alignment strategies, and training techniques in a unified framework. This tripartite organisation reflects a considered view that understanding any given MLLM requires attending not only to how its components are structured, but also to how visual and linguistic representations are brought into correspondence during training, and what specific optimisation procedures are employed to achieve robust generalisation.

The survey by [arxiv_2402.12451] also extends its analysis across a wide range of downstream tasks, including visual grounding, image generation and editing, visual understanding, and domain-specific applications. This task-centric perspective complements the architecture-centric analysis by revealing how design decisions translate into differential performance profiles across problem types. Visual grounding, for instance, places particular demands on the spatial precision of cross-modal alignment, whereas image generation and editing require the model to operate generatively rather than merely interpretively. By treating these as distinct evaluative dimensions rather than collapsing them into a single performance metric, the survey provides a more granular picture of where current MLLMs excel and where significant gaps remain.

Equally important is the survey's treatment of training datasets and evaluation benchmarks. The compilation and description of these resources addresses a practical need in the research community: as the number of available benchmarks has grown rapidly, it has become increasingly difficult to assess whether reported performance improvements reflect genuine capability gains or merely optimisation toward particular evaluation artefacts. By conducting systematic comparisons among benchmarks, [arxiv_2402.12451] contributes to the methodological infrastructure needed for rigorous progress measurement. This is particularly valuable given that different benchmarks emphasise different aspects of multimodal competence, and a model that achieves state-of-the-art results on one may perform modestly on another.

Taken together, the survey literature on MLLMs reflects a field that is simultaneously expanding in scope and consolidating in methodology. The move from narrow, task-specific evaluations toward broad, multi-task benchmark suites mirrors a similar trajectory in the development of the models themselves, which have grown from specialised vision-language systems into general-purpose assistants capable of handling diverse inputs and instructions. Survey works such as [arxiv_2402.12451] play an essential role in this consolidation process by providing the community with shared vocabulary, comparative frameworks, and curated resource inventories. As the field continues to evolve, such overviews will remain indispensable reference points, not only for situating new contributions within the existing literature but also for identifying the open problems and underexplored directions that will define the next generation of multimodal research.

#### Evaluation and Benchmarking of Multimodal Large Language Models

The evaluation and benchmarking of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has emerged as a critical research priority, with scholars developing increasingly sophisticated frameworks to measure model capabilities across a diverse range of vision-language tasks. This body of work spans from broad, multi-task evaluation hubs to focused analyses of specific task categories such as visual question answering (VQA), image captioning, and complex reasoning, collectively forming a layered understanding of how MLLMs perform in both controlled and open-world settings.

At the most comprehensive end of the evaluation spectrum, LVLM-eHub represents a landmark effort to systematically assess the multimodal capabilities of publicly available large vision-language models. The earlier iteration of this benchmark evaluated eight representative models, including InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, across six categories of multimodal capabilities spanning 47 standard text-related visual benchmarks, while also incorporating an online arena platform for user-level, open-world question answering [arxiv_2306.09265]. A subsequent and expanded version of the same framework scaled the evaluation to thirteen models, including LLaVA, and reorganized the assessment into five capability categories across 42 in-domain benchmarks, additionally investigating how model-specific features such as architectural configurations and modality alignment strategies influence overall performance [ss_478371]. Together, these two iterations of LVLM-eHub illustrate the rapid growth of the MLLM landscape and the corresponding need to continuously revise evaluation infrastructure to accommodate new models and emerging task types. A notable finding across both versions is the identification of object hallucination as a persistent challenge, underscoring that quantitative benchmark performance does not always translate into reliable real-world behavior.

Complementing these broad evaluation hubs, more targeted comparative studies have examined the performance of multimodal instruction-tuned models across specific task configurations. One such study conducts a rigorous benchmarking and ablation analysis of different multimodal instruction tuning approaches, evaluating their zero-shot generalization across tasks including complex reasoning, open-ended conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification [arxiv_2310.03211]. This work is particularly valuable for its architectural insights, revealing how the method by which vision encoders are grafted onto pretrained language models—and subsequently fine-tuned through instruction tuning—substantially shapes downstream task performance. Whereas LVLM-eHub prioritizes breadth by aggregating results across dozens of benchmarks and many models, this comparative study prioritizes depth by isolating the contributions of specific design choices, making the two approaches methodologically complementary.

Within the broader evaluation landscape, VQA occupies a central and historically significant role as a benchmark task for multimodal systems. A comprehensive survey of VQA in the context of MLLMs traces the evolution of the task from early pipeline-based systems to contemporary vision-language pretraining and large model approaches, synthesizing progress in natural language understanding of images, multimodal fusion strategies, and knowledge reasoning modules [arxiv_2411.17558]. This survey distinguishes between models that rely on internal parametric knowledge and those that incorporate external knowledge sources, offering a nuanced taxonomy of reasoning capabilities that static leaderboard benchmarks often fail to capture. By situating VQA within the broader trajectory of MLLM development, this work highlights how the task has transitioned from a narrow image-question classification problem into a rich testbed for evaluating language grounding, commonsense inference, and cross-modal alignment simultaneously.

Taken together, these works reveal a clear progression in evaluation methodology: from single-task metrics toward multi-capability hubs, and from aggregate leaderboard rankings toward diagnostic analyses that illuminate the sources of model strengths and failures. The convergence of broad benchmarking efforts like LVLM-eHub [arxiv_2306.09265; ss_478371], task-specific comparative studies [arxiv_2310.03211], and survey-level syntheses of foundational tasks like VQA [arxiv_2411.17558] reflects the field's growing recognition that no single evaluation paradigm is sufficient to characterize the full scope of MLLM capabilities.

#### Interpretability and In-Context Learning in Multimodal Large Language Models

The interpretability of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) and their capacity for in-context learning represent two intertwined research frontiers, each demanding dedicated methodological frameworks. Progress in these areas has moved from foundational visualization tools designed to illuminate internal model mechanisms toward applied benchmarks that systematically probe emergent multimodal capabilities.

At the foundational end of this trajectory, VL-InterpreT [arxiv_2203.17247] addresses a critical gap that emerged as transformer-based architectures began dominating vision-language research: while interpretability tooling had matured considerably for unimodal NLP systems, the internal workings of multimodal transformers remained largely opaque. The authors respond by constructing an interactive visualization system that tracks attention statistics across all layers for both visual and linguistic components, rendering cross-modal and intra-modal attention patterns accessible to researchers. Crucially, the tool is designed to be task-agnostic, meaning it can be applied across diverse vision-language objectives without modification. This design philosophy reflects an understanding that interpretability infrastructure must be general enough to accompany the field as it scales, rather than being tightly coupled to any single downstream task. By making hidden representations and attention head behavior legible, VL-InterpreT lays the conceptual groundwork for asking more precise questions about how multimodal models integrate information across modalities.

Building on the recognition that interpretability is not merely a technical convenience but a prerequisite for trustworthy deployment, the survey by [arxiv_2412.02104] offers a comprehensive taxonomy of explainability and interpretability methods as they apply specifically to MLLMs. The survey situates the challenge within the broader convergence of large language models and computer vision, noting that while this convergence has produced impressive capabilities in tasks such as image-text generation, visual question answering, and cross-modal retrieval, it has simultaneously introduced complexity that resists straightforward analysis. The authors systematically categorize approaches to transparency and reliability, covering modalities that span text, vision, audio, and video. Compared with VL-InterpreT [arxiv_2203.17247], which offers a concrete tool targeting attention visualization in a specific architectural family, [arxiv_2412.02104] operates at a higher level of abstraction, mapping the landscape of methods and identifying where interpretability research has concentrated and where significant gaps remain. Together, these two contributions frame the problem at complementary levels of granularity: one providing hands-on mechanistic inspection, the other providing a conceptual map for the field.

The question of what MLLMs can actually learn from context—without any weight updates—introduces a distinct but related methodological challenge. In-context learning (ICL) was initially characterized as an emergent property of large language models, but its extension to the multimodal setting has proven more complex than early work suggested. [arxiv_2403.13164] argues that prior investigations into multimodal ICL have been methodologically narrow, concentrating almost exclusively on few-shot visual question answering and image captioning. The authors contend that these tasks neither exploit the genuine strengths of ICL nor expose its limitations, leaving the broader capability profile of multimodal ICL poorly understood. In response, they introduce VL-ICL Bench, a comprehensive benchmark spanning a wide spectrum of tasks that treat both images and text as inputs and outputs, and that vary the nature of the challenge presented to the model. This applied contribution is methodologically significant because it reframes evaluation itself as a form of mechanistic inquiry: by carefully designing tasks that stress-test ICL rather than merely confirm it, the benchmark enables researchers to ask sharper questions about when and why multimodal models succeed or fail at rapid adaptation.

Taken together, these works trace a coherent methodological arc. VL-InterpreT [arxiv_2203.17247] establishes the infrastructure for inspecting internal representations; [arxiv_2412.02104] surveys and organizes the broader interpretability landscape for MLLMs; and VL-ICL Bench [arxiv_2403.13164] applies rigorous evaluation methodology to probe one of the most consequential emergent behaviors these models exhibit. The progression from visualization tooling through taxonomic survey to targeted benchmarking reflects the field's gradual movement from asking what is happening inside these models to asking what they are genuinely capable of learning from context.

## Conclusion

The literature surveyed in this review tells a coherent story of a field that has moved with unusual speed from theoretical possibility to practical deployment. Beginning with the foundational insight that vision and language can be grounded in shared semantic spaces, researchers progressively constructed the architectural scaffolding, pretraining objectives, and scaling strategies necessary to realize that insight at the level of general-purpose systems. The subsequent development of instruction-tuning methodologies, efficient adaptation techniques, and domain-specific applications has further extended the reach of these models, embedding multimodal intelligence into an expanding range of real-world contexts.

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this synthesis. First, the tension between scale and efficiency is a persistent organizing concern: as foundation models have grown in capability, a parallel research tradition has worked to recover those capabilities in more accessible and deployable forms. Second, data quality and construction methodology have proven to be as consequential as architectural innovation, with the composition and curation of instruction-tuning corpora exerting substantial influence over downstream model behavior. Third, evaluation remains an open and contested problem; the proliferation of benchmarks reflects genuine uncertainty about what multimodal competence means and how it should be measured.

Perhaps most significantly, the field is still in the process of developing the interpretive tools necessary to understand what these models are actually doing when they succeed or fail. Work on interpretability and in-context learning represents an early but important effort to move beyond behavioral evaluation toward mechanistic understanding, and this direction seems likely to grow in importance as models are deployed in higher-stakes settings.

Future research will need to grapple seriously with questions of robustness, fairness, and the alignment of multimodal systems with human values and intentions. The architectural and empirical foundations reviewed here provide a strong basis for that work, but they do not resolve it. The field of multimodal large language models is, in the most productive sense, still becoming.
