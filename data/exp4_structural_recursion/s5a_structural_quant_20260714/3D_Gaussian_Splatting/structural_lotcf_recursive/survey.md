## Introduction

Three-dimensional Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) has rapidly emerged as one of the most transformative techniques in neural scene representation and novel view synthesis, offering a compelling combination of real-time rendering speed, high visual fidelity, and explicit geometric interpretability. Unlike its predecessor, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which relies on implicit volumetric representations encoded in neural networks, 3DGS represents scenes as collections of learnable, anisotropic Gaussian primitives that can be rasterized efficiently on modern graphics hardware. This fundamental shift in representation has catalyzed an explosion of research activity spanning compression, robustness, geometric reconstruction, dynamic scene modeling, and a wide array of downstream applications.

This literature review traces the intellectual lineage and current frontiers of 3DGS research through a structured, recursive examination of the field, organized along a foundational-to-applied axis. We begin with the core algorithmic principles of 3DGS and the rendering techniques and precursor methods that made it possible, before ascending through increasingly specialized and applied research directions. The review then examines broad surveys and comprehensive overviews that contextualize 3DGS within the wider landscape of radiance field methods, followed by a focused treatment of compactness and compression—a critical concern for practical deployment—spanning both high-level surveys and specific technical approaches including quantization, entropy coding, and structured spatial factorization.

From there, the review turns to robustness challenges that arise in real-world capture scenarios, including degraded inputs such as motion blur, imprecise or entirely unknown camera calibration, and the sparse-view regime where only a handful of images are available. We then address the fidelity of geometric surface reconstruction, a dimension in which vanilla 3DGS has historically lagged behind mesh-based and implicit methods. The review proceeds to examine alternative and enhanced primitive representations that extend or replace the standard Gaussian formulation, as well as scene decomposition strategies that disentangle materials, illumination, and structural components to enable physically grounded rendering and editing. Finally, we survey the dynamic and application-oriented frontier, encompassing deformable scenes, temporal modeling, scene editing, and the integration of 3DGS into broader pipelines for tasks such as autonomous driving, robotics, and content creation.

Together, these threads reveal a field that has matured with remarkable speed, moving from a single seminal method to a rich ecosystem of techniques addressing diverse practical and scientific challenges. This review aims to provide researchers and practitioners with a coherent map of that ecosystem, illuminating both the progress achieved and the open problems that continue to motivate new inquiry.

#### Core 3DGS Method, Rendering Principles, and Precursor Techniques

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for real-time novel-view synthesis can only be fully understood against the backdrop of the neural radiance field literature it sought to accelerate, and through careful scrutiny of the rendering approximations it introduced in pursuit of that acceleration.

Neural Radiance Fields established a principled framework for representing scenes as continuous volumetric functions queried by multilayer perceptrons, enabling photorealistic synthesis from unobserved viewpoints. However, the computational cost of this approach proved prohibitive for interactive applications, since rendering a single frame requires querying the network hundreds of times per ray. An influential line of work addressed this bottleneck through the concept of "baking": training a NeRF to convergence and then precomputing its outputs into an explicit, cache-friendly data structure. Hedman et al. proposed the Sparse Neural Radiance Grid (SNeRG), which reformulates the NeRF architecture and distils its learned volumetric content into a sparse voxel grid augmented with compact feature vectors [arxiv_2103.14645; ss_478166]. This baked representation retains the ability to reproduce fine geometric detail and view-dependent appearance while enabling real-time rendering on commodity hardware, demonstrating that the implicit-to-explicit conversion of neural scene representations was both feasible and practically valuable. The SNeRG line of work thus established a clear motivating precedent: the community recognised that the expressive power of volumetric neural representations need not be permanently coupled to the inference cost of deep networks, and that explicit or semi-explicit structures could serve as efficient surrogates.

Building directly on this motivation, Kerbl et al. introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting, which abandons the implicit MLP entirely in favour of a scene representation composed of anisotropic 3D Gaussians initialised from the sparse point cloud produced during camera calibration [arxiv_2308.04079]. Each Gaussian is parameterised by a position, a full 3D covariance matrix, an opacity, and view-dependent colour encoded via spherical harmonics. Optimisation proceeds by differentiable rasterisation: Gaussians are projected onto the image plane, sorted by depth, and alpha-composited in a tile-based renderer that exploits GPU parallelism to achieve rendering rates exceeding 30 frames per second at 1080p resolution. Crucially, the method preserves the desirable continuity properties of volumetric radiance fields during scene optimisation while sidestepping the per-ray network queries that made NeRF inference slow. The result is a system that matches or surpasses the visual quality of leading NeRF variants on standard benchmarks while reducing both training time and rendering latency by orders of magnitude [arxiv_2308.04079]. Compared with the baking approach of SNeRG [arxiv_2103.14645], 3DGS does not require a pre-trained NeRF as a teacher; it learns the explicit representation directly from multi-view images, making the pipeline more streamlined and removing the two-stage training dependency.

Despite its empirical success, the rendering pipeline of 3DGS departs from volumetrically principled ray-marching in several respects, raising the question of whether these approximations limit reconstruction quality. Hahlbohm et al. conduct a systematic analysis of exactly this issue, examining the gap between the hybrid rasterisation-based rendering employed by 3DGS and the theoretically grounded volumetric rendering that underpins NeRF [arxiv_2502.19318]. Their investigation finds that while 3DGS shares a broadly similar image formation model with NeRF, its practical implementation introduces approximations—including the projection of 3D Gaussians onto 2D splats and the tile-based depth sorting—that deviate from strict volumetric correctness. Importantly, the study asks whether replacing these approximations with more principled solutions yields measurable quality improvements, providing nuanced evidence that the relationship between rendering accuracy and perceptual quality is non-trivial [arxiv_2502.19318]. This analytical work complements the original 3DGS contribution [arxiv_2308.04079] by clarifying which design choices are load-bearing for quality and which represent acceptable engineering trade-offs, thereby informing subsequent efforts to refine the method's theoretical foundations without sacrificing its signature real-time performance.

#### Surveys and Comprehensive Reviews of 3DGS and Radiance Field Methods

The rapid maturation of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a research paradigm has prompted the community to produce a growing body of survey literature aimed at systematically situating the method within the broader landscape of novel-view synthesis and radiance field research. These overviews collectively serve a dual function: they document the historical trajectory from implicit neural representations toward explicit, rasterization-based approaches, and they provide taxonomic frameworks that help researchers navigate an increasingly dense publication space.

The most directly focused of these efforts is the work by Chen and colleagues [arxiv_2401.03890], which presents what the authors describe as the first systematic overview of 3DGS developments. Their survey begins by tracing the foundational principles that motivated the shift away from mainstream implicit neural models, emphasizing that the use of millions of learnable 3D Gaussians as an explicit scene representation, combined with a differentiable rendering pipeline, enables both real-time rendering and a degree of scene editability that implicit methods struggle to match. By foregrounding practical applicability—particularly rendering speed—the survey frames 3DGS not merely as an incremental improvement over Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) but as a potentially transformative paradigm for 3D reconstruction and representation more broadly. The scope of downstream applications surveyed ranges from virtual reality to interactive content creation, reflecting the authors' argument that the explicit nature of the representation opens qualitatively new use cases.

A complementary perspective is offered by Fei and colleagues [arxiv_2403.11134], whose literature review of recent 3DGS methods adopts a functionality-driven taxonomy, organizing contributions into three broad categories: 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications. This classification scheme is particularly useful for readers seeking to understand how the explicit Gaussian ellipsoid representation facilitates tasks that are difficult for coordinate-based neural networks. The authors draw a clear architectural contrast between 3DGS and NeRF: whereas NeRF encodes a scene through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks queried via volumetric ray marching, 3DGS models the scene as a collection of Gaussian ellipsoids that are rasterized directly into images, yielding substantially faster rendering. By emphasizing dynamic reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation as areas where the explicit representation confers practical advantages, this survey complements [arxiv_2401.03890] while offering a somewhat more granular functional decomposition of the literature.

Both of these 3DGS-centric surveys benefit from being read alongside the broader historical account provided by Gao and colleagues [arxiv_2210.00379], whose comprehensive review of NeRF spans the period from 2020 to 2025 and explicitly bridges the pre- and post-Gaussian Splatting eras. This longitudinal perspective is valuable because it contextualizes 3DGS not as an isolated innovation but as a direct competitor to the NeRF framework that, following its introduction in August 2023, rapidly overtook implicit methods as the dominant paradigm for novel-view synthesis in terms of research interest. The survey's dual-era structure allows readers to trace how implicit, hybrid, and explicit representations have coexisted and competed, and it situates 3DGS within a longer arc of neural field research rather than treating it as a self-contained development. Where [arxiv_2401.03890] and [arxiv_2403.11134] focus inward on the 3DGS ecosystem, [arxiv_2210.00379] provides the outward, field-level framing that explains why the community's attention shifted so decisively.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a consensus on several key points: that the explicit Gaussian representation resolves fundamental speed limitations inherent to implicit volumetric rendering; that editability and downstream applicability are primary drivers of research interest; and that 3DGS must be understood in relation to, rather than in isolation from, the NeRF lineage it has largely supplanted. The existence of multiple overlapping surveys also signals the field's rapid growth and the corresponding need for periodic taxonomic consolidation as new methods continue to emerge.

#### Surveys and Overviews of 3DGS Compression and Compactness

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm in explicit scene representation has prompted a growing body of work dedicated to understanding, cataloguing, and systematizing the compression strategies that have developed around it. At the broadest level of this literature, survey and overview contributions occupy a foundational role, establishing the conceptual vocabulary, taxonomic structure, and evaluative benchmarks against which more specialized methods are measured. The single most encompassing reference in this space is the survey by [arxiv_2502.19457], which provides a comprehensive overview of compression methods, trends, and future directions for 3DGS as of 2025.

To appreciate why such a survey is necessary, one must first understand the representational commitments of 3DGS itself. Unlike neural radiance field (NeRF) approaches, which rely on implicit, coordinate-based models that map spatial coordinates to pixel values through a learned neural network, 3DGS adopts an explicitly parameterized scene representation composed of millions of learnable three-dimensional Gaussians [arxiv_2502.19457]. Each Gaussian primitive carries a set of attributes — including position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients encoding view-dependent appearance — that together define the scene. Rendering proceeds through a differentiable splatting pipeline that projects these primitives onto the image plane, enabling real-time rendering speeds that implicit methods have historically struggled to match [arxiv_2502.19457]. The explicit nature of the representation also confers a degree of editability that is largely absent from coordinate-based neural representations, making 3DGS an attractive candidate for interactive applications, augmented reality, and the next generation of 3D reconstruction pipelines [arxiv_2502.19457].

However, these advantages come at a significant cost. Because scene fidelity in 3DGS scales with the number of Gaussian primitives, high-quality reconstructions routinely require millions of such primitives, each carrying multiple floating-point attributes. The resulting memory footprint and storage requirements are substantial, and they pose a direct challenge to deployment on resource-constrained devices such as mobile platforms, embedded systems, and edge hardware [arxiv_2502.19457]. It is precisely this tension — between the representational power of large Gaussian populations and the practical constraints of real-world deployment — that motivates the entire subfield of 3DGS compression, and that the survey of [arxiv_2502.19457] is designed to frame and organize.

The survey's contribution is therefore not merely bibliographic. By situating 3DGS within the broader history of scene representation and contrasting it systematically with NeRF-based alternatives, [arxiv_2502.19457] provides the conceptual scaffolding that allows disparate compression strategies — spanning pruning, quantization, neural codecs, and compact primitive representations — to be understood as responses to a common set of constraints. This framing is essential for readers approaching the field for the first time, as the compression literature has grown rapidly and in multiple directions simultaneously, making it difficult to assess the relative maturity, applicability, and trade-offs of individual methods without a unifying reference point.

From an applied perspective, the survey also performs the important function of identifying open problems and projecting future research directions [arxiv_2502.19457]. This prospective dimension distinguishes it from purely descriptive catalogues and positions it as a living map of the field rather than a historical record. For researchers designing new compression algorithms, the identification of underexplored trade-offs — such as the balance between compression ratio, rendering fidelity, and editing flexibility — provides actionable guidance that purely empirical papers do not supply.

In sum, survey-level work such as [arxiv_2502.19457] serves as the indispensable foundation for the more specialized literature reviewed in subsequent sections of this chapter. By establishing what 3DGS is, why compression is necessary, and how the solution space is structured, it enables a coherent, cumulative reading of the methods that follow.

##### Quantization, Encoding, and Attribute Compression for Compact 3DGS

Early efforts to compress individual Gaussian primitives focused on relatively straightforward quantization schemes before progressing toward more sophisticated learned attribute encodings. This trajectory from simple to complex compression strategies reflects the field's growing recognition that both the number of Gaussians and the precision with which their attributes are stored must be addressed simultaneously to achieve meaningful reductions in memory footprint.

A foundational step in this direction is taken by EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564], which introduces quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements in 3D Gaussian splatting. The approach is conceptually direct: by representing Gaussian attributes at lower numerical precision through quantization, the per-primitive storage cost is substantially reduced without requiring a complete redesign of the splatting pipeline. Alongside this quantization strategy, EAGLES employs a coarse-to-fine training procedure intended to stabilize optimization and a pruning stage that reduces the total number of Gaussians in the scene representation. The combination of fewer primitives and more compact per-primitive encodings yields improvements in both training speed and rendering throughput. While the quantization mechanism itself is relatively simple in design, EAGLES establishes an important baseline by demonstrating that lightweight per-point encodings can be integrated into the 3DGS training loop without catastrophic degradation of visual quality.

Building on the recognition that attribute compactness and primitive count must be co-optimized, Compact 3D Gaussian Representation for Radiance Field [arxiv_2311.13681] pursues a more structured approach to attribute compression. Rather than relying solely on numerical quantization, this work places explicit emphasis on two complementary objectives: reducing the number of Gaussian points and compressing the attributes associated with each retained Gaussian. By treating these as joint design goals rather than independent post-processing steps, the method moves toward a more principled framework for compact scene representation. The explicit focus on attribute compression as a first-class objective distinguishes this work from EAGLES, where quantization serves primarily as a storage-reduction mechanism applied after the core splatting framework is established. This shift in framing signals a maturation in the community's understanding of where compression gains are most effectively realized.

The most comprehensive treatment of compact per-Gaussian attribute representation among these works is presented in Compact 3D Gaussian Splatting for Static and Dynamic Radiance Fields [arxiv_2408.03822], which extends the dual objectives of point reduction and attribute compression to both static and dynamic scene settings. A key contribution is the introduction of a learnable mask strategy that governs which Gaussians are retained, enabling the model to jointly optimize scene fidelity and representational economy in an end-to-end manner. Critically, this work also targets the compression of specific Gaussian attributes, including view-dependent color encoded via spherical harmonics and the covariance parameters that define each primitive's shape and orientation. These attributes are among the most storage-intensive components of a 3DGS representation, and compressing them through learned encodings rather than simple scalar quantization represents a meaningful increase in methodological sophistication relative to EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564]. The extension to dynamic radiance fields further broadens the applicability of compact attribute representations beyond the static scene assumption that underlies most prior work in this area.

Taken together, these three works trace a clear progression in the treatment of per-Gaussian attribute compression. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] establishes quantization as a viable and practical tool for reducing per-point memory costs. Compact 3D Gaussian Representation [arxiv_2311.13681] elevates attribute compression to a primary design objective alongside point-count reduction. Compact 3DGS for Static and Dynamic Radiance Fields [arxiv_2408.03822] then realizes this objective through learned, end-to-end encodings that target the most memory-intensive attributes and generalize across scene types. The cumulative effect of this line of work is a substantially richer toolkit for achieving compact Gaussian representations without sacrificing the rendering quality that makes 3DGS attractive in the first place.

##### Structured Spatial Representations: Grids, Factorization, and Hybrid Fields for 3DGS Compactness

Achieving compactness in 3D Gaussian Splatting through structural reorganization of Gaussian parameters—rather than through post-hoc compression of individual attributes—has motivated a distinct line of research that exploits spatial regularity, algebraic factorization, and hybrid explicit-implicit decompositions. These approaches share the insight that the sheer number of independent Gaussian parameters is itself the primary source of storage overhead, and that imposing structured spatial layouts can eliminate redundancy at a representational level.

The earliest and most spatially explicit of these strategies is the self-organizing Gaussian grid proposed by Morgenstern and colleagues [arxiv_2312.13299]. Their central observation is that natural scenes contain perceptual redundancies that permit many different permutations of Gaussian parameters to yield equivalent renderings. Rather than exploiting this degeneracy arbitrarily, they organize the full set of 3DGS parameters into a 2D grid that enforces local homogeneity—neighboring grid cells are encouraged to store similar parameter values, making the resulting layout amenable to conventional image compression pipelines. The approach is explicitly spatial: the grid is not a latent or learned embedding but a direct rearrangement of the Gaussian attributes themselves, achieved through a highly parallel sorting procedure. By mapping the inherently unstructured point cloud of Gaussians onto a regular 2D manifold, the method converts the storage problem into one of compressing a locally smooth image, achieving drastic reductions in file size without sacrificing rendering quality. This work is foundational in the sense that it treats structure as a property to be imposed on the parameter space rather than derived from a neural network.

Building on the broader principle of structured decomposition, F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083] draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization to address the same storage bottleneck from a coordinate-centric perspective. Where [arxiv_2312.13299] reorganizes parameters spatially, F-3DGS factorizes the coordinate representation of dense Gaussian clusters, approximating groups of Gaussians through low-rank factor matrices rather than storing each Gaussian independently. This approach is motivated by the observation that Gaussians representing smooth or homogeneous scene regions form dense clusters whose collective description is highly redundant. By decomposing these clusters into compact factorized forms, the method achieves substantial reductions in storage while preserving image quality. The factorization strategy is algebraic rather than perceptual, yet it converges on a similar conclusion to [arxiv_2312.13299]: the redundancy latent in a large Gaussian population can be exploited structurally, and doing so at the level of coordinate representation yields efficiency gains that attribute-level compression alone cannot match.

Both [arxiv_2312.13299] and [arxiv_2405.17083] operate primarily within the explicit Gaussian paradigm, reorganizing or factorizing parameters without introducing learned neural components to predict Gaussian properties. HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] represents a more applied synthesis of these ideas, combining explicit Gaussians with grid-based neural fields in a hybrid architecture. The key design decision in HyRF is a decomposition of scene information by frequency: a compact set of explicit Gaussians retains only the high-frequency parameters that are difficult for smooth neural fields to capture, while grid-based neural fields predict the remaining, lower-frequency properties such as view-dependent color and coarse shape. This decomposition directly addresses a limitation that purely neural compression methods encounter—their tendency to blur high-frequency spatial variations—while simultaneously avoiding the memory overhead of storing all parameters per Gaussian. Compared to [arxiv_2312.13299], which achieves compactness through spatial sorting and local smoothness, HyRF achieves it through a principled separation of representational responsibilities between explicit and implicit components. Compared to [arxiv_2405.17083], which factorizes coordinate clusters algebraically, HyRF introduces a learned grid structure that can adapt to scene-specific frequency content.

Taken together, these three works trace a progression from purely explicit spatial reorganization [arxiv_2312.13299], through algebraic factorization of coordinate representations [arxiv_2405.17083], to hybrid neural-explicit field decompositions [arxiv_2509.17083]. Each step increases the degree to which learned or structured representations absorb scene redundancy, reflecting a broader trajectory in the field toward representations that are compact by design rather than by compression after the fact.

##### Robustness to Blurry Inputs and Inaccurate/Unknown Camera Poses

The challenge of reconstructing high-fidelity 3D scenes from degraded or poorly calibrated image inputs represents a significant frontier in Gaussian splatting research. While the baseline 3D Gaussian Splatting framework delivers compelling real-time rendering quality under ideal conditions, its performance degrades substantially when training images are affected by blur or when accurate camera poses are unavailable. A coherent body of work has emerged to address these interrelated failure modes, progressing from foundational deblurring formulations toward more integrated pipelines that jointly handle pose estimation and image degradation.

The problem of blurry training images was identified early as a critical vulnerability of Gaussian-based scene representations. Blurriness arises from multiple sources in practice, including lens defocus, object motion, and camera shake, and it inevitably compromises the sharpness of acquired training data. As noted in [arxiv_2401.00834], while prior neural field methods had attempted to render clean images from blurry inputs, these approaches relied on volumetric rendering and neural networks that are computationally expensive and unsuitable for real-time applications. The work in [arxiv_2401.00834] directly confronts this limitation within the 3D Gaussian Splatting paradigm, proposing a deblurring framework that preserves the real-time rendering advantages of explicit Gaussian representations while mitigating the severe quality degradation caused by blurry inputs. By modeling the blur process and incorporating it into the optimization of the Gaussian scene representation, this approach establishes a foundational methodology for handling degraded inputs without sacrificing the efficiency that makes Gaussian splatting practically attractive.

Building upon this foundation, [arxiv_2403.11831] introduces BAD-Gaussians (Bundle Adjusted Deblur Gaussian Splatting), which specifically targets motion blur arising from real-world conditions such as low-light or long-exposure photography. A key distinction of this work relative to [arxiv_2401.00834] lies in its explicit treatment of camera motion during exposure: rather than treating blur as a static image-level artifact, BAD-Gaussians models the continuous camera trajectory over the exposure interval and synthesizes the blurred image as an aggregate of sharp renders along that trajectory. This physically grounded formulation allows the method to recover intricate scene details from severely motion-blurred inputs more accurately than NeRF-based predecessors, which struggle with implicit representations under such conditions. Furthermore, by coupling bundle adjustment with Gaussian optimization, BAD-Gaussians simultaneously refines camera poses and scene geometry, acknowledging that motion blur and pose inaccuracy are often co-occurring problems in practice. The explicit point cloud representation of 3D Gaussians is identified in [arxiv_2403.11831] as a key enabler of this joint optimization, offering geometric tractability that implicit NeRF representations cannot easily provide.

The challenge of unknown or inaccurate camera poses is addressed more broadly by [arxiv_2312.07504], which proposes a COLMAP-free Gaussian splatting pipeline that eliminates the dependency on Structure-from-Motion preprocessing entirely. Whereas both [arxiv_2401.00834] and [arxiv_2403.11831] assume some form of camera pose availability—even if refined during optimization—[arxiv_2312.07504] operates without any pre-computed poses, leveraging the sequential continuity of video input to progressively grow the Gaussian set one frame at a time. The explicit geometric nature of 3D Gaussians is again highlighted as a decisive advantage over NeRF-based pose-free methods, where simultaneous optimization of implicit scene structure and camera parameters presents compounded difficulties. By processing frames incrementally and exploiting inter-frame continuity, the method sidesteps the need for global pose initialization, making it applicable in scenarios where calibration infrastructure is entirely absent.

Taken together, these three works trace a methodological arc from targeted deblurring of static artifacts [arxiv_2401.00834], through physically motivated joint deblurring and pose refinement [arxiv_2403.11831], to fully pose-free reconstruction pipelines [arxiv_2312.07504]. Each contribution exploits the explicit representational properties of 3D Gaussians as a core technical lever, collectively demonstrating that robustness to degraded inputs and unreliable camera calibration is achievable without sacrificing the real-time rendering efficiency that distinguishes Gaussian splatting from its neural field predecessors.

##### Robustness to Sparse/Few-Shot Input Views

Reconstructing high-quality 3D Gaussian scenes from sparse or few-shot input views represents one of the most practically significant challenges in the field, as real-world capture scenarios rarely afford the dense multi-view coverage assumed by standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) pipelines. When training data is limited, Gaussian primitives tend to overfit the observed viewpoints, producing degraded geometry and appearance when rendered from novel perspectives. The methods reviewed here address this problem through progressively more sophisticated strategies, ranging from co-regularization between parallel models, through monocular depth supervision, to multi-view stereo priors and structural consistency enforcement.

The simplest conceptual departure from vanilla 3DGS is to introduce a self-supervisory signal that does not require additional ground-truth data. CoR-GS [arxiv_2405.12110] pursues this direction by training two independent 3D Gaussian radiance fields simultaneously and exploiting the disagreements that naturally arise between them due to the stochasticity of the densification process. The authors identify two measurable forms of disagreement—point disagreement and rendering disagreement—and demonstrate empirically that both are negatively correlated with reconstruction accuracy. By quantifying and suppressing regions of high disagreement, CoR-GS effectively identifies and penalises inaccurate reconstruction without any access to ground-truth supervision. This co-regularization perspective is elegant in its simplicity, requiring no external priors or additional networks, and establishes a principled unsupervised criterion for quality assessment under sparse-view conditions.

Building on the intuition that geometric priors can constrain underdetermined reconstructions, depth-aware approaches introduce explicit shape supervision. The method proposed in [arxiv_2410.11080] incorporates monocular depth predictions as a prior, coupling them with a scale-invariant depth loss to regularise the 3D structure when only a handful of input images are available. Beyond depth supervision, the authors address overfitting in the appearance domain by restricting spherical harmonic coefficients to lower orders, thereby limiting the model's capacity to memorise view-dependent colour effects from sparse observations. Notably, they also reconsider the standard opacity-based pruning strategy: whereas the original 3DGS periodically removes low-opacity splats to maintain a compact representation, the authors find that this practice leads to an excessively sparse point cloud under few-shot conditions, and they instead retain all splats to preserve coverage. This modification highlights how design choices that are beneficial in the dense-view regime can become detrimental when input is scarce.

Whereas [arxiv_2410.11080] relies on monocular depth estimation, MVPGS [arxiv_2409.14316] draws on learning-based Multi-view Stereo (MVS) to obtain richer geometric initialisation for the Gaussian primitives. The key insight is that MVS networks, trained on large corpora of multi-view data, encode strong geometric priors that can compensate for the absence of dense input views at test time. To further mitigate overfitting in appearance, MVPGS introduces a forward-warping mechanism that generates additional appearance constraints consistent with the geometry, effectively augmenting the supervisory signal without requiring extra captured images. The combination of improved geometric initialisation and appearance regularisation positions MVPGS as a more comprehensive solution than depth-only approaches, though it introduces a dependency on a pre-trained MVS network.

The most structurally ambitious approach among those reviewed is SCGaussian [arxiv_2411.03637], which targets the fundamental difficulty that Gaussian primitive positions—unlike rendering outputs—cannot be directly constrained in the standard 3DGS formulation due to its non-structural nature. SCGaussian addresses this by proposing a hybrid Gaussian representation that enables optimisation of scene structure at two levels: the rendered geometry and, crucially, the spatial positions of the Gaussian primitives themselves. Matching priors are incorporated to enforce 3D structural consistency across views, making the method particularly well-suited to large scenes where sparse inputs leave vast regions of the scene unconstrained. Compared to MVPGS, which focuses on initialisation and appearance regularisation, SCGaussian intervenes more directly in the structural properties of the representation throughout optimisation.

Taken together, these works trace a clear trajectory from lightweight co-regularization [arxiv_2405.12110], through monocular depth supervision [arxiv_2410.11080] and multi-view stereo priors [arxiv_2409.14316], to explicit structural consistency enforcement [arxiv_2411.03637], reflecting the community's growing recognition that robust few-shot reconstruction requires constraints operating at multiple levels of the Gaussian representation.

#### Geometric Surface Fidelity and Reconstruction Quality

The challenge of recovering geometrically faithful surfaces from 3D Gaussian Splatting representations has motivated a distinct line of research that couples the efficiency of explicit Gaussian primitives with the regularity of continuous or implicit geometric constraints. Standard 3DGS achieves impressive rendering quality but inherits structural limitations from its discrete, unordered set of primitives: surfaces reconstructed directly from Gaussian opacity fields tend to be rough, poorly aligned with true geometry, and particularly unreliable in regions where Gaussian density is low. The works examined here address these shortcomings through complementary strategies, ranging from kernel-based continuous field construction to dual-branch architectures that enforce signed-distance-function (SDF) consistency.

A foundational observation motivating this body of work is that the discreteness of 3DGS is simultaneously its greatest computational asset and its primary geometric liability. Because each Gaussian primitive is an independent entity, the aggregate surface emerges only implicitly from the collective opacity of potentially millions of splats. In sparsely covered regions—common at object boundaries, in open outdoor scenes, or wherever Structure-from-Motion initialization is thin—this aggregation produces gaps and rough surface details that no amount of photometric supervision can reliably correct. Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) methods avoid this problem through their inherently continuous implicit representations, but at the cost of substantially longer training and rendering times. The papers reviewed here seek a middle path that preserves the rasterization speed of 3DGS while recovering the geometric coherence associated with implicit approaches.

The work on Gaussian Voxel Kernel Functions (GVKF) [arxiv_2411.01853] addresses the open-scene surface reconstruction problem by constructing a continuous scene representation on top of the discrete Gaussian set through kernel regression. Rather than treating each Gaussian as an isolated primitive, GVKF aggregates local Gaussian contributions within a voxel grid using kernel functions, effectively converting the discrete field into a smooth, query-able implicit representation. This design retains the fast rasterization pipeline of standard 3DGS for rendering while enabling the extraction of high-fidelity surface meshes even in sparse Gaussian areas where direct Gaussian-based meshing would otherwise fail. The authors demonstrate that excessive memory consumption—another consequence of relying on huge numbers of Gaussian primitives to approximate smooth surfaces—is also mitigated by the voxel-structured aggregation. GVKF thus represents a largely post-hoc geometric regularization strategy: the Gaussian field is trained in a broadly conventional manner, and the kernel regression layer imposes continuity at the surface extraction stage.

A structurally different but complementary approach is taken by GSDF [arxiv_2403.16964], which introduces a dual-branch architecture in which a 3DGS branch and an SDF-based neural implicit branch are trained jointly and mutually constrained. The motivation is that neither representation alone is sufficient: the Gaussian branch provides high rendering quality and fast optimization, while the SDF branch enforces a well-defined, differentiable surface that the Gaussian branch can be guided toward. By coupling the two branches—allowing gradients from the SDF to regularize the geometry of the Gaussians and vice versa—GSDF addresses the tendency of density-field or primitive-shape constraints in prior work to degrade rendering quality when geometric regularity is imposed too aggressively. The dual-branch design is particularly relevant for larger and more intricate scenes where fine surface details are difficult to capture with either representation in isolation.

Comparing the two approaches, GVKF operates primarily at the level of surface extraction and is agnostic to the training objective of the underlying Gaussian field, making it broadly applicable but potentially limited in its ability to reshape the Gaussian distribution itself during optimization. GSDF, by contrast, intervenes during training through the coupled SDF branch, allowing geometric constraints to influence where and how Gaussians are placed. Together, these works illustrate a progression from post-hoc continuity imposition toward tightly integrated geometric supervision, reflecting a broader methodological trajectory in which the geometric fidelity of 3DGS is increasingly treated as a first-class training objective rather than a byproduct of photometric reconstruction.

#### Alternative and Enhanced Primitive Representations

A notable direction within the 3D Gaussian Splatting literature concerns the replacement or augmentation of the standard Gaussian primitive with geometrically richer alternatives, motivated by well-documented shortcomings of the original ellipsoidal representation. The canonical 3DGS formulation, while achieving impressive rendering speed and quality, is known to struggle with sharp discontinuities, hard edges, and flat surfaces—limitations that have prompted researchers to explore fundamentally different primitive shapes.

One of the earliest and most direct responses to these limitations is the proposal of half-Gaussian kernels. The work on 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting (3D-HGS) [arxiv_2406.02720] identifies that standard 3D Gaussian Splatting encounters difficulties specifically with shape and color discontinuities, which arise because a symmetric Gaussian kernel cannot naturally encode an abrupt transition in appearance or geometry. The authors propose 3D Half-Gaussian (3D-HGS) kernels as a plug-and-play replacement that can be integrated into existing 3DGS pipelines without sacrificing rendering speed. By truncating the Gaussian along one axis, the primitive gains an asymmetric profile better suited to representing boundaries and discontinuous surfaces. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that this modification achieves state-of-the-art rendering quality across standard benchmarks while remaining compatible with the broader ecosystem of 3DGS-based methods, underscoring its practical appeal as an incremental but impactful enhancement.

A more structurally ambitious departure from the Gaussian primitive is offered by 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) [arxiv_2411.14974], which replaces Gaussians entirely with smooth convex shapes. The motivation here is explicitly geometric: standard Gaussians are diffuse volumetric entities that disperse irregularly around surfaces unless constrained by hand-crafted regularizers, making it difficult to represent flat planar regions or capture hard edges without a substantial increase in primitive count and associated memory cost. Smooth convex primitives, by contrast, offer greater geometric flexibility—they can approximate a wider variety of local surface shapes, including flat patches and sharp boundaries, with fewer primitives. This directly addresses the memory footprint concern that plagues dense Gaussian reconstructions of complex scenes. Compared to 3D-HGS [arxiv_2406.02720], which retains the Gaussian functional form and modifies only its symmetry, 3DCS represents a more thoroughgoing rethinking of the primitive, trading the mathematical convenience of Gaussians for a richer shape vocabulary grounded in convex geometry.

A third approach, TRIPS (Trilinear Point Splatting) [arxiv_2401.06003], takes a hybrid strategy that draws on both Gaussian Splatting and the neural point-based renderer ADOP. The authors observe that 3DGS produces blurring and cloudy artifacts in highly detailed scenes, while ADOP, though capable of crisper reconstructions, suffers from temporal instability, reduced performance due to its neural reconstruction network, and an inability to handle large gaps in the point cloud. TRIPS addresses these complementary weaknesses by rasterizing points into a screen-space image pyramid using trilinear interpolation, combining the efficiency of splatting with a more structured image-space representation. Unlike 3D-HGS and 3DCS, which intervene at the level of the 3D primitive geometry, TRIPS intervenes at the rasterization and image-formation stage, using the trilinear splat as a bridge between point-based scene representations and high-fidelity screen-space rendering. This distinction is significant: TRIPS is less concerned with improving geometric fidelity of individual primitives and more concerned with how primitives project and accumulate in image space to recover fine detail.

Taken together, these three methods illustrate a spectrum of strategies for enriching the primitive representation in Gaussian-based radiance field rendering. 3D-HGS [arxiv_2406.02720] offers a conservative, drop-in modification; 3DCS [arxiv_2411.14974] proposes a wholesale geometric redesign; and TRIPS [arxiv_2401.06003] reframes the problem at the rendering pipeline level. All three converge on the diagnosis that the standard isotropic or anisotropic Gaussian is insufficient for capturing the full complexity of real scenes, and each offers a distinct methodological answer to that shared challenge.

#### Scene Decomposition for Material, Illumination, and Structural Fidelity

The faithful reconstruction of real-world scenes demands more than photometric accuracy; it requires disentangling the physically meaningful components that govern appearance—surface materials, illumination conditions, and structural regularities. A growing body of work within the 3D Gaussian Splatting and Neural Radiance Field literature has therefore moved beyond view-dependent colour reproduction toward principled scene decomposition, addressing the inverse rendering problem and exploiting geometric redundancy to recover representations that are both more interpretable and more robust under challenging observation conditions.

A foundational challenge in this decomposition agenda is the inherent ill-posedness of inverse rendering: given only multi-view images, one must simultaneously recover geometry, spatially varying surface materials, and environmental lighting from observations in which all three factors are entangled. PBR-NeRF [arxiv_2412.09680] confronts this challenge directly by grounding a Neural Radiance Field framework in Physics-Based Rendering (PBR) theory. The authors argue that the dominant paradigm in both NeRF and 3D Gaussian Splatting—estimating view-dependent appearance as a monolithic function—conflates material reflectance with illumination effects, thereby producing representations that cannot be reliably edited, relighted, or transferred to novel environments. To resolve this, PBR-NeRF introduces a joint estimation model for geometry, materials, and illumination, augmented by two novel physics-based priors that are formulated as differentiable loss terms. These priors impose physically motivated constraints on the solution space, regularising the otherwise underdetermined inverse problem without sacrificing novel view synthesis quality. The result is a method that achieves state-of-the-art material estimation while remaining architecturally adaptable to other inverse rendering pipelines. The significance of this contribution lies not merely in its quantitative gains but in its conceptual reframing: by insisting that scene representations encode physically meaningful quantities rather than opaque appearance functions, PBR-NeRF establishes a principled baseline for decomposition-oriented reconstruction.

Where PBR-NeRF addresses the material-illumination axis of scene decomposition, a complementary line of inquiry targets structural fidelity under sparse or occluded observations. Splat and Replace [arxiv_2506.06462] approaches this problem through the lens of geometric redundancy, observing that real-world environments are frequently populated by repetitive elements—tiled surfaces, regularly spaced columns, identical fixtures—whose recurrence constitutes an exploitable prior. Standard 3DGS reconstructions treat each region of the scene independently, so areas that are poorly covered by training views or occluded by foreground objects tend to exhibit degraded geometry and appearance. The key insight of Splat and Replace is that repeated instances of the same structural element collectively provide far richer observational coverage than any single instance alone; information from well-observed instances can therefore be shared with poorly observed counterparts. The method segments individual repeated instances within a 3DGS reconstruction, registers them into a common coordinate frame, and enables cross-instance information transfer while explicitly modelling appearance variation across instances to avoid over-smoothing. This last point is important: naive averaging across instances would erase legitimate inter-instance differences such as weathering, lighting variation, or minor geometric deviations, so the method must balance structural consistency with appearance fidelity.

Considered together, these two works illuminate complementary dimensions of scene decomposition. PBR-NeRF [arxiv_2412.09680] operates at the level of physical signal decomposition, separating the radiometric contributions of material and light, while Splat and Replace [arxiv_2506.06462] operates at the level of structural decomposition, leveraging scene-level repetition to propagate geometric and appearance information across instances. Both approaches share the conviction that naive, monolithic scene representations are insufficient for faithful reconstruction under real-world conditions—whether those conditions involve ambiguous lighting, limited viewpoint diversity, or occlusion. Together they suggest that the next generation of high-fidelity reconstruction systems will need to integrate multiple decomposition strategies, coupling physics-based material and illumination modelling with structural priors that exploit the regularities inherent in man-made and natural environments.

### Dynamic Scenes, Editing, and Application Extensions

The extension of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) beyond static scene reconstruction into dynamic, editable, and application-driven domains has generated a substantial body of research, each contribution addressing a distinct limitation of the baseline framework. A natural progression in this literature moves from the fundamental challenge of representing temporal change, through increasingly sophisticated mechanisms for scene manipulation, to expressive representational enhancements that unify multiple reconstruction objectives.

The most foundational challenge in extending 3DGS to real-world applications is the modelling of dynamic scenes, where objects deform and move over time. Early neural radiance field approaches handled deformation implicitly through learned mappings, but such methods lack geometric grounding and can produce physically incoherent results. Addressing this directly, [arxiv_2404.06270] proposes a geometry-aware deformable Gaussian splatting framework in which each Gaussian primitive is explicitly optimised to translate and rotate over time. Crucially, the method extracts 3D geometric features from the scene and integrates them into the deformation learning process, enforcing spatial coherence that purely implicit approaches cannot guarantee. This geometry-driven formulation represents a principled departure from NeRF-based dynamic modelling, leveraging the explicit point-cloud nature of 3DGS to impose structural constraints during temporal evolution.

A complementary approach to dynamic reconstruction is taken by [arxiv_2405.17891], which refines the Gaussian representation itself rather than solely modifying the deformation pipeline. This work introduces a deformable multi-layer perceptron network to predict per-Gaussian dynamic offsets, while employing hash encoding to represent colour features compactly. The motivation is dual: to improve rendering fidelity in dynamic settings and to mitigate the substantial memory overhead that arises when naively scaling 3DGS to temporal sequences. Compared with [arxiv_2404.06270], which foregrounds geometric coherence as the primary concern, [arxiv_2405.17891] places equal emphasis on memory efficiency, reflecting the practical constraints that emerge when deploying dynamic Gaussian representations at scale. Together, these two works illustrate that dynamic scene reconstruction with 3DGS requires simultaneous attention to geometric consistency, temporal expressiveness, and computational tractability.

Beyond reconstruction, a growing body of work addresses the need for interactive manipulation and editing of Gaussian scenes, capabilities that are essential for content creation workflows. [arxiv_2405.17811] tackles this problem by coupling 3DGS with a triangular mesh proxy, enabling users to manipulate the underlying mesh and propagate those transformations to the associated Gaussian primitives. The authors argue that while NeRF-based editing methods exist, they suffer from limited controllability and slow inference, whereas 3DGS offers the speed and explicit structure necessary for responsive manipulation. By binding Gaussians to mesh geometry, Mani-GS achieves photo-realistic rendering quality under free-form deformation, bridging the gap between the geometric controllability of mesh-based pipelines and the visual fidelity of neural rendering. This mesh-coupled strategy contrasts with the deformation-field approaches of [arxiv_2404.06270] and [arxiv_2405.17891], which are oriented toward reconstructing observed dynamics rather than enabling user-driven scene editing.

At a higher level of generality, [arxiv_2511.18873] identifies a shared limitation across the 3DGS family: the representational capacity of individual Gaussian kernels is inherently constrained, limiting performance on tasks ranging from novel view synthesis to geometry reconstruction and 4D modelling. The paper proposes neural texture splatting, augmenting each Gaussian primitive with per-splat texture capacity to enhance local expressiveness. Unlike prior per-splat texture methods that target dense view synthesis with reduced primitive counts, this approach is designed to yield consistent gains across diverse reconstruction scenarios, including the dynamic settings addressed by [arxiv_2404.06270] and [arxiv_2405.17891]. In this sense, [arxiv_2511.18873] can be read as a unifying contribution, proposing a representational upgrade that is broadly applicable rather than task-specific.

Taken together, these works trace a coherent trajectory: from geometry-aware dynamic reconstruction, through memory-efficient temporal modelling, to mesh-guided interactive editing, and finally to expressive primitive representations that generalise across the full spectrum of 3DGS applications.

## Conclusion

The trajectory of 3D Gaussian Splatting research, as traced through this review, reflects a field in vigorous and productive expansion. Beginning from a compact set of foundational ideas—differentiable rasterization of anisotropic Gaussian primitives, adaptive density control, and spherical harmonic appearance modeling—the community has systematically extended the method's reach across nearly every dimension of practical and scientific relevance.

On the efficiency front, substantial progress has been made in reducing the memory and storage footprint of 3DGS through quantization, entropy coding, structured spatial representations, and neural compression, bringing the representation closer to deployment viability on resource-constrained platforms. Robustness research has broadened the capture conditions under which 3DGS can succeed, relaxing assumptions about input sharpness, camera calibration accuracy, and the density of available viewpoints—each advance expanding the set of real-world scenarios the method can address. Geometric fidelity, long a weakness relative to classical reconstruction pipelines, has seen meaningful improvement through regularization strategies and hybrid representations that couple Gaussian primitives with explicit surface constraints.

The exploration of alternative primitives, scene decomposition, and physically based rendering integration signals a maturing understanding that the original Gaussian formulation, while powerful, is one point in a broader design space. Meanwhile, extensions to dynamic scenes and diverse application domains demonstrate that 3DGS is not merely a novel-view synthesis technique but a general-purpose scene representation with broad utility.

Despite this progress, important challenges remain. Achieving consistently accurate geometry, handling highly complex lighting and material interactions, scaling gracefully to unbounded large-scale environments, and enabling semantically meaningful editing all represent active areas where current methods fall short of the ideal. The intersection of 3DGS with generative models and foundation models also presents an underexplored frontier with significant potential.

This review has aimed to provide a structured foundation for navigating this rich and rapidly evolving landscape, and it is hoped that the organizational framework offered here will assist researchers in identifying both the contributions of prior work and the most promising directions for future investigation.
