# 3D Gaussian Splatting (short)

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most consequential developments in computer vision and graphics in recent years. Introduced by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079], the method departs fundamentally from the neural implicit representations that had previously dominated the field, most notably Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), by representing scenes as collections of explicit 3D Gaussian ellipsoids rather than as position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks. This architectural choice enables efficient rendering through rasterization of Gaussian primitives into images, achieving real-time novel view synthesis at 1080p resolution at frame rates exceeding 30 fps while maintaining competitive training times and state-of-the-art visual quality [arxiv_2308.04079]. The significance of this contribution is difficult to overstate: as documented in comprehensive survey literature, 3DGS rapidly overtook NeRF-based research as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis following its introduction in August 2023, gaining tremendous momentum across the research community [arxiv_2210.00379].

To appreciate the novelty of 3DGS, it is useful to situate it within the broader landscape of radiance field methods. NeRF and its many derivatives rely on a principled ray-marching approach to volumetric rendering, querying a neural network at sampled points along each ray to accumulate color and density [arxiv_2210.00379]. While this approach yields high visual fidelity, it incurs substantial computational costs both during training and at inference time, making real-time deployment challenging. 3DGS shares a similar image formation model with NeRF in that it draws on volumetric rendering concepts, but it achieves its performance gains through a hybrid rendering solution that combines the strengths of volume rendering with the efficiency of primitive rasterization [arxiv_2502.19318]. This hybridization necessarily involves a set of approximations with respect to volumetric rendering theory, and a natural question—addressed directly by recent work—is whether replacing these approximations with more principled solutions would improve reconstruction quality [arxiv_2502.19318]. The answer is nuanced: the approximations embedded in the original 3DGS formulation are not merely engineering shortcuts but are integral to its performance profile, and the relationship between rendering accuracy and visual quality is more complex than a straightforward substitution would suggest.

The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation confers advantages beyond rendering speed. As noted in survey literature, the explicit scene parameterization facilitates a range of downstream editing tasks including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation, tasks that are considerably more difficult to perform on neural implicit representations [arxiv_2403.11134]. This versatility has driven a rapid proliferation of 3DGS-based methods across diverse application domains, and recent literature reviews have organized this growing body of work into broad categories of 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications [arxiv_2403.11134].

Despite its many strengths, the original 3DGS formulation exhibits several well-documented limitations that have motivated a substantial body of follow-on research. One of the most prominent concerns is memory consumption. Because each Gaussian primitive stores per-point parameters—including position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent appearance—scenes represented by millions of Gaussians impose significant memory overhead during both training and storage [arxiv_2312.04564]. This challenge is compounded by the fact that high-quality scene representation typically requires large numbers of primitives, creating a tension between representational fidelity and resource efficiency. Multiple lines of work have sought to address this tension through complementary strategies. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] proposes quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements alongside a coarse-to-fine training strategy that produces sparser Gaussian point clouds through a pruning stage, yielding faster training and rendering without proportional quality loss. F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083] takes a different approach inspired by classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques, representing and approximating dense clusters of Gaussians through factorized coordinates and representations to drastically reduce storage requirements while preserving image quality. These two approaches are broadly complementary: EAGLES targets the per-Gaussian parameter footprint through quantization and pruning, while F-3DGS targets the structural redundancy among clusters of Gaussians through factorization.

A further approach to the memory problem is explored in HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083], which proposes a hybrid scene representation that combines explicit Gaussians with grid-based neural fields. The key insight is that while recent compression methods attempt to encode all Gaussian properties within neural fields, they struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details. HyRF addresses this by decomposing the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians that store only critical high-frequency parameters, while grid-based neural fields predict the remaining properties. This hybrid strategy can be contrasted with the pure compression approaches of EAGLES and F-3DGS: rather than compressing the Gaussian representation uniformly, HyRF selectively retains explicit parameterization where it matters most and delegates smoother, lower-frequency properties to the neural field component. The result is a method that aims to capture the best of both worlds—the memory efficiency of neural field compression and the fine-detail fidelity of explicit Gaussian representation [arxiv_2509.17083].

Beyond memory, the quality of the training data itself poses challenges for 3DGS. The method assumes sharp, well-exposed input images, and its performance degrades significantly when training images are blurry due to lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake [arxiv_2401.00834]. This is a practically important limitation because blur is ubiquitous in real-world image capture. Deblurring 3DGS [arxiv_2401.00834] addresses this by extending the 3DGS framework to model and compensate for blur during training, enabling the synthesis of clean, sharp novel views from degraded inputs. This work highlights a broader theme in the 3DGS literature: the original method's assumptions about input quality and scene structure must often be relaxed or augmented to handle the diversity of real-world capture conditions.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reflects a field in rapid evolution. The foundational contribution of 3DGS [arxiv_2308.04079] established a new performance frontier for real-time novel view synthesis, and subsequent work has systematically addressed its limitations in memory efficiency [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2405.17083, arxiv_2509.17083], rendering accuracy [arxiv_2502.19318], and robustness to imperfect inputs [arxiv_2401.00834]. The breadth of downstream applications enabled by the explicit Gaussian representation continues to expand [arxiv_2403.11134], and the relationship between 3DGS and its NeRF predecessors remains an active area of comparative investigation [arxiv_2210.00379].
