# 3D Gaussian Splatting (short)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most significant shifts in the field of 3D scene representation in recent years. To appreciate the magnitude of this shift, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of neural rendering research, particularly in relation to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which had previously established itself as the leading framework for implicit scene representation.

NeRF, introduced in 2020, fundamentally changed how the computer vision community approached novel view synthesis by encoding scene geometry and appearance within the weights of a neural network, queried through a ray-marching volumetric rendering procedure [arxiv_2210.00379]. For several years, NeRF-based methods dominated the field, finding applications across robotics, autonomous navigation, and augmented reality, among others. However, despite their impressive visual fidelity, NeRF models are computationally expensive both to train and to render, making real-time performance at high resolutions largely unattainable [arxiv_2308.04079]. This limitation motivated the search for alternative representations that could preserve the visual quality of neural radiance fields while dramatically reducing computational overhead.

3D Gaussian Splatting, introduced by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079], addressed this challenge by replacing the implicit neural representation with an explicit set of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids. Rather than querying a neural network along sampled rays, 3DGS rasterizes these Gaussian primitives directly onto the image plane, enabling real-time novel view synthesis at 1080p resolution with frame rates exceeding 30 fps. The method initializes its Gaussian representation from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration and optimizes the position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients of each Gaussian to reproduce the appearance of the captured scene. This combination of competitive training times and high-quality real-time rendering marked a decisive departure from the speed-quality trade-offs that had constrained prior methods [arxiv_2308.04079].

The rapid adoption of 3DGS across the research community has been well documented. As surveyed in [arxiv_2403.11134], the field has expanded quickly into a wide range of downstream tasks, including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation. The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation is particularly advantageous for such editing applications, since individual primitives can be manipulated directly without the need to disentangle information encoded implicitly within network weights. This stands in contrast to NeRF-based approaches, where editing operations typically require more indirect and computationally demanding interventions [arxiv_2403.11134].

Despite its practical success, 3DGS relies on a hybrid rendering formulation that incorporates approximations relative to principled volumetric rendering theory. While it shares a similar image formation model with NeRF, 3DGS departs from strict ray-marching in favor of rasterization-based approximations that prioritize efficiency [arxiv_2502.19318]. This raises a fundamental question about whether the approximations embedded in the standard 3DGS pipeline limit its representational accuracy, and whether replacing them with more theoretically grounded volumetric rendering solutions could yield quality improvements. Recent work has begun to examine this question in depth, analyzing the specific approximations made in 3DGS and their consequences for rendering fidelity [arxiv_2502.19318].

Taken together, these developments illustrate a field in rapid transition. The displacement of NeRF as the dominant framework by 3DGS, as chronicled in comprehensive surveys of both paradigms [arxiv_2210.00379], reflects not merely a technical preference but a broader reorientation toward explicit, rasterizable representations that balance quality, speed, and editability. The ongoing interrogation of 3DGS's theoretical foundations [arxiv_2502.19318] suggests that, even as the method matures, fundamental questions about the relationship between rendering approximations and visual quality remain open and actively contested.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has prompted a substantial body of research addressing its core limitations, particularly those relating to memory efficiency, storage overhead, rendering quality under degraded inputs, and the tension between explicit and implicit representations. This subsection surveys these developments, tracing the principal directions researchers have pursued to extend and refine the foundational 3DGS framework.

A recurring concern in the literature is the considerable memory and storage burden imposed by 3DGS. Because the method represents scenes through millions of explicit 3D Gaussians, each carrying its own set of learnable parameters, the aggregate memory footprint can become prohibitive for deployment in resource-constrained or real-time settings. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] directly confronts this problem by introducing quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements, coupling this with a coarse-to-fine training strategy designed to accelerate and stabilize optimization. Crucially, EAGLES also incorporates a pruning stage that yields scene representations composed of fewer Gaussians, thereby improving both training speed and rendering throughput without sacrificing visual fidelity. A complementary approach is taken by F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], which draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a factorized form. By decomposing coordinates and representations, F-3DGS achieves substantial reductions in storage requirements while preserving image quality, positioning itself as a structurally distinct solution to the same fundamental problem that EAGLES addresses through quantization and pruning. Together, these works illustrate that storage compression in 3DGS can be pursued either through parameter-level quantization or through higher-level structural factorization, and that both strategies are viable paths toward more compact scene representations.

A related but distinct challenge concerns the interaction between explicit Gaussian representations and neural field components. HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] argues that neither pure explicit Gaussian representations nor purely neural-field-based compressions are fully satisfactory: the former incur heavy memory overhead due to per-Gaussian modeling of view-dependent effects and anisotropic shapes, while the latter struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details. In response, HyRF proposes a hybrid architecture that decomposes the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians retaining only critical high-frequency parameters, alongside grid-based neural fields that predict the remaining properties. This hybrid decomposition represents a methodological synthesis that seeks to inherit the rendering efficiency of explicit Gaussians while offloading low-frequency information to more compact neural representations, thereby addressing memory overhead without the quality degradation that purely neural compression methods tend to introduce.

Beyond compression and hybrid representation, researchers have also examined the robustness of 3DGS to imperfect training data. Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting [arxiv_2401.00834] identifies a significant vulnerability in the standard 3DGS pipeline: when training images are affected by blur arising from lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake, rendering quality degrades severely. This work situates itself within a broader tradition of neural-field-based approaches to clean image reconstruction from blurry inputs, while adapting and extending these ideas to the Gaussian splatting setting. The problem it addresses is orthogonal to the memory and compression concerns raised by EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564], F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], and HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083], yet it underscores that the practical deployment of 3DGS depends not only on computational efficiency but also on resilience to the noise and degradation inherent in real-world image capture.

Taken together, these works reflect a maturing research agenda in which the initial promise of 3DGS—real-time, high-quality novel view synthesis—is being systematically consolidated through targeted solutions to memory overhead, representational compactness, hybrid explicit-implicit design, and input robustness.
