# 3D Gaussian Splatting (medium)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS — evidence cluster A

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most consequential developments in real-time rendering and scene reconstruction in recent years. To appreciate the significance of this shift, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of radiance field methods and to examine both the foundational contributions that defined the approach and the subsequent body of work that has extended, critiqued, and refined it.

Prior to the introduction of 3DGS, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) established a compelling framework for novel view synthesis by encoding a 3D scene as a continuous volumetric function parameterized by a neural network, conditioned on spatial position and viewing direction. While NeRF-based methods demonstrated impressive visual fidelity, they suffered from substantial computational costs at both training and inference time, relying on ray-marching procedures that required dense sampling along each camera ray and expensive network evaluations at every sample point [arxiv_2308.04079]. Faster variants of NeRF were proposed over time, but these methods consistently required trading off rendering speed against reconstruction quality, and none achieved real-time display rates for complete, unbounded scenes at high resolutions such as 1080p [arxiv_2308.04079].

The seminal work introducing 3D Gaussian Splatting addressed these limitations directly by proposing a fundamentally different scene representation [arxiv_2308.04079]. Rather than encoding scene geometry and appearance implicitly within the weights of a neural network, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each characterized by a position, covariance matrix, opacity, and view-dependent color encoded via spherical harmonics. Rendering is accomplished by projecting, or "splatting," these Gaussians onto the image plane through a differentiable rasterization pipeline, enabling real-time synthesis at frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second at 1080p resolution while maintaining competitive training times and state-of-the-art visual quality [arxiv_2308.04079]. The initialization of Gaussians from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration via Structure-from-Motion provides a geometrically grounded starting point, and an adaptive densification strategy allows the representation to grow in complexity where the scene demands it.

The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation confers several advantages beyond rendering speed. As surveyed in [arxiv_2403.11134], the accessibility and manipulability of the underlying primitives facilitates a wide range of downstream tasks that are considerably more difficult with implicit neural representations. These include dynamic scene reconstruction, where Gaussians can be deformed or animated over time; geometry editing, where individual primitives can be added, removed, or transformed; and physical simulation, where the Gaussian ellipsoids can serve as proxies for physical objects. The survey in [arxiv_2403.11134] organizes the rapidly expanding literature around these functional categories—3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications—reflecting the breadth of impact that 3DGS has had across computer vision and graphics since its introduction.

Despite its practical success, the theoretical underpinnings of 3DGS have attracted scrutiny. A key observation is that while 3DGS shares a conceptual image formation model with NeRF—both ultimately seek to model the accumulation of radiance along viewing rays—the rendering pipeline in 3DGS departs from principled volumetric rendering in several important respects [arxiv_2502.19318]. Specifically, 3DGS employs a set of approximations relative to volumetric rendering theory, most notably in how Gaussian contributions are composited along the viewing direction. The rasterization-based approach treats the problem as one of ordered alpha compositing of projected 2D Gaussians rather than performing rigorous numerical integration along rays as NeRF does. This hybrid approach—drawing on the strengths of both volume rendering and primitive rasterization—is precisely what enables the performance gains that make 3DGS attractive in practice [arxiv_2502.19318].

The question of whether these approximations impose a ceiling on reconstruction quality is non-trivial. The analysis presented in [arxiv_2502.19318] investigates whether replacing the approximate rendering components of 3DGS with more theoretically grounded volumetric rendering solutions would yield measurable improvements in visual quality. This line of inquiry is important because it probes the extent to which the empirical success of 3DGS is attributable to the expressive power of the Gaussian primitive itself versus the specific rendering approximations employed. The findings contribute to a growing understanding that the approximations in 3DGS, while departing from volumetric rendering theory, are not necessarily detrimental to quality in the majority of practical scenarios, suggesting that the performance-quality trade-off struck by the original formulation is more favorable than one might expect from a theoretical standpoint [arxiv_2502.19318].

Taken together, these works delineate a research landscape in which 3DGS has rapidly transitioned from a novel rendering technique to a foundational representation underpinning a diverse ecosystem of methods. The original contribution [arxiv_2308.04079] established the core primitives and training procedure; subsequent surveys [arxiv_2403.11134] have mapped the expanding frontier of applications; and analytical investigations [arxiv_2502.19318] have begun to interrogate the theoretical assumptions embedded in the approach. This combination of empirical productivity and emerging theoretical scrutiny characterizes a maturing field, and it is within this context that further developments in 3DGS must be understood.

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS — evidence cluster B

The emergence of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) in 2020 marked a watershed moment in computer vision, introducing implicit, neural network-based scene representations capable of photorealistic novel view synthesis [arxiv_2210.00379]. Over the subsequent years, NeRF-based approaches proliferated rapidly, finding applications in robotics, urban mapping, autonomous navigation, and augmented and virtual reality environments. These methods demonstrated that volumetric rendering through learned radiance fields could produce compelling visual results across a wide range of scene types. However, the computational demands inherent to NeRF's reliance on neural networks and volumetric rendering imposed significant barriers to real-time deployment, limiting the practical utility of these approaches in latency-sensitive applications [arxiv_2401.00834].

The introduction of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) in August 2023 represented a fundamental shift in the landscape of novel view synthesis. Rather than relying on implicit neural representations, 3DGS models scenes using explicit, optimizable 3D Gaussian primitives that can be rendered in real time through a differentiable splatting pipeline [arxiv_2210.00379]. The approach rapidly gained momentum, overtaking NeRF-based research as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis in terms of community interest and publication volume. This transition reflects a broader recognition that explicit representations offer practical advantages in rendering speed and interpretability, even as they introduce distinct challenges related to memory consumption and robustness to imperfect input data.

One of the central tensions in the 3DGS literature concerns the trade-off between rendering quality and memory efficiency. Because 3DGS encodes view-dependent appearance effects and anisotropic shape information through per-Gaussian parameters, the memory overhead associated with large-scale or high-fidelity scenes can become prohibitive [arxiv_2509.17083]. Several works have attempted to address this limitation by compressing the Gaussian representation using neural fields, effectively offloading some of the representational burden from explicit parameters to learned functions. However, such compression strategies introduce their own difficulties: neural field-based compression tends to struggle with high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, resulting in degraded reconstruction of fine geometric and appearance details [arxiv_2509.17083]. This observation highlights a fundamental tension between compactness and fidelity that motivates hybrid approaches.

The Hybrid Radiance Fields (HyRF) framework proposed by [arxiv_2509.17083] addresses this tension directly by decomposing the scene into two complementary components: a compact set of explicit Gaussians that retain only the most critical high-frequency parameters, and grid-based neural fields responsible for predicting the remaining properties. This decomposition is designed to leverage the complementary strengths of explicit and implicit representations—the precision and rendering efficiency of Gaussians on one hand, and the compactness and generalization capacity of neural fields on the other. By selectively assigning high-frequency information to explicit primitives and delegating smoother, lower-frequency properties to the neural grid, HyRF aims to achieve both memory efficiency and high reconstruction quality. The approach exemplifies a broader trend in the field toward hybrid representations that resist the limitations of either purely explicit or purely implicit paradigms.

A separate but equally significant challenge for 3DGS concerns robustness to degraded input data, particularly motion blur and defocus blur arising from camera shake, object motion, or lens imperfections during image capture [arxiv_2401.00834]. Standard 3DGS optimization assumes sharp, clean training images, and the presence of blur in the input data leads to severe degradation in rendering quality. This problem is not unique to Gaussian splatting; prior work in the NeRF literature also grappled with blur, and several neural field methods were developed to synthesize clean, sharp images from blurry inputs. However, as [arxiv_2401.00834] notes, the majority of these earlier approaches were designed specifically for the volumetric rendering paradigm and do not transfer straightforwardly to the splatting-based framework. The deblurring challenge for 3DGS therefore requires dedicated solutions that account for the distinct rendering mechanics of Gaussian primitives, motivating a new line of research focused on integrating blur modeling directly into the Gaussian optimization process.

Taken together, these threads of research illustrate the dual nature of progress in 3DGS: on one side, efforts to reduce the memory footprint and improve scalability of the representation without sacrificing visual fidelity [arxiv_2509.17083]; on the other, efforts to extend the robustness of the framework to real-world capture conditions that violate the assumptions of idealized training pipelines [arxiv_2401.00834]. Both directions reflect the maturation of 3DGS from a proof-of-concept demonstration into a practical technology that must contend with the complexities of deployment at scale. The broader survey context provided by [arxiv_2210.00379] situates these developments within the longer arc of neural scene representation research, underscoring how the field has progressively shifted from implicit to explicit and hybrid paradigms in pursuit of the dual goals of quality and efficiency.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The rapid adoption of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) as a scene representation paradigm has been accompanied by a growing body of work addressing one of its most pressing practical limitations: the substantial memory and storage demands that arise from representing scenes with millions of individual Gaussian primitives. While 3D-GS offers compelling advantages over Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) in terms of training speed and real-time rendering capability, the sheer scale of its point cloud representations introduces significant overhead that constrains deployment in resource-limited and real-time environments. Researchers have consequently pursued a range of strategies to reduce these costs without sacrificing the visual fidelity that makes 3D-GS attractive in the first place.

One prominent line of inquiry focuses on compressing the per-point attribute storage associated with each Gaussian primitive. The EAGLES framework [arxiv_2312.04564] exemplifies this direction by introducing quantized embeddings to reduce the memory footprint of individual Gaussian points. Rather than storing full-precision attributes for every point in the cloud, EAGLES encodes these attributes in a compact quantized form, achieving meaningful reductions in per-point storage requirements. Crucially, this compression strategy is paired with a coarse-to-fine training procedure designed to stabilize and accelerate the optimization of the Gaussian point cloud. The coarse-to-fine approach allows the model to first establish a rough structural approximation of the scene before refining finer geometric and appearance details, which the authors argue leads to both faster convergence and more stable training dynamics. Additionally, EAGLES incorporates a dedicated pruning stage that removes redundant or low-contribution Gaussians from the representation, resulting in sparser scene models. This pruning not only reduces storage but also directly accelerates rendering, since fewer primitives must be rasterized per frame. The combined effect of quantization, structured training, and pruning positions EAGLES as a holistic compression pipeline rather than a single-axis optimization.

A complementary but structurally distinct approach is taken by F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], which draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to address the storage problem. Where EAGLES operates primarily at the level of individual point attributes, F-3DGS targets the dense clusters of Gaussians that collectively describe regions of a scene, proposing a factorized representation that approximates these clusters more compactly. The core intuition is that the coordinates and appearance attributes of spatially proximate Gaussians are not independent but exhibit structured redundancy that can be exploited through factorization. By decomposing these dense clusters into lower-rank factors, F-3DGS achieves substantial reductions in storage overhead while preserving image quality. This approach is notably different in spirit from the quantization strategy of EAGLES: whereas EAGLES compresses the representation of individual Gaussians, F-3DGS restructures the collective representation of groups of Gaussians, effectively changing the granularity at which compression is applied.

Both works share a common motivation rooted in the recognized limitations of vanilla 3D-GS. As [arxiv_2312.04564] and [arxiv_2405.17083] each observe, the requirement to maintain millions of Gaussians per scene imposes memory costs that are difficult to reconcile with deployment on resource-constrained hardware or in applications demanding real-time performance. NeRF-based methods, despite their own computational burdens, typically encode scene information in compact neural network weights rather than explicit point clouds, and this architectural difference means that 3D-GS trades one form of overhead for another. The works reviewed here represent efforts to recover some of the compactness of implicit representations while retaining the rendering speed advantages of explicit Gaussian splatting.

There are, however, meaningful differences in the trade-offs each method navigates. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] emphasizes not only storage reduction but also training acceleration, presenting the coarse-to-fine strategy and pruning as mechanisms that improve the efficiency of the optimization process itself, not merely the size of the final model. F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], by contrast, frames its contribution primarily in terms of storage mitigation, with the factorization approach serving as a post-hoc or integrated structural change to how scene geometry and appearance are encoded. This distinction suggests that the two approaches may be more complementary than competing: quantization and pruning of individual Gaussian attributes could in principle be combined with cluster-level factorization to achieve compounding compression benefits.

Taken together, these works illustrate a broader trend in the 3D-GS literature toward efficiency-oriented extensions that seek to democratize the use of Gaussian splatting across a wider range of hardware platforms and application scenarios. The challenge of balancing representational fidelity against memory and computational cost remains an active area of investigation, and the strategies introduced by [arxiv_2312.04564] and [arxiv_2405.17083] represent important early contributions to what is likely to become an increasingly rich subfield of research.

### Evidence theme 3

The development of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has prompted substantial research into its foundational mechanisms, optimization strategies, and the theoretical underpinnings that distinguish it from prior volumetric and neural rendering approaches. Understanding the core algorithmic and representational choices embedded in 3DGS requires situating the method within the broader landscape of differentiable rendering and point-based graphics, where the tension between rendering fidelity, computational efficiency, and scene editability has long been a central concern.

At its heart, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of anisotropic three-dimensional Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by a position, covariance matrix, opacity, and view-dependent color encoded through spherical harmonics. This explicit, unstructured representation contrasts sharply with the implicit volumetric fields popularized by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), where scene geometry and appearance are encoded in the weights of a multilayer perceptron queried along camera rays. The shift from implicit to explicit representation carries profound consequences for both training dynamics and inference speed, as the rasterization-based rendering pipeline employed by 3DGS avoids the costly per-ray numerical integration that bottlenecks NeRF-style methods. The tile-based rasterizer introduced alongside the original 3DGS formulation sorts Gaussians by depth and composites their contributions in a front-to-back order, enabling real-time rendering at high resolutions without sacrificing the photorealistic quality that neural approaches had established as a benchmark.

A critical aspect of the 3DGS pipeline is the adaptive density control mechanism, which governs how the set of Gaussian primitives evolves during optimization. Primitives are periodically cloned in regions where gradients indicate underfitting and split when individual Gaussians become too large to faithfully represent fine geometric detail. This adaptive refinement strategy allows the representation to allocate expressive capacity where it is most needed, concentrating primitives in texturally complex or geometrically intricate regions while leaving simpler areas sparsely populated. The interplay between this densification procedure and the photometric loss driving optimization is subtle: because the rendering is fully differentiable with respect to all Gaussian parameters, gradients flow back through the rasterizer to update positions, covariances, opacities, and spherical harmonic coefficients simultaneously, making the joint optimization of geometry and appearance a defining feature of the approach.

The choice of Gaussian primitives as the atomic unit of scene representation is motivated by several mathematical conveniences. Gaussians project analytically onto the image plane under perspective projection, yielding two-dimensional Gaussian splats whose contributions can be evaluated efficiently without sampling. The covariance parameterization, decomposed into a rotation matrix and a scaling vector to ensure positive semi-definiteness, provides a flexible shape prior that can approximate both thin surface-like structures and volumetric fog-like regions. This geometric flexibility, combined with the smooth differentiability of the Gaussian function, makes the representation amenable to gradient-based optimization from multi-view photometric supervision alone, without requiring explicit depth or normal supervision.

Despite these advantages, the explicit nature of 3DGS introduces challenges that have motivated considerable follow-on research. The memory footprint of storing millions of Gaussian primitives with their full parameter sets can be substantial, and the unstructured point cloud initialization inherited from Structure-from-Motion pipelines means that the quality of the final reconstruction is sensitive to the density and coverage of the initial point set. Regions with insufficient initial coverage may remain poorly reconstructed even after densification, as the optimization landscape for placing new primitives in textureless or occluded areas is poorly conditioned. Furthermore, the tile-based rasterizer, while highly efficient, introduces approximations in the handling of semi-transparent Gaussians and can produce artifacts in scenes with complex transparency or fine specular highlights.

The relationship between 3DGS and earlier point-based rendering methods is also worth examining. Classical point splatting techniques projected surface samples onto the image plane and blended their contributions using reconstruction kernels, but these methods lacked the differentiability and view-dependent appearance modeling that modern neural rendering demands. 3DGS can thus be understood as a synthesis of classical splatting efficiency with the differentiable, appearance-aware optimization paradigm pioneered by neural rendering, inheriting the strengths of both lineages while introducing new challenges specific to the Gaussian parameterization.

The scalability of 3DGS to large or unbounded scenes has also emerged as a significant research question. The original formulation assumes a bounded scene captured from a relatively dense set of calibrated cameras, and extending it to outdoor environments, forward-facing captures, or scenes with significant depth variation requires modifications to the initialization strategy, the densification heuristics, and sometimes the rendering pipeline itself. The sensitivity of the method to camera pose accuracy further underscores the importance of the upstream reconstruction pipeline, as errors in the Structure-from-Motion estimates propagate directly into the Gaussian placement and covariance optimization.

Collectively, these foundational considerations establish 3DGS as a rich and rapidly evolving research area, where advances in representation design, optimization theory, and rendering algorithms are tightly coupled. The explicit, differentiable, and rasterization-friendly nature of the Gaussian primitive representation provides a strong foundation for downstream applications in novel view synthesis, scene editing, and dynamic scene modeling, while simultaneously posing open questions about scalability, robustness, and the theoretical limits of Gaussian-based scene approximation.
