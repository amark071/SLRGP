# 3D Gaussian Splatting (medium)

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most consequential shifts in the recent history of scene representation and rendering research. To appreciate the significance of this development, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of radiance field methods, examine the foundational technical choices that distinguish it from its predecessors, and survey the expanding body of work that has sought to extend, refine, and apply it across a wide range of problems. This subsection reviews the literature on 3DGS with particular attention to its flat, scene-level reconstruction capabilities, its relationship to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), and the principal challenges—including memory overhead, rendering fidelity, surface quality, and robustness to imperfect inputs—that have motivated subsequent research.

Neural Radiance Fields, introduced in 2020, established a compelling framework for implicit scene representation in which a multilayer perceptron (MLP) maps continuous spatial coordinates and viewing directions to volumetric density and view-dependent color [arxiv_2210.00379]. The photorealistic quality achievable by NeRF-based methods, combined with their ability to model fine geometric detail and view-dependent appearance, drove an enormous wave of follow-on research spanning robotics, urban mapping, autonomous navigation, and augmented reality [arxiv_2210.00379]. However, the computational demands of NeRF are substantial: rendering a single image requires querying the MLP hundreds of times per ray, making real-time performance on commodity hardware essentially unattainable in the original formulation [ss_478166]. Efforts to accelerate NeRF, such as baking the trained network into a Sparse Neural Radiance Grid (SNeRG) that can be rendered in real time [ss_478166], demonstrated that the implicit representation could be partially decoupled from the rendering bottleneck, but such approaches introduced their own trade-offs in flexibility and editability.

Against this backdrop, 3D Gaussian Splatting was introduced in 2023 as a fundamentally different approach to radiance field rendering [arxiv_2308.04079]. Rather than encoding scene information in the weights of a neural network, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of explicit 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterized by a position, covariance matrix, opacity, and view-dependent color expressed through spherical harmonics. Rendering proceeds by rasterizing these Gaussian primitives into image space through a differentiable tile-based splatting pipeline, enabling real-time novel view synthesis at 1080p resolution and frame rates exceeding 30 fps [arxiv_2308.04079]. The method is initialized from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration and optimized end-to-end through a combination of photometric loss and adaptive density control, which dynamically splits and prunes Gaussians to match scene complexity [arxiv_2308.04079]. This combination of explicit representation, fast differentiable rasterization, and adaptive optimization yielded state-of-the-art visual quality with competitive training times, and the method rapidly displaced NeRF as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis in terms of research interest [arxiv_2210.00379].

The success of 3DGS has been analyzed from multiple perspectives. A comprehensive literature review of recent 3DGS methods classifies the growing body of work into three broad functional categories: 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and downstream applications [arxiv_2403.11134]. This taxonomy reflects the versatility of the explicit Gaussian representation, which facilitates not only high-quality rendering but also scene manipulation tasks such as dynamic reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation—tasks that are considerably more difficult with implicit neural representations [arxiv_2403.11134]. The explicit, point-based nature of 3DGS means that individual primitives can be identified, transformed, and composed in ways that are natural for graphics pipelines, a property that has been exploited extensively in the editing literature.

A recurring theme in the 3DGS literature is the tension between rendering speed and fidelity on one hand, and memory and storage requirements on the other. Because 3DGS represents scenes with potentially millions of Gaussian primitives—each carrying multiple parameters including position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients—the memory footprint of a trained model can be substantial [arxiv_2502.19457]. This stands in contrast to NeRF, where scene information is compressed into network weights, albeit at the cost of slow inference. The survey on compression in 3DGS provides a comprehensive overview of the methods, trends, and future directions in this space, noting that the storage requirements of 3DGS pose significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices [arxiv_2502.19457]. Several strategies have been proposed to address this issue, including quantization, pruning, and factorized representations.

EAGLES (Efficient Accelerated 3D Gaussians with Lightweight EncodingS) addresses memory overhead through quantized embeddings that significantly reduce per-point storage requirements, combined with a coarse-to-fine training strategy and a pruning stage that reduces the total number of Gaussians [arxiv_2312.04564]. The result is a more compact scene representation that achieves faster training and rendering speeds while maintaining competitive visual quality [arxiv_2312.04564]. A complementary approach is taken by F-3DGS (Factorized 3D Gaussian Splatting), which draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to represent and approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a factorized form, drastically reducing storage requirements while preserving image quality [arxiv_2405.17083]. Both EAGLES and F-3DGS illustrate a broader trend toward compact representations that retain the rendering advantages of 3DGS while mitigating its storage costs, a challenge that the compression survey identifies as one of the central open problems in the field [arxiv_2502.19457].

A different approach to the memory problem is taken by HyRF (Hybrid Radiance Fields), which combines explicit Gaussians with grid-based neural fields [arxiv_2509.17083]. HyRF decomposes the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians that store only critical high-frequency parameters, while the remaining properties are predicted by neural fields. This hybrid architecture is motivated by the observation that while recent works propose compressing 3DGS with neural fields, these methods often struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details [arxiv_2509.17083]. By retaining explicit Gaussians for the most spatially variable parameters and offloading smoother properties to neural fields, HyRF seeks to achieve both memory efficiency and high reconstruction fidelity. This approach represents a convergence between the NeRF and 3DGS paradigms, suggesting that the dichotomy between implicit and explicit representations may be less sharp in practice than it appears in principle.

The question of whether 3DGS's approximations to volumetric rendering theory are consequential for reconstruction quality has been examined in depth by a recent analytical study [arxiv_2502.19318]. While 3DGS shares a similar image formation model with NeRF—both model scenes as volumetric densities that are integrated along rays—3DGS employs a hybrid rendering solution that combines volume rendering with primitive rasterization, introducing a set of approximations relative to principled volumetric rendering [arxiv_2502.19318]. The study investigates whether replacing these approximations with more accurate volumetric rendering solutions improves quality, providing a systematic analysis of the trade-offs involved. This work is important for understanding the theoretical foundations of 3DGS and for identifying where the method's practical performance diverges from what volumetric rendering theory would predict.

Surface reconstruction quality is another dimension along which 3DGS has been found wanting. Because Gaussian primitives are volumetric and diffuse by nature, they do not naturally align with sharp geometric boundaries or flat surfaces. Without hand-crafted regularizers, Gaussians tend to disperse irregularly around the actual surface, making it difficult to extract clean meshes or accurately represent hard edges [arxiv_2411.14974]. This limitation has motivated the development of alternative primitive types. 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) replaces Gaussian primitives with 3D smooth convexes, which offer greater geometric flexibility and can more accurately represent flat surfaces and sharp edges [arxiv_2411.14974]. By modeling scenes as compositions of smooth convex shapes rather than Gaussians, 3DCS aims to produce geometrically meaningful radiance fields that better capture the underlying scene structure. Similarly, TRIPS (Trilinear Point Splatting) combines ideas from Gaussian splatting and the ADOP point-based rendering framework, rasterizing points into a screen-space image pyramid and using a lightweight neural reconstruction network to address the blurring and cloudy artifacts that can afflict 3DGS in highly detailed scenes [arxiv_2401.06003]. These alternative primitive approaches suggest that the choice of scene primitive is a significant design dimension with implications for both rendering quality and geometric fidelity.

The challenge of surface reconstruction in open scenes is specifically addressed by GVKF (Gaussian Voxel Kernel Functions), which establishes a continuous scene representation based on discrete 3DGS through kernel regression [arxiv_2411.01853]. The motivation for this approach is that while 3DGS uses an explicit and discrete representation that enables fast rasterization, the reconstructed surface built from a large number of Gaussian primitives leads to excessive memory consumption and rough surface details in sparse Gaussian areas [arxiv_2411.01853]. GVKF integrates fast 3DGS rasterization with effective implicit scene representations, achieving high-fidelity surface reconstruction in open scenes. This work exemplifies a recurring strategy in the 3DGS literature: using the explicit Gaussian representation as a fast rendering backbone while augmenting it with continuous or implicit components to address its geometric limitations.

The robustness of 3DGS to imperfect training data is a practical concern of considerable importance, since real-world image capture frequently involves motion blur, defocus blur, and other degradations. Standard 3DGS suffers from severe degradation in rendering quality when training images are blurry, because the optimization conflates the blur present in the training images with the scene appearance [arxiv_2401.00834]. Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting addresses this problem by incorporating blur modeling into the 3DGS optimization framework, enabling the recovery of sharp scene representations from blurry inputs [arxiv_2401.00834]. A related approach, BAD-Gaussians (Bundle Adjusted Deblur Gaussian Splatting), leverages the explicit Gaussian representation to jointly optimize scene structure and camera motion trajectories from motion-blurred images [arxiv_2403.11831]. BAD-Gaussians is motivated by the observation that while NeRF-based deblurring methods exist, the implicit representation of NeRF struggles to accurately recover intricate details from severely motion-blurred images and cannot achieve real-time rendering [arxiv_2403.11831]. By contrast, the explicit Gaussian representation enables more accurate detail recovery and real-time rendering, demonstrating a concrete advantage of 3DGS over NeRF in this challenging setting.

The few-shot novel view synthesis problem—reconstructing a scene from a small number of input images—poses particular difficulties for 3DGS, which relies on dense multi-view supervision to accurately place and optimize Gaussian primitives. SCGaussian (Structure Consistent Gaussian Splatting) addresses this challenge by incorporating matching priors to learn 3D consistent scene structure from sparse inputs [arxiv_2411.03637]. The method optimizes scene structure in two complementary ways: through rendering geometry and through the positions of Gaussian primitives, which are difficult to constrain directly in vanilla 3DGS due to its non-structural nature [arxiv_2411.03637]. A hybrid Gaussian representation is introduced to enable this structural consistency, illustrating how the flexibility of the Gaussian primitive can be both a strength and a limitation depending on the data regime.

Dynamic scene reconstruction represents one of the most active and challenging frontiers for 3DGS. While the original 3DGS formulation is designed for static scenes, many real-world applications require modeling scenes with moving objects or deforming surfaces. A refined 3D Gaussian representation for dynamic scene reconstruction addresses this by using a deformable MLP network to capture the dynamic offset of Gaussian points and expressing color features through hash encoding [arxiv_2405.17891]. This approach builds on the insight that the explicit, point-based nature of 3DGS is well-suited to dynamic reconstruction, since individual Gaussians can be tracked and deformed over time in a manner that is more interpretable than deforming an implicit neural field [arxiv_2403.11134].

The editability of 3DGS representations has also been a major focus of research. Mani-GS (Gaussian Splatting Manipulation with Triangular Mesh) tackles the challenge of achieving manipulable photo-realistic rendering by coupling 3D Gaussians with a triangular mesh, enabling fine-grained control over scene geometry and appearance [arxiv_2405.17811]. The motivation for this work is that while 3DGS achieves extremely high-fidelity novel view synthesis with fast training and rendering, there is still a lack of effective means to manipulate 3DGS freely while maintaining rendering quality [arxiv_2405.17811]. By binding Gaussians to a mesh, Mani-GS inherits the controllability of mesh-based representations while retaining the rendering quality of 3DGS, addressing a key limitation of both pure mesh-based and pure Gaussian-based approaches.

Inverse rendering—the joint estimation of scene geometry, materials, and illumination from images—is another area where 3DGS has been applied and compared to NeRF-based methods. PBR-NeRF addresses the limitation that most NeRF and 3DGS approaches estimate view-dependent appearance without modeling scene materials and illumination, proposing an inverse rendering model that jointly estimates geometry, materials, and illumination using physics-based priors [arxiv_2412.09680]. The inclusion of 3DGS in this comparison reflects the recognition that both paradigms share the fundamental limitation of conflating appearance with illumination in the absence of explicit material modeling.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals a field that has moved rapidly from the foundational 3DGS formulation [arxiv_2308.04079] to a rich ecosystem of methods addressing its principal limitations: memory and storage overhead [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2405.17083, arxiv_2502.19457], surface reconstruction quality [arxiv_2411.14974, arxiv_2411.01853], robustness to imperfect inputs [arxiv_2401.00834, arxiv_2403.11831], few-shot generalization [arxiv_2411.03637], dynamic scene modeling [arxiv_2405.17891], editability [arxiv_2405.17811], and the theoretical foundations of the rendering approximations involved [arxiv_2502.19318]. The hybrid approaches that combine explicit Gaussians with neural fields [arxiv_2509.17083, arxiv_2411.01853] suggest a convergence between the NeRF and 3DGS paradigms, while alternative primitive formulations [arxiv_2411.14974, arxiv_2401.06003] indicate that the Gaussian ellipsoid, though highly effective, is not the only viable primitive for radiance field rendering. The breadth and pace of this research activity underscore the centrality of 3DGS to contemporary scene representation research and motivate continued investigation into its capabilities and limitations.

The preceding synthesis establishes the core trajectory of 3DGS research, yet several important threads merit further elaboration, particularly concerning generalizable reconstruction, illumination modeling, alternative primitive designs, robotics applications, and the broader competitive landscape with respect to NeRF-based methods.

A significant limitation of per-scene optimized 3DGS is its inability to generalize across scenes without retraining from scratch. Feed-forward approaches that predict Gaussian parameters directly from image observations address this bottleneck. pixelSplat introduces a feed-forward model that reconstructs 3D radiance fields parameterized by Gaussian primitives from pairs of images, enabling real-time and memory-efficient rendering at inference time without per-scene optimization [arxiv_2312.12337]. To overcome the local minima inherent to sparse and locally supported representations, pixelSplat predicts a dense probability distribution over 3D space and samples Gaussian means from it, making the approach scalable to large datasets [arxiv_2312.12337]. This generalizable paradigm contrasts sharply with the per-scene optimization of vanilla 3DGS and points toward a future in which Gaussian-based representations can be deployed in settings where rapid scene understanding is required, such as autonomous navigation or augmented reality. Complementary efforts in the sparse-view regime, such as depth-aware Gaussian splatting for few-shot novel view synthesis, incorporate monocular depth predictions as geometric priors alongside scale-invariant depth loss formulations to regularize Gaussian placement when only a handful of training views are available [arxiv_2410.11080]. Similarly, MVPGS excavates multi-view priors to constrain the Gaussian optimization under sparse input conditions, demonstrating that the integration of learned geometric priors is a broadly effective strategy for mitigating the overfitting tendencies of 3DGS in data-scarce regimes [arxiv_2409.14316]. CoR-GS approaches the same problem from a co-regularization perspective, training two Gaussian radiance fields simultaneously and exploiting their disagreement as an unsupervised signal for identifying and correcting poorly reconstructed regions [arxiv_2405.12110]. Collectively, these sparse-view methods reveal that the explicit, point-based nature of 3DGS, while advantageous for rendering efficiency, introduces particular vulnerability to underconstrained optimization that requires careful regularization strategies beyond those needed for implicit NeRF representations.

The treatment of view-dependent appearance and illumination within 3DGS has evolved considerably beyond the original spherical harmonic parameterization. The choice of optimizing the outgoing radiance of each Gaussian independently as spherical harmonics produces unsatisfactory view-dependent effects, particularly for scenes with complex specular reflections or spatially varying illumination [arxiv_2408.03753]. 3iGS addresses this by introducing factorized tensorial illumination, replacing per-Gaussian spherical harmonics with a shared illumination representation that better captures global lighting structure [arxiv_2408.03753]. A complementary direction is pursued by 6DGS, which introduces a six-dimensional spatial-angular Gaussian representation to more faithfully model view-dependent radiance, demonstrating improved rendering quality for scenes with strong directional lighting effects [arxiv_2410.04974]. Neural Texture Splatting augments each Gaussian primitive with per-splat texture capacity, substantially increasing the representational expressiveness of the 3DGS framework for both static and dynamic reconstruction tasks [arxiv_2511.18873]. These illumination-aware extensions are particularly relevant in the context of inverse rendering, where disentangling appearance from material and lighting properties is essential for applications such as relighting and material editing. The convergence of 3DGS with physics-based rendering principles, as exemplified by works like PBR-NeRF [arxiv_2412.09680], suggests that the boundary between appearance-based and physically-grounded scene representations is becoming increasingly porous.

Beyond Gaussian ellipsoids, a growing body of work investigates alternative primitive geometries that may better capture certain scene properties. 3D Convex Splatting replaces Gaussian kernels with smooth convex primitives, arguing that convexes are better suited to representing flat surfaces and sharp edges without requiring a prohibitively large number of primitives [arxiv_2411.14974]. 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting introduces asymmetric half-Gaussian kernels as a plug-and-play replacement for standard Gaussians, addressing the difficulty of representing shape and color discontinuities that arises from the symmetric, smooth nature of Gaussian functions [arxiv_2406.02720]. Triangle Splatting revisits the classical triangle mesh primitive within a differentiable splatting framework, arguing that triangles offer complementary advantages in terms of surface compactness and compatibility with established graphics pipelines [arxiv_2505.19175]. TRIPS (Trilinear Point Splatting) offers yet another alternative, combining point-based splatting with trilinear interpolation to reduce the blurring and cloudy artifacts that afflict standard 3DGS in highly detailed scenes [arxiv_2401.06003]. Splat the Net introduces splattable neural primitives that combine the real-time efficiency of splatting with the representational power of neural formulations, positioning itself explicitly in the design space between NeRF and 3DGS [arxiv_2510.08491]. The proliferation of these alternative primitives underscores that the Gaussian ellipsoid, while highly effective as a general-purpose primitive, represents one point in a broader design space, and that the optimal primitive geometry is likely scene- and task-dependent.

The application of 3DGS to robotics has emerged as a particularly active area, motivated by the need for dense, real-time scene representations that can support tasks such as navigation, manipulation, and scene understanding. A dedicated survey of 3DGS in robotics documents the advantages of the explicit Gaussian representation over NeRF-based alternatives for robotic applications, noting that the real-time rendering capability and explicit scene structure of 3DGS are especially valuable in dynamic, interactive environments [arxiv_2410.12262]. The COLMAP-free variant of 3DGS, which jointly optimizes camera poses and scene structure without relying on pre-computed structure-from-motion, is particularly relevant for robotic deployment where accurate camera calibration may not be available in advance [arxiv_2312.07504]. The exploitation of scene structure, such as repetitive elements, further extends the applicability of 3DGS to real-world environments where training view coverage is inevitably incomplete [arxiv_2506.06462]. Virtual reality represents another demanding application domain, where the high frame rates and foveated rendering requirements of head-mounted displays push the performance boundaries of 3DGS; VR-Splatting addresses this by combining Gaussian splatting with neural point rendering in a foveated rendering framework that allocates computational resources according to gaze direction [arxiv_2410.17932].

Rendering efficiency and model compactness remain persistent concerns as 3DGS is deployed in increasingly resource-constrained settings. Speedy-Splat identifies and addresses two key inefficiencies in the standard 3DGS rasterization pipeline—redundant pixel coverage and redundant primitive evaluation—achieving substantial improvements in rendering speed alongside reductions in model size [arxiv_2412.00578]. The self-organizing Gaussian grid approach organizes Gaussian parameters into a structured 2D grid with local coherence, enabling aggressive compression without sacrificing rendering quality [arxiv_2312.13299]. Compact 3D Gaussian Splatting for both static and dynamic radiance fields demonstrates that the compactness gains achievable through structured representations extend naturally to the dynamic setting, where the number of Gaussians required to represent a scene over time would otherwise grow prohibitively large [arxiv_2408.03822]. These efficiency-oriented contributions are complementary to the compression survey perspective offered by [arxiv_2502.19457], which frames the memory and storage challenge as a central bottleneck for the practical deployment of 3DGS across diverse platforms. Taken together, they suggest that the path toward truly ubiquitous deployment of Gaussian-based scene representations will require continued co-design of primitive representations, rendering algorithms, and compression strategies, rather than optimization of any single component in isolation.
