# 3D Gaussian Splatting (medium)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most significant shifts in the field of 3D scene representation and rendering in recent years. To appreciate the magnitude of this shift, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of radiance field research, examine its foundational technical contributions, and survey the expanding body of work that has sought to extend, refine, and critically interrogate its core assumptions.

Prior to the advent of 3DGS, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) constituted the dominant framework for novel view synthesis. Introduced in 2020, NeRF demonstrated that a continuous volumetric scene representation could be encoded implicitly within the weights of a multilayer perceptron, conditioned on spatial position and viewing direction, and rendered via differentiable ray marching [arxiv_2210.00379]. The photorealistic quality achievable by NeRF-based methods attracted enormous research interest, spawning a rich ecosystem of extensions addressing speed, scalability, dynamic scenes, and generalization [arxiv_2210.00379]. Nevertheless, a persistent limitation of NeRF and its derivatives remained the computational cost of volumetric rendering: querying a neural network at hundreds of sample points along each ray during both training and inference imposed substantial burdens that made real-time rendering at high resolutions largely infeasible [arxiv_2308.04079].

The seminal work introducing 3D Gaussian Splatting directly addressed this bottleneck [arxiv_2308.04079]. Rather than encoding scene geometry and appearance implicitly within a neural network, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of explicit 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterized by a mean position, a covariance matrix encoding shape and orientation, an opacity value, and view-dependent color coefficients typically expressed via spherical harmonics. Rendering proceeds not through ray marching but through a differentiable tile-based rasterization pipeline that projects the 3D Gaussians onto the image plane and composites their contributions in depth order. This approach preserves many of the desirable properties of continuous volumetric radiance fields for scene optimization while enabling real-time novel view synthesis at 1080p resolution at frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second, a threshold that no prior method had achieved for unbounded, complete scenes [arxiv_2308.04079]. The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation also facilitates a range of downstream tasks, including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation, that are considerably more cumbersome with implicit neural representations [arxiv_2403.11134].

The rapid adoption of 3DGS has prompted comprehensive survey efforts to organize the growing literature. Reviewing recent advances, [arxiv_2403.11134] classifies the expanding body of 3DGS methods into three broad functional categories: 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications. This taxonomy reflects the versatility of the Gaussian primitive as a scene representation and underscores how the explicit, editable nature of 3DGS distinguishes it from NeRF-based approaches in practical deployment contexts. Similarly, [arxiv_2210.00379] documents the broader historical arc, noting that following the introduction of Gaussian Splatting in August 2023, research interest rapidly shifted away from NeRF as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis, with 3DGS-based work overtaking NeRF-based research in terms of community momentum.

Despite its considerable strengths, 3DGS is not without limitations, and a growing body of work has sought to identify and address these shortcomings. One important line of inquiry concerns the theoretical foundations of the rendering pipeline itself. While 3DGS shares a conceptual image formation model with NeRF, it achieves its performance through a set of approximations relative to principled volumetric rendering theory [arxiv_2502.19318]. The question of whether replacing these approximations with more rigorous volumetric rendering solutions would improve reconstruction quality is non-trivial. [arxiv_2502.19318] presents an in-depth analysis of this question, examining the extent to which the approximations embedded in the standard 3DGS pipeline affect visual fidelity and whether the practical benefits of the hybrid rasterization approach can be retained while moving closer to volumetric rendering principles. This work highlights a fundamental tension in the design of 3DGS: the approximations that enable its remarkable speed may simultaneously impose a ceiling on achievable quality in certain scenarios.

Memory efficiency represents another axis along which 3DGS has attracted critical scrutiny. Because each Gaussian primitive carries its own set of per-primitive parameters to model view-dependent appearance and anisotropic shape, scenes with complex geometry and appearance can require a very large number of Gaussians, resulting in substantial memory overhead [arxiv_2509.17083]. Attempts to compress 3DGS representations using neural fields have been proposed, but such methods can struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine geometric and appearance details [arxiv_2509.17083]. The Hybrid Radiance Fields (HyRF) framework addresses this challenge by decomposing the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians that retain only the most critical high-frequency parameters, while grid-based neural fields predict the remaining properties [arxiv_2509.17083]. This hybrid strategy illustrates a broader trend in the field toward combining the complementary strengths of explicit primitive-based representations and implicit neural fields, seeking to balance rendering speed, memory footprint, and reconstruction fidelity in ways that neither paradigm achieves alone.

The quality of training data also has a pronounced effect on 3DGS performance. Because the optimization of Gaussian parameters is driven by photometric consistency with training images, degraded inputs such as blurry photographs introduce significant challenges. Blur arising from lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake is common in real-world image acquisition and leads to severe degradation in rendering quality when 3DGS is trained naively on such data [arxiv_2401.00834]. Prior work on deblurring within neural field frameworks had begun to address this issue, but the architectural differences between NeRF and 3DGS necessitate tailored solutions [arxiv_2401.00834]. The development of deblurring extensions for 3DGS thus represents an important practical contribution, extending the applicability of the framework to the imperfect imaging conditions encountered outside controlled laboratory settings.

Taken together, these works paint a picture of a field in rapid evolution. The foundational contribution of [arxiv_2308.04079] established 3DGS as a viable and highly competitive alternative to NeRF by demonstrating that explicit Gaussian primitives, combined with efficient rasterization, could achieve real-time high-quality rendering. Subsequent survey work [arxiv_2403.11134, arxiv_2210.00379] has documented the breadth of applications and extensions that followed. Meanwhile, more targeted investigations have probed the rendering approximations underlying 3DGS [arxiv_2502.19318], proposed hybrid architectures to address memory and detail-capture limitations [arxiv_2509.17083], and extended the framework to handle degraded training inputs [arxiv_2401.00834]. This body of literature collectively defines the current state of 3DGS research and motivates continued investigation into its theoretical foundations, practical limitations, and potential for integration with complementary representation strategies.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) as a prominent paradigm for novel-view synthesis has catalyzed a substantial body of research aimed at addressing its core computational and storage limitations. While 3D-GS offers compelling advantages over Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) in terms of rendering speed and training efficiency, the method's reliance on millions of explicit Gaussian primitives to represent individual scenes introduces significant memory and storage overhead that constrains its applicability in resource-limited and real-time deployment contexts. The literature has responded to these challenges along several converging lines of inquiry, including memory-efficient encoding, structural compression through factorization, and optimized training strategies that reduce the number of Gaussians required without sacrificing reconstruction fidelity.

A foundational tension in the 3D-GS literature concerns the trade-off between representational fidelity and computational economy. The original formulation of 3D-GS achieves real-time rendering through differentiable rasterization of Gaussian point clouds, a process that is substantially faster than the volumetric ray-marching procedures characteristic of NeRF-based approaches [arxiv_2312.04564]. However, this speed advantage comes at a cost: the point cloud representations employed by 3D-GS typically require millions of individual Gaussians per scene, each carrying attributes such as position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients. The cumulative memory footprint of these per-point attributes during both training and inference is substantial, and it scales with scene complexity in ways that can render deployment on consumer-grade hardware or mobile platforms impractical [arxiv_2405.17083]. This observation has motivated researchers to treat memory efficiency not as a secondary concern but as a primary design objective in the development of next-generation Gaussian splatting systems.

One influential direction in addressing these limitations involves the application of quantized embeddings to reduce per-point storage requirements. The EAGLES framework [arxiv_2312.04564] exemplifies this approach, proposing a technique in which the attributes associated with each Gaussian primitive are represented through compact quantized encodings rather than full-precision floating-point values. By reducing the bit-width of stored attributes, EAGLES achieves significant reductions in memory consumption without requiring a fundamental restructuring of the underlying Gaussian representation. Crucially, this compression strategy is paired with a coarse-to-fine training procedure designed to stabilize and accelerate the optimization of Gaussian point clouds. The coarse-to-fine strategy addresses a known instability in Gaussian splatting optimization, wherein the simultaneous adjustment of large numbers of Gaussian parameters can lead to slow convergence or suboptimal local minima. By progressively refining the representation from a coarser initialization, EAGLES promotes more stable gradient flow and faster convergence. The framework also incorporates a dedicated pruning stage that eliminates redundant or low-contribution Gaussians from the scene representation, yielding sparser point clouds that support faster training and rendering speeds while maintaining competitive image quality [arxiv_2312.04564].

A complementary but structurally distinct approach to the storage problem is pursued by Factorized 3D Gaussian Splatting (F-3DGS) [arxiv_2405.17083]. Rather than compressing individual Gaussian attributes through quantization, F-3DGS draws on classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to represent dense clusters of Gaussians in a more compact factorized form. The core insight motivating this approach is that the spatial and attribute distributions of Gaussians within a scene are not independent: nearby Gaussians tend to share similar geometric and appearance properties, and this redundancy can be exploited through low-rank factorization to achieve substantial reductions in storage overhead. By decomposing the representation of Gaussian clusters into factorized coordinate and attribute components, F-3DGS approximates the original dense representation with far fewer stored parameters, thereby reducing the storage burden while preserving the visual quality of synthesized views [arxiv_2405.17083].

Comparing these two approaches reveals a meaningful methodological divergence. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] operates at the level of individual Gaussian primitives, applying quantization to compress per-point attributes and pruning to reduce the total count of primitives. This strategy preserves the explicit, point-based structure of the Gaussian representation and integrates naturally with the standard 3D-GS rendering pipeline, requiring relatively modest modifications to the underlying framework. F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], by contrast, intervenes at a higher structural level, reorganizing the representation of Gaussian clusters through factorization in a manner inspired by classical dimensionality reduction techniques. This approach has the potential to capture inter-Gaussian correlations more systematically than per-point compression, but it also introduces additional complexity in the representation and rendering pipeline, as the factorized components must be reconstructed or evaluated during inference. Both methods share the overarching goal of reducing the storage and memory costs of 3D-GS while maintaining rendering quality, and both demonstrate that the explicit, attribute-rich nature of Gaussian primitives creates multiple avenues for compression that do not require abandoning the core splatting paradigm.

It is also worth noting that both EAGLES and F-3DGS situate their contributions explicitly within the broader context of the NeRF-versus-3D-GS comparison that has structured much of the recent novel-view synthesis literature. Both works acknowledge that 3D-GS was itself developed as a response to the high computational costs of NeRF, offering faster training and real-time rendering at the expense of increased memory usage [arxiv_2312.04564; arxiv_2405.17083]. Their contributions can therefore be understood as second-order optimizations that seek to preserve the speed advantages of 3D-GS while mitigating the storage disadvantages that distinguish it from implicit neural representations. This framing underscores a broader pattern in the field, wherein successive generations of methods inherit and refine the trade-offs introduced by their predecessors rather than resolving them entirely.

Taken together, the works reviewed here illuminate the central role that memory and storage efficiency play in the ongoing development of 3D Gaussian Splatting as a practical technology for real-time scene synthesis. The convergence of quantization-based compression [arxiv_2312.04564] and factorization-based structural reduction [arxiv_2405.17083] suggests that the field is exploring a diverse solution space, with different methods offering complementary advantages depending on the deployment context and the specific trade-offs between compression ratio, rendering speed, and reconstruction fidelity that a given application demands.

### Evidence theme 3

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a real-time, high-fidelity scene representation has prompted a substantial body of research aimed at extending its capabilities beyond static scene reconstruction. This subsection surveys the principal directions along which 3DGS has been developed, refined, and applied, drawing attention to the methodological innovations and comparative trade-offs that characterise the current state of the field.

A foundational concern in 3DGS research is the quality and efficiency of the underlying representation itself. The original formulation relies on a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussians whose parameters—position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients—are optimised through differentiable rasterisation. Subsequent work has interrogated each of these components. Researchers have observed that the adaptive densification strategy used to grow the Gaussian population can produce redundant or poorly conditioned primitives, motivating principled pruning and regularisation schemes that improve compactness without sacrificing visual fidelity. Parallel efforts have examined the spherical harmonic basis used to encode view-dependent appearance, proposing higher-order or alternative representations to capture complex specular effects that low-order harmonics cannot faithfully reproduce. These representational refinements collectively push the Pareto frontier of the quality–efficiency trade-off that is central to real-time rendering applications.

A second major strand of research addresses the scalability of 3DGS to large and unbounded scenes. Indoor and outdoor environments of practical interest extend far beyond the bounded volumes that early implementations handled comfortably. Strategies for partitioning large scenes into manageable sub-regions, training local Gaussian fields independently, and compositing them at inference time have been proposed to overcome GPU memory constraints. Such hierarchical or block-wise approaches introduce new challenges around boundary consistency and level-of-detail management, and different works make different choices about how aggressively to merge or prune Gaussians at block boundaries. The tension between local optimisation quality and global scene coherence remains an active research question, with some methods prioritising seamless transitions and others accepting minor boundary artefacts in exchange for greater parallelism during training.

Dynamic scene reconstruction represents one of the most actively explored extensions of 3DGS. Because Gaussians are explicit geometric primitives, they are in principle amenable to deformation: one can attach a motion field to the canonical Gaussian set and warp primitives through time. Early dynamic extensions parameterised per-Gaussian deformations using multi-layer perceptrons conditioned on time, effectively coupling the explicit Gaussian representation with an implicit neural component. This hybrid strategy inherits the rendering speed of splatting while leveraging the expressive capacity of neural networks for complex non-rigid motion. Alternative approaches avoid neural deformation fields entirely by optimising per-frame Gaussian parameters directly, which simplifies the training pipeline but scales poorly with sequence length. A third family of methods draws on the 4D Gaussian concept, extending the spatial covariance to a spatio-temporal covariance that naturally encodes motion and deformation within the primitive itself. Comparative evaluations suggest that purely explicit per-frame methods excel on short sequences with limited motion, whereas neural deformation field approaches generalise better to longer sequences and more complex dynamics, though at the cost of increased training time and reduced interpretability of the learned motion representation.

The integration of 3DGS with generative models constitutes another prominent research direction. Text-to-3D and image-to-3D generation pipelines have adopted Gaussian representations as their output domain, motivated by the differentiability of the splatting renderer and the speed advantage over NeRF-based alternatives during score distillation or diffusion-guided optimisation. Because Gaussian primitives are initialised from sparse point clouds or random seeds rather than dense geometry, generation methods must contend with the initialisation sensitivity of the optimisation landscape. Some works address this by incorporating geometric priors from monocular depth estimators or multi-view diffusion models to provide a more informative starting point. Others propose coarse-to-fine optimisation schedules that first establish global structure before refining local appearance details. The interplay between the stochastic nature of diffusion-based supervision and the deterministic, explicit character of Gaussian primitives introduces unique training dynamics that differ substantially from those encountered in NeRF-based generation, and the field has not yet converged on a single dominant paradigm.

Semantic and compositional understanding of Gaussian scenes has attracted growing attention as researchers seek to move beyond passive novel-view synthesis toward interactive and editable representations. By associating semantic feature vectors with individual Gaussians—either by distilling features from foundation models such as CLIP or DINO, or by training auxiliary feature fields alongside the appearance model—it becomes possible to perform open-vocabulary segmentation, object selection, and scene editing directly in the Gaussian domain. Editing operations such as object removal, insertion, and style transfer can be implemented by manipulating subsets of Gaussians identified through semantic queries, a workflow that is considerably more interpretable than the equivalent operation on an implicit neural field. However, feature distillation introduces a tension between the compactness of the Gaussian representation and the high dimensionality of modern vision-language embeddings, and different works resolve this tension through dimensionality reduction, quantisation, or hierarchical feature aggregation with varying degrees of success.

Avatar and human body reconstruction using 3DGS has emerged as a specialised but rapidly growing sub-field. Parametric body models such as SMPL provide a structured deformation space that can be used to drive Gaussian primitives attached to a canonical body surface, enabling animatable human avatars that render in real time. Compared with neural radiance field approaches to avatar modelling, Gaussian-based methods typically achieve faster rendering and more stable training, though they can struggle with loose clothing and hair that deviates significantly from the underlying body surface. Some methods augment the parametric deformation with learned residual displacements to handle such non-rigid elements, while others represent clothing as a separate Gaussian layer with its own dynamics. The choice of how tightly to couple the Gaussian representation to the parametric body model reflects a broader design philosophy: tighter coupling yields more controllable and generalisable avatars, whereas looser coupling allows greater expressive freedom at the cost of reduced editability.

Compression and efficient transmission of 3DGS scenes have also received attention, motivated by the practical need to deploy Gaussian-based representations on bandwidth-constrained or compute-limited devices. The number of Gaussians required to represent a high-quality scene can reach into the millions, and the associated parameter storage is non-trivial. Vector quantisation, entropy coding, and neural compression schemes have all been applied to reduce the storage footprint, with different methods achieving different points on the rate–distortion curve. Some compression approaches are tightly integrated with the training process, applying quantisation-aware optimisation from the outset, while others treat compression as a post-processing step applied to a fully trained scene. The former tend to achieve better rate–distortion performance but require more complex training pipelines, whereas the latter offer greater flexibility and compatibility with existing 3DGS training frameworks.

Taken together, these research directions illustrate both the versatility of the 3DGS paradigm and the breadth of open problems that remain. The explicit, differentiable, and real-time-renderable nature of Gaussian primitives makes them an attractive substrate for a wide range of vision and graphics tasks, and the pace of methodological innovation suggests that the field will continue to expand in scope and sophistication in the near term.
