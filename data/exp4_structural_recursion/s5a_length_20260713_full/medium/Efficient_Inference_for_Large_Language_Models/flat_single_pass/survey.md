# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (medium)

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently has become one of the most actively researched problems in contemporary natural language processing and machine learning systems. As transformer-based architectures have scaled to hundreds of billions of parameters, the gap between raw model capability and practical deployability has widened considerably, motivating a rich and rapidly expanding body of literature on inference optimization. This subsection surveys the principal directions in that literature, drawing connections across quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, architectural search, key-value cache management, speculative decoding, and system-level acceleration, while situating each within the broader context of the efficiency challenge.

Several comprehensive surveys have framed the problem space in complementary ways. The survey by [arxiv_2404.14294] identifies three root causes of inefficient LLM inference: the large model size, the quadratic complexity of the attention operation with respect to sequence length, and the inherently sequential nature of autoregressive decoding. This tripartite framing is useful because it maps naturally onto distinct families of solutions: compression techniques address model size, attention approximations and cache management address quadratic complexity, and speculative or parallel decoding strategies address the sequential bottleneck. A similarly broad treatment is offered by [arxiv_2402.09748], which organizes the field into quantization, pruning, distillation, compact architecture design, and dynamic networks, while emphasizing a characteristic that distinguishes LLMs from their smaller predecessors: the prohibitive cost of fine-tuning or retraining after compression. This observation has profound practical implications, because it renders many classical compression pipelines—which assume a relatively cheap post-compression fine-tuning step—inapplicable at scale without significant adaptation. The survey by [arxiv_2408.03130] reinforces this perspective, noting that the sheer scale and complexity of LLMs present unique challenges that have prompted researchers to explore novel training, optimization, and deployment methods simultaneously. Together, these surveys establish that efficient LLM inference is not a single technical problem but a constellation of interrelated challenges spanning algorithms, hardware, and systems.

Quantization is arguably the most widely studied compression technique for LLMs, and its prominence in the literature reflects both its practical effectiveness and the theoretical interest it generates. The fundamental idea is to represent model weights and, in some formulations, activations using lower-precision numerical formats, thereby reducing memory footprint and potentially accelerating arithmetic operations. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is particularly attractive for LLMs precisely because it avoids the need for retraining, addressing the fine-tuning cost concern raised by [arxiv_2402.09748]. However, PTQ at very low bit-widths introduces non-trivial accuracy degradation, a tension that has driven substantial methodological innovation. The ABQ-LLM framework [arxiv_2408.08554] addresses this tension directly by proposing an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm that achieves superior performance across various quantization settings and enables efficient arbitrary-precision quantized inference on GPU hardware. A key insight motivating ABQ-LLM is that existing GPU integer computing units support only a limited set of precision types, meaning that quantized matrix operations at non-standard precisions cannot be straightforwardly accelerated; the framework introduces mechanisms to overcome this hardware constraint. This work thus illustrates a recurring theme in the quantization literature: the co-design of algorithmic precision choices and hardware execution strategies is essential for realizing theoretical speedups in practice.

The challenge of quantization is further complicated when fine-tuning is required after compression. The PEQA method [arxiv_2305.14152] addresses this by combining parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with quantized LLMs, updating only the quantization scales during adaptation. This approach achieves sub-4-bit integer quantization while preserving the ability to perform task-specific fine-tuning at low memory cost, effectively bridging the gap between deployment-phase quantization and the need for task adaptation. The work highlights that quantization and fine-tuning need not be treated as sequential, independent steps; instead, they can be jointly designed to yield models that are both compressed and adaptable. For CPU deployment specifically, [arxiv_2311.00502] demonstrates that INT4 weight-only quantization, combined with a custom LLM runtime featuring highly optimized sparse and quantized kernels, can achieve practical inference efficiency on commodity hardware for models such as Llama2 and GPT-NeoX. This is significant because it extends the reach of quantization-based acceleration beyond specialized GPU clusters to more widely available computing infrastructure.

Pruning constitutes another major family of compression techniques, operating by identifying and removing redundant parameters or structural components from the model. The survey [arxiv_2402.09748] notes that pruning, like quantization, faces the challenge of post-compression fine-tuning costs at LLM scale. Hardware-aware pruning has emerged as a particularly practical variant, as demonstrated in the Fast DistilBERT pipeline [arxiv_2211.07715], which combines hardware-aware pruning with knowledge distillation, quantization, and a custom inference runtime to achieve minimal accuracy loss with substantial throughput improvements on CPUs. This work is instructive in showing that pruning is most effective when integrated into a holistic pipeline rather than applied in isolation, a lesson that generalizes beyond the BERT-scale models studied there to the LLM setting. The efficiency optimization analysis by [arxiv_2405.11704] similarly situates pruning within a broader set of strategies, evaluating its contribution alongside quantization and architectural modifications to the overall inference efficiency of large-scale language models.

Knowledge distillation offers a complementary approach to compression, training a smaller student model to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher. While distillation has a long history in the deep learning literature, its application to LLMs raises specific challenges related to the cost of generating soft targets from very large teachers and the difficulty of matching complex emergent capabilities. The survey [arxiv_2408.03130] discusses distillation as one of the primary techniques for reducing resource requirements, noting that it must be carefully designed to preserve the task-relevant knowledge encoded in the teacher's representations. The Fast DistilBERT work [arxiv_2211.07715] provides a concrete example of distillation integrated with other compression techniques, demonstrating that the combination can yield models suitable for production deployment under strict latency constraints. More broadly, the literature reviewed in [arxiv_2402.09748] and [arxiv_2404.14294] suggests that distillation is most powerful when combined with other compression methods, as the student model can be simultaneously trained to be compact, quantization-friendly, and structurally efficient.

An important and somewhat counterintuitive perspective on the relationship between model size and efficiency is offered by [arxiv_2002.11794], which argues that the most compute-efficient training strategy is to train extremely large models but stop after a relatively small number of iterations. The key empirical finding is that wider and deeper transformer models converge in significantly fewer steps than smaller models, and this acceleration in convergence typically outpaces the additional per-iteration computational overhead of larger models. This "train large, then compress" paradigm has influenced subsequent work by establishing that the starting point for compression need not be a model trained to full convergence; instead, a partially trained large model may serve as a better compression target than a fully trained small model. This insight connects to the broader theme of co-designing training and inference strategies rather than treating them as independent phases.

Architectural search and low-rank methods represent a more recent and increasingly prominent direction in LLM compression. The work on training-free architecture search [arxiv_2409.17372] proposes a framework for identifying optimal subnets that preserve the fundamental strengths of the original LLM while achieving inference acceleration, arguing that most compression techniques concentrate on weight optimization while overlooking the exploration of optimal architectures. This is a meaningful critique: quantization and pruning modify the values or sparsity of weights within a fixed architectural template, whereas architecture search asks whether a different structural configuration might achieve the same capability at lower cost. The integration of low-rank adapters with neural architecture search (NAS) [arxiv_2501.16372] extends this direction by combining low-rank representations—which reduce the effective dimensionality of weight matrices—with weight-sharing super-networks that enable efficient search over architectural configurations. The resulting approach is argued to democratize LLM deployment by producing models with reduced memory footprints and faster inference times suitable for resource-constrained environments. Compared to pure weight optimization approaches, these architecture-aware methods offer the potential for more fundamental efficiency gains, though they typically require more sophisticated search procedures and careful validation to ensure that the discovered architectures generalize across tasks.

The key-value (KV) cache has emerged as a distinct and critical bottleneck in LLM inference, warranting dedicated attention in the literature. During autoregressive generation, the transformer's attention mechanism requires storing the key and value projections of all previously processed tokens, and this cache grows linearly with sequence length and batch size. For long-context generation, the KV cache can dominate GPU memory consumption, severely limiting the batch sizes and sequence lengths that can be served in practice. A comprehensive review of KV cache compression techniques [arxiv_2508.06297] systematically examines strategies including selective token retention, quantization of cached tensors, and attention compression, evaluating their trade-offs in terms of memory usage and inference speed. The review identifies compatibility issues with different model architectures and the challenge of maintaining generation quality under aggressive compression as key open problems.

Several specific KV cache optimization methods have been proposed to address these challenges. The CORM method [arxiv_2404.15949] introduces a cache eviction policy based on two empirical observations: adjacent tokens' query vectors exhibit striking similarity, and the attention calculation for the current query can rely on a small fraction of preceding queries' attention information. By exploiting these observations, CORM dynamically retains only essential key-value pairs, considerably reducing the memory footprint without requiring modifications to the model architecture. This approach exemplifies the broader strategy of exploiting structural regularities in attention patterns to achieve compression. The InfiniGen framework [arxiv_2406.19707] takes a different approach, focusing on long-text generation by speculatively prefetching only the essential KV entries for each attention layer. The key insight is that the important tokens for computing attention in a subsequent layer can be predicted by performing a minimal rehearsal using the inputs of the current layer and partial weight information from the next layer, enabling selective prefetching that reduces memory pressure while maintaining generation quality. InfiniGen is designed to work synergistically with offloading-based inference systems, where KV cache entries are stored in CPU memory and fetched to GPU as needed, making it particularly relevant for deployment scenarios where GPU memory is severely constrained.

The Layer-Condensed KV Cache method [arxiv_2405.10637] proposes a structurally different solution: rather than evicting or compressing entries within each layer's cache, it computes and caches KV pairs for only a small number of layers, with other layers reusing these cached representations. This approach achieves up to 26 times higher throughput than standard transformers in reported experiments, with competitive performance on language modeling and downstream tasks. Importantly, the method is described as orthogonal to other memory-saving techniques, meaning it can be combined with quantization or eviction-based approaches for further gains. Comparing CORM [arxiv_2404.15949], InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707], and the Layer-Condensed approach [arxiv_2405.10637], one observes a spectrum of strategies: CORM operates within the standard layer structure by selectively evicting tokens, InfiniGen uses speculative prefetching to manage offloaded caches, and the Layer-Condensed method restructures the caching architecture itself. Each approach makes different assumptions about the deployment environment and the acceptable trade-off between memory savings and computational overhead.

Speculative decoding addresses the sequential bottleneck in autoregressive generation, which is identified by [arxiv_2404.14294] as one of the three primary causes of inference inefficiency. The core idea is to use a smaller, faster draft model to propose candidate token sequences, which are then verified in parallel by the larger target model. If the draft tokens are accepted, multiple tokens are generated in a single forward pass of the large model, effectively amortizing its computational cost. The SPIN system [arxiv_2503.15921] advances this paradigm by addressing three limitations of existing speculative decoding approaches: the use of homogeneous draft models that cannot adapt to requests of varying difficulty, limited support for batch processing, and insufficient joint optimization of the speculation and verification phases. SPIN introduces heterogeneous small speculative models with a learning-based selection algorithm that matches draft model complexity to request difficulty, improving both token acceptance rates and overall throughput. This work illustrates that speculative decoding, while conceptually simple, requires careful systems engineering to realize its theoretical benefits in practical serving scenarios with diverse workloads.

System-level and hardware-aware optimizations form an essential complement to algorithmic compression techniques. The tutorial by [arxiv_2407.09111] provides a comprehensive discussion of inference optimization on AI accelerators, covering tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, memory management, and operator fusion as complementary techniques to model compression. The observation that hardware-software co-design is necessary for realizing efficiency gains is echoed across multiple works: [arxiv_2211.07715] demonstrates this for CPU deployment with custom sparse and quantized kernels, [arxiv_2408.08554] addresses GPU-specific constraints on integer precision types, and [arxiv_2311.00502] shows that INT4 quantization requires a purpose-built runtime to achieve practical speedups on CPU hardware. The broader survey [arxiv_2401.02038] situates these system-level concerns within the full lifecycle of LLM development, covering parallel computation, memory scheduling, and structural optimization as components of a comprehensive inference deployment strategy.

The literature also reflects a growing recognition that efficiency optimization must be evaluated holistically rather than through single-metric comparisons. Compression techniques that reduce memory footprint may increase latency if they introduce irregular memory access patterns; quantization that accelerates matrix multiplication may degrade accuracy in ways that require compensatory fine-tuning; architectural modifications that improve throughput may reduce flexibility for downstream adaptation. The survey [arxiv_2404.14294] addresses this by including comparative experiments on representative methods, enabling more grounded assessments of relative effectiveness. Similarly, [arxiv_2408.03130] emphasizes the importance of understanding the practical applications and unique challenges of each optimization method, rather than treating them as interchangeable tools. The efficiency optimization study [arxiv_2405.11704] contributes to this holistic perspective by analyzing both training-phase and inference-phase optimizations together, recognizing that decisions made during training—such as the use of mixed-precision training and adaptive optimization algorithms—have downstream consequences for inference efficiency.

Taken together, the literature on efficient LLM inference reveals a field characterized by rapid methodological diversification, increasing systems sophistication, and a growing emphasis on co-design across algorithmic, architectural, and hardware dimensions. The flat landscape of techniques—quantization, pruning, distillation, architectural search, KV cache management, speculative decoding, and system-level optimization—is not a collection of independent solutions but an interconnected set of complementary approaches, each addressing different facets of the efficiency challenge identified by [arxiv_2404.14294]. The most impactful recent works, such as ABQ-LLM [arxiv_2408.08554], SPIN [arxiv_2503.15921], InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707], and the low-rank NAS integration [arxiv_2501.16372], share a common characteristic: they move beyond single-technique optimization to address the interactions between algorithmic choices, hardware constraints, and deployment requirements. This integrative orientation is likely to define the next phase of progress in efficient LLM inference.

The convergence of compression techniques with fine-tuning workflows represents one of the more practically consequential developments in the efficient inference literature. A recurring challenge is that post-training compression often degrades task-specific performance, necessitating additional adaptation steps that themselves impose memory and compute costs. The L4Q framework [arxiv_2402.04902] directly confronts this tension by integrating quantization-aware training with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), enabling simultaneous reduction of inference costs and training overhead. This joint formulation avoids the sequential pipeline of first compressing and then fine-tuning, which can introduce compounding approximation errors. Similarly, AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858] explores quantization-aware parameter-efficient adaptation for large pre-trained language models, demonstrating that the benefits of low-precision computation can be preserved even when the model undergoes downstream task adaptation. The work of [arxiv_2305.14152] extends this line of inquiry to sub-4-bit integer quantization, showing that memory-efficient fine-tuning remains feasible at extremely low bit widths when combined with appropriate PEFT methods. Collectively, these contributions suggest that the boundary between compression and adaptation is increasingly artificial: the most resource-efficient deployment pipelines treat quantization, low-rank approximation, and task-specific fine-tuning as jointly optimizable objectives rather than sequential post-processing steps.

The challenge of evaluating inference efficiency in a rigorous and reproducible manner has received comparatively less attention than the development of new optimization techniques, yet it is no less important for translating research advances into practical deployment decisions. The coarse-to-fine evaluation framework proposed by [arxiv_2404.11502] addresses this gap by providing a structured methodology for comparing optimization algorithms and code libraries across multiple granularities of efficiency measurement, from high-level throughput metrics to fine-grained operator-level profiling. This work highlights that users frequently find it difficult to compare the effectiveness of competing methods because evaluations are conducted under inconsistent conditions, with different hardware configurations, batch sizes, and sequence length distributions. The TokenSim simulation framework [arxiv_2503.08415] complements this by enabling hardware and software co-exploration for LLM inference systems before physical deployment, allowing practitioners to profile the interaction between model configuration, batching strategy, and accelerator characteristics in a controlled environment. The importance of such tooling is underscored by the observation in [arxiv_2408.03130] that the practical impact of an optimization technique depends heavily on the specific deployment context, and that techniques which appear superior under one set of conditions may underperform under another. Establishing shared evaluation protocols and simulation infrastructure is therefore a prerequisite for the field to move from demonstrating isolated improvements to enabling principled system design.

Hardware-aware memory controller design represents an emerging direction that complements software-level compression by addressing the physical bottlenecks of memory bandwidth and capacity at the architectural level. The compression-aware memory controller proposed in [arxiv_2503.18869] enhances on-chip memory management in AI accelerators to work synergistically with quantization and pruning, achieving further reductions in memory bottlenecks beyond what software techniques alone can provide. This approach reflects a broader recognition, also articulated in [arxiv_2407.09111], that the efficiency gains achievable through algorithmic compression are ultimately bounded by the memory subsystem of the underlying hardware, and that co-designing compression schemes with memory controller behavior can unlock additional performance headroom. The KV Pareto framework [arxiv_2512.01953] similarly adopts a systems-level perspective by jointly optimizing KV cache quantization, chunked prefill strategies, and model weight quantization, mapping the Pareto frontier between total memory consumption and generation quality for edge deployment scenarios. This joint optimization perspective stands in contrast to the predominant practice of tuning individual compression dimensions in isolation, and the empirical results reported suggest that the interaction effects between these dimensions are non-negligible, with suboptimal configurations arising when each component is tuned independently.

Speculative decoding has continued to evolve beyond its original formulation, with several works addressing the practical limitations that arise when deploying it in production serving systems. The Minions framework [arxiv_2402.15678] introduces aggregated speculative execution, where multiple small draft models operate concurrently to generate candidate tokens, improving the robustness of the speculation phase across diverse input distributions. The challenge of aligning draft model outputs with the behavioral characteristics of instruction-tuned or chat-fine-tuned target models is addressed in [arxiv_2403.00858], which proposes direct alignment of draft models to reduce the distribution mismatch that degrades token acceptance rates in practice. The ALISE system [arxiv_2410.23537] approaches the serving efficiency problem from a scheduling perspective, introducing speculative scheduling to mitigate head-of-line blocking in first-come-first-serve serving queues, demonstrating that inference efficiency is not solely a function of per-request computation but also of how requests are ordered and batched at the system level. Together, these works illustrate that speculative decoding is maturing from a theoretical acceleration mechanism into a family of practically deployable techniques, each addressing a distinct failure mode of the original approach.

The search for efficient architectures, as distinct from the compression of existing ones, has gained renewed momentum through the integration of neural architecture search with modern LLM design constraints. The work of [arxiv_2409.17372] argues that most compression research focuses on weight optimization while neglecting the exploration of optimal architectures, and proposes search methods that identify structurally efficient models rather than merely compressing overparameterized ones. The synergy between low-rank representations and weight-sharing neural architecture search explored in [arxiv_2501.16372] extends this perspective by demonstrating that LoRA-style adapters can serve not only as fine-tuning tools but as structural components within a NAS framework, enabling simultaneous optimization of model capacity and inference cost. The broader survey of model compression for resource-constrained environments [arxiv_2505.02309] situates these architectural search approaches within the full taxonomy of compression techniques, noting that knowledge distillation, quantization, and pruning each have characteristic failure modes that architectural optimization can partially circumvent. The empirical study in [arxiv_2404.05741] contributes a concrete finding to this discussion, demonstrating that selectively skipping attention sublayers in later transformer blocks is a surprisingly effective and low-cost architectural modification, suggesting that not all components of a large model contribute equally to output quality and that targeted structural simplification can yield meaningful efficiency gains without the overhead of full NAS procedures. The "train large, then compress" paradigm examined in [arxiv_2002.11794] provides an important historical anchor for these discussions, establishing that wider and deeper models converge to better solutions per unit of compute even when the final deployment target is a compressed model, a finding that continues to motivate the practice of training at scale before applying structural reduction.
