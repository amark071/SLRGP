# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) across a wide range of natural language processing tasks has been accompanied by a growing recognition that their computational and memory demands present serious obstacles to practical deployment. As these models have scaled to billions of parameters, the gap between their impressive capabilities and the feasibility of running them efficiently on real-world hardware has widened considerably. This tension has motivated a substantial body of research aimed at reducing the resource requirements of LLM inference without sacrificing the quality of model outputs. Several comprehensive surveys and focused empirical studies have sought to map this landscape, and together they reveal both the breadth of available techniques and the persistent challenges that remain unresolved.

A foundational observation shared across the literature is that the inefficiency of LLM inference stems from a small number of structural causes. As articulated by [arxiv_2404.14294], the three primary sources of inefficiency are the sheer size of modern models, the quadratic complexity of the attention operation with respect to sequence length, and the sequential nature of autoregressive decoding. This diagnostic framing is useful because it clarifies why no single optimization technique is sufficient on its own: each of these bottlenecks calls for a different class of solution, and practical deployment typically requires combining multiple approaches. [arxiv_2402.09748] similarly situates the problem in terms of the prohibitive memory and computational costs incurred during inference, and notes that while the algorithmic categories used to address these costs—quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, compact architecture design, and dynamic networks—are broadly shared with the literature on smaller models, LLMs introduce two distinctive complications. First, the cost of fine-tuning or retraining after compression is dramatically higher at scale, which constrains the applicability of many classical compression pipelines. Second, LLMs exhibit emergent capabilities that may be disproportionately sensitive to compression, making it harder to predict how a given technique will affect downstream task performance.

The taxonomy proposed by [arxiv_2404.14294], which organizes the literature into data-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations, offers a useful complement to the more algorithm-centric classification of [arxiv_2402.09748]. The data-level perspective draws attention to techniques such as prompt compression and efficient context management, which reduce the effective input size before computation begins. Model-level optimizations encompass the more familiar methods of quantization, pruning, and distillation, as well as architectural modifications that reduce the inherent cost of a forward pass. System-level approaches, by contrast, focus on how inference is scheduled and executed across hardware, including memory management strategies, batching policies, and parallelism. This multi-level view is echoed in [arxiv_2401.02038], which covers parallel computation, memory scheduling, and structural optimization alongside model compression, and emphasizes that cost-efficient deployment requires coordinated progress across all of these dimensions rather than isolated advances in any one area.

Quantization has emerged as one of the most widely studied model-level techniques, owing to its ability to reduce both memory footprint and arithmetic cost by representing weights and activations in lower-precision formats. [arxiv_2408.03130] provides a detailed treatment of quantization alongside pruning and knowledge distillation, highlighting the unique challenges that arise when applying these methods to very large models. A recurring theme in this work is that the practical benefits of quantization depend heavily on hardware support: reductions in numerical precision translate into inference speedups only when the underlying compute substrate can exploit them efficiently. This hardware-software co-design consideration is also central to [arxiv_2211.07715], which describes a pipeline for deploying compressed transformer models on CPUs that integrates hardware-aware pruning, knowledge distillation, quantization, and a custom inference runtime with optimized kernels for sparse and quantized operators. The resulting Fast DistilBERT model demonstrates that substantial throughput improvements can be achieved with minimal accuracy loss when these techniques are applied in concert and the deployment environment is treated as an integral part of the design process. While [arxiv_2211.07715] focuses on a smaller model than the multi-billion-parameter LLMs that are the primary concern of the other works surveyed here, its emphasis on the importance of specialized runtime support anticipates a challenge that becomes even more acute at larger scales.

Pruning, which removes redundant weights or structural components from a trained model, is another central technique across the literature. [arxiv_2409.17372] makes a pointed observation that most compression research, including the majority of pruning and quantization work, concentrates on optimizing the weights of a fixed architecture while neglecting the question of whether the architecture itself is optimal. This critique motivates a training-free architecture search framework designed to identify efficient subnets that inherit weights from the original model while achieving inference acceleration. The argument that architectural redundancy is an underexplored dimension of LLM efficiency represents a meaningful departure from the dominant paradigm and suggests that the field may benefit from greater integration between neural architecture search and compression research. [arxiv_2402.09748] acknowledges compact architecture design as a distinct category within its taxonomy, but the literature as a whole has devoted comparatively less attention to this direction than to post-training compression of existing models.

Knowledge distillation, in which a smaller student model is trained to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher, occupies an important place in the efficiency literature but faces particular challenges in the LLM setting. As [arxiv_2402.09748] notes, the high cost of training at scale makes it difficult to perform the kind of iterative distillation that has proven effective for smaller models. [arxiv_2408.03130] similarly identifies the computational overhead of distillation as a practical barrier, though it also points to cases where distillation can be combined with other techniques to achieve compounding benefits, as illustrated concretely by the pipeline described in [arxiv_2211.07715].

Taken together, these works paint a picture of a research area that is both technically rich and practically urgent. The convergence on a shared set of core techniques—quantization, pruning, distillation, architectural optimization, and system-level scheduling—reflects genuine progress in understanding the sources of inefficiency in LLM inference. At the same time, the recurring acknowledgment across [arxiv_2404.14294], [arxiv_2402.09748], [arxiv_2401.02038], and [arxiv_2408.03130] that no single method is sufficient, and that the interaction between algorithmic choices and hardware constraints is decisive, underscores the need for integrated approaches that span the full stack from model design to deployment infrastructure.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently has become one of the central preoccupations of contemporary natural language processing research. As these models have grown in scale, the gap between their theoretical capabilities and their practical deployability has widened considerably, motivating a rich body of work that addresses inefficiencies at every stage of the model lifecycle—from training and compression to inference-time memory management. This subsection surveys the principal strategies that have emerged to address these challenges, drawing connections across complementary lines of inquiry.

A foundational tension in the field concerns the relationship between model size and computational efficiency during training. Conventional intuition suggests that smaller models, by virtue of executing faster per iteration, should be preferred when compute is constrained. However, empirical evidence challenges this assumption. Research on Transformer models for self-supervised pretraining and high-resource machine translation demonstrates that wider and deeper models converge in significantly fewer steps, and that this acceleration in convergence typically outpaces the additional per-iteration overhead of larger architectures [arxiv_2002.11794]. The implication is counterintuitive but consequential: the most compute-efficient training strategy may be to train an extremely large model but halt after relatively few iterations. This "train large, then compress" paradigm reframes the problem of efficiency not as one of choosing small models from the outset, but of strategically managing the interplay between model capacity and training duration [arxiv_2002.11794]. Such findings set the stage for downstream compression techniques, since models trained in this manner are large at the point of deployment and must be reduced before practical inference becomes feasible.

The compression of pre-trained models has accordingly attracted substantial research attention, with quantization, pruning, and low-rank decomposition emerging as the dominant methodological families. Among these, post-training quantization (PTQ) is particularly appealing because it avoids the cost of retraining from scratch. Nevertheless, PTQ is not without its difficulties. Low-bit quantization, while reducing memory footprint and potentially accelerating computation, introduces performance degradation that becomes increasingly severe as bit-width decreases [arxiv_2408.08554]. A further practical obstacle is that the limited variety of integer computing units available on contemporary GPUs constrains which quantization configurations can actually be accelerated in hardware; mixed-precision operations across different bit-widths cannot always be efficiently executed [arxiv_2408.08554]. The ABQ-LLM framework addresses both concerns by introducing an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm paired with an inference framework designed to enable efficient execution of varied-precision matrix operations on GPU hardware, achieving competitive performance across a range of quantization settings [arxiv_2408.08554]. This work illustrates a broader principle: algorithmic advances in compression must be co-designed with hardware-aware inference systems if theoretical gains are to translate into wall-clock speedups.

A complementary approach to compression involves low-rank representations of model weights. The success of parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods based on low-rank adapters has demonstrated that large pre-trained models contain substantial redundancy that can be exploited without catastrophic loss of capability [arxiv_2501.16372]. More recent work has sought to combine low-rank adapter techniques with neural architecture search (NAS), particularly through weight-sharing super-networks that allow simultaneous exploration of architectural configurations and low-rank factorizations [arxiv_2501.16372]. This synergy between PEFT and NAS offers a route toward models with reduced memory footprints and faster inference times, and is argued to democratize access to LLMs by making deployment feasible in resource-constrained environments [arxiv_2501.16372]. Compared to quantization-based approaches, low-rank compression methods tend to preserve model structure more transparently and integrate naturally with fine-tuning workflows, though they may offer less aggressive compression ratios at very low bit-widths than methods like ABQ-LLM [arxiv_2408.08554; arxiv_2501.16372].

Beyond weight compression, a distinct and increasingly prominent source of inference inefficiency lies in the memory consumed by the key-value (KV) cache during autoregressive generation. As context lengths have grown, the KV cache has become a primary bottleneck: its memory usage scales linearly with sequence length, and for long-context workloads it can rival or exceed the memory occupied by model weights themselves [arxiv_2404.15949; arxiv_2508.06297]. This has motivated a substantial literature on KV cache optimization, which encompasses selective token retention, quantization of cached representations, and attention-level compression strategies [arxiv_2508.06297]. The CORM method exemplifies the selective retention approach: it observes that adjacent token query vectors exhibit striking similarity, and that the attention computation for a given query can be accurately approximated using only a small fraction of preceding key-value pairs [arxiv_2404.15949]. On the basis of these observations, CORM implements a dynamic eviction policy that retains only the most essential key-value pairs, substantially reducing the memory footprint of the cache without requiring modifications to model weights [arxiv_2404.15949]. The broader review of KV cache compression techniques situates such eviction policies within a taxonomy that also includes quantization of cached tensors and structural modifications to the attention mechanism itself, evaluating each family of methods in terms of its trade-offs between memory savings, inference speed, and task performance [arxiv_2508.06297]. A recurring finding across this literature is that no single strategy dominates across all deployment scenarios; compatibility with diverse model architectures and the risk of accuracy degradation under aggressive compression remain open challenges [arxiv_2508.06297].

The efficiency challenges of LLMs are not confined to any single phase of the pipeline, and several works adopt a more integrative perspective. Analyses of the internal structure of Transformer architectures identify how the attention mechanism's quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length constitutes a fundamental bottleneck for capturing long-range dependencies efficiently [arxiv_2405.11704]. At the training stage, techniques such as adaptive optimization algorithms, massively parallel computation, and mixed-precision training have been shown to accelerate convergence and reduce memory consumption, with the mathematical principles underlying these gains receiving detailed treatment [arxiv_2405.11704]. At the inference stage, the same work reviews compression strategies including quantization and pruning as complementary tools for reducing the cost of deployment [arxiv_2405.11704]. This systems-level perspective reinforces the view, implicit in much of the literature, that efficient inference for LLMs is not a problem amenable to a single silver-bullet solution but rather requires coordinated advances across training methodology, model compression, and runtime memory management.

Taken together, the works surveyed here reveal a field organized around several interlocking insights. First, the decision of how large to train a model has non-obvious implications for downstream compression and inference efficiency [arxiv_2002.11794]. Second, weight compression through quantization and low-rank methods can substantially reduce deployment costs, but must contend with hardware constraints and accuracy trade-offs [arxiv_2408.08554; arxiv_2501.16372]. Third, the KV cache represents an increasingly critical memory bottleneck that demands its own suite of optimization techniques, from dynamic eviction to quantization of cached representations [arxiv_2404.15949; arxiv_2508.06297]. The convergence of these research threads points toward a future in which efficient inference is achieved not through any single technique but through their principled combination.

### Evidence theme 3

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) at inference time has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary machine learning research. As model sizes continue to scale into the hundreds of billions of parameters, the computational and memory demands of serving these systems in real-world applications have motivated a diverse body of work spanning hardware-aware optimization, memory management, and algorithmic acceleration. This subsection surveys key contributions to this rapidly evolving landscape, examining how researchers have approached the fundamental tension between model capability and deployment efficiency.

A foundational observation motivating much of this work is that LLM inference is constrained by two interrelated bottlenecks: memory capacity and memory bandwidth. The sheer volume of model parameters, combined with the dynamic memory structures generated during autoregressive decoding, creates prohibitive costs that standard deployment pipelines struggle to absorb. This dual constraint is articulated clearly in work on CPU-based inference [arxiv_2311.00502], which demonstrates that with appropriate INT4 weight-only quantization and highly optimized runtime kernels, LLM inference can be made tractable even on commodity CPU hardware. By targeting the memory bandwidth problem directly through aggressive weight compression, this approach achieves practical inference efficiency on models such as Llama2 and GPT-NeoX without requiring specialized GPU accelerators. The broader implication is that hardware-aware quantization is not merely a deployment convenience but a necessary design consideration for democratizing access to large models.

Quantization as an inference optimization strategy has been explored along multiple dimensions. While post-training quantization for deployment is well established, the intersection of quantization with fine-tuning presents additional complexity. The PEQA method [arxiv_2305.14152] addresses this by combining parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with sub-4-bit integer quantization, updating only the quantization scales rather than the full weight matrices. This design allows PEQA to be applied directly to already-quantized LLMs, enabling task-specific adaptation without inflating memory requirements during training. Compared to approaches that apply quantization solely at the deployment stage, PEQA bridges the gap between training-time efficiency and inference-time efficiency, recognizing that the two phases are not independent concerns. The method is particularly relevant in scenarios where models must be continually adapted to new tasks under tight memory budgets, a common constraint in production environments.

Beyond weight compression, the key-value (KV) cache has emerged as a distinct and significant source of memory pressure during inference. In transformer-based LLMs, the KV cache stores intermediate attention states across all layers and all tokens in the current context, and its memory footprint scales linearly with both sequence length and batch size. Two complementary approaches to managing this bottleneck are represented in the literature reviewed here. The Layer-Condensed KV Cache method [arxiv_2405.10637] proposes a structural modification in which KV pairs are computed and cached for only a small subset of layers rather than all layers in the network. This selective caching strategy yields up to 26× higher throughput than standard transformer inference while maintaining competitive performance on language modeling benchmarks. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that their method is orthogonal to other memory-saving techniques, meaning it can be composed with quantization or attention approximation methods to achieve compounding efficiency gains.

InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] approaches the KV cache problem from a different angle, focusing specifically on the challenge of long-text generation where the cache grows to sizes that exceed GPU memory capacity. Rather than reducing the number of cached layers, InfiniGen introduces a dynamic management framework that speculatively identifies which tokens are most important for computing subsequent attention layers. By performing a lightweight rehearsal using inputs from the current layer and partial weight information from the next layer, the system can prefetch only the essential KV entries from offloaded storage, avoiding the latency penalty of loading the full cache. This speculative prefetching mechanism represents a sophisticated integration of algorithmic insight with systems-level memory management, and it is designed to work synergistically with existing offloading-based inference pipelines. Comparing InfiniGen with the Layer-Condensed KV Cache approach, the two methods target different regimes: the former is optimized for long-context generation where offloading is unavoidable, while the latter addresses throughput maximization in settings where the full model fits within accelerator memory but layer-wise caching is redundant.

Speculative decoding represents another major paradigm for accelerating LLM inference, operating at the level of token generation rather than memory management. The core idea is to use a smaller, faster draft model to propose candidate tokens that are then verified in parallel by the full target model, thereby reducing the number of sequential forward passes required. The SPIN system [arxiv_2503.15921] extends this paradigm by introducing heterogeneous speculative models, recognizing that a single small speculative model may not be equally well-suited to requests of varying difficulty. SPIN employs a learning-based algorithm for dynamically selecting among multiple draft models without requiring prior knowledge of request characteristics, and it incorporates holistic optimizations for both the speculation and verification phases. Additionally, SPIN addresses the practical challenge of batch processing, which prior speculative decoding systems have handled inadequately. By treating the inference serving system as a whole rather than optimizing individual components in isolation, SPIN represents a systems-level maturation of the speculative decoding approach. This contrasts with earlier speculative decoding work that typically assumed homogeneous draft models and single-request settings, limitations that SPIN explicitly identifies and resolves.

Taken together, these contributions are surveyed and contextualized within a broader tutorial framework on inference optimization for foundation models on AI accelerators [arxiv_2407.09111], which provides a comprehensive taxonomy of techniques spanning quantization, attention optimization, and hardware-specific deployment strategies. This work underscores that no single technique is sufficient in isolation; rather, efficient LLM inference requires the coordinated application of complementary methods tailored to the specific constraints of the deployment environment. The convergence of weight quantization [arxiv_2311.00502, arxiv_2305.14152], KV cache management [arxiv_2405.10637, arxiv_2406.19707], and speculative decoding [arxiv_2503.15921] reflects a maturing research agenda in which algorithmic innovation and systems engineering are increasingly intertwined. Future progress in this space will likely depend on tighter co-design across these dimensions, particularly as model sizes continue to grow and deployment scenarios become more diverse.
