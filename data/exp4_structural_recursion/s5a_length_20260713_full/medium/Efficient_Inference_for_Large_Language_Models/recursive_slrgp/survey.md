# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference — evidence cluster A

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated substantial scholarly interest in the problem of efficient inference, driven by the fundamental tension between the remarkable capabilities of these systems and the prohibitive computational and memory costs their deployment entails. Three recent survey works collectively map the landscape of this challenge and the algorithmic responses that have emerged to address it.

A foundational observation shared across the literature is that LLM inference is rendered inefficient by several interacting factors. Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] identify three primary causes: the sheer scale of model parameters, the quadratic complexity of the attention operation with respect to sequence length, and the sequential nature of auto-regressive decoding. This diagnostic framing is important because it motivates distinct families of solutions targeting different bottlenecks, rather than a single universal remedy. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] similarly ground their survey in the observation that the memory and computational costs incurred during inference make deployment on resource-constrained devices a persistent challenge, while Huang et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] situate the problem within a broader trajectory, arguing that low-cost training and deployment of LLMs represent the dominant future development trend for the field.

In terms of taxonomic organization, the surveys converge on a set of core compression and acceleration strategies while differing in how they structure them. Zhao et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] adopt a taxonomy inherited from the smaller-model compression literature, organizing techniques into quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, compact architecture design, and dynamic networks. They are careful to note, however, that LLMs introduce two characteristics that complicate direct application of these methods: the extremely high cost of fine-tuning or retraining after compression, and the emergent capabilities that may be disproportionately sensitive to aggressive compression. This nuance distinguishes their treatment from analyses of smaller models and underscores why LLM-specific adaptations of classical methods are necessary. Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294], by contrast, propose a three-level taxonomy spanning data-level, model-level, and system-level optimization, a framing that explicitly acknowledges that efficiency gains can be achieved not only by modifying model weights but also by restructuring inputs and optimizing the runtime environment. This broader scope allows their survey to incorporate techniques such as prompt compression and speculative decoding alongside more traditional model compression approaches.

Huang et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] take the widest view, covering both training and inference within a single framework. On the inference side, they address model compression, parallel computation, memory scheduling, and structural optimization, situating these techniques within the full lifecycle of LLM development. This integrated perspective is valuable for understanding how decisions made during training—such as architecture choices and pre-training objectives—constrain or enable subsequent inference optimization.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that is rapidly maturing but still grappling with the tension between generality and specificity. Classical compression techniques such as quantization and pruning remain central, but their application to LLMs demands careful attention to the unique properties of these models, including their scale, their sensitivity to weight perturbations, and the impracticality of full retraining [arxiv_2402.09748]. Meanwhile, system-level and data-level interventions are gaining recognition as complementary strategies that can yield substantial efficiency improvements without modifying model parameters at all [arxiv_2404.14294]. The convergence of these perspectives suggests that efficient LLM inference is best understood as a multi-dimensional optimization problem requiring coordinated solutions across algorithmic, architectural, and systems dimensions [arxiv_2401.02038].

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference — evidence cluster B

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary natural language processing research. While LLMs demonstrate remarkable adaptability to new tasks without retraining, their scale and computational demands impose substantial barriers to practical deployment [arxiv_2408.03130]. In response, a broad family of model compression and optimization techniques has been developed, each targeting different aspects of the inference pipeline and offering distinct trade-offs between efficiency and model quality.

Quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation represent the most widely studied approaches to reducing the resource footprint of large models. These methods have been applied both individually and in combination, with the goal of reducing memory consumption and accelerating inference without catastrophic degradation of task performance [arxiv_2408.03130]. The survey by [arxiv_2408.03130] provides a useful taxonomy of these techniques, situating them within a broader optimization landscape and clarifying their respective practical applications and limitations. Such taxonomic organization is valuable for understanding the research trajectory, as it reveals how different methods address complementary bottlenecks—quantization primarily targeting memory bandwidth, pruning reducing parameter counts, and knowledge distillation transferring representational capacity from larger teacher models to more compact students.

A concrete instantiation of combining these techniques is demonstrated in the work on Fast DistilBERT [arxiv_2211.07715], which proposes an end-to-end pipeline integrating hardware-aware pruning, knowledge distillation, quantization, and a custom inference runtime engine with optimized kernels for sparse and quantized operators. This work is notable for its explicit attention to the gap between research-oriented compression results and the demands of industrial deployment, where latency constraints often prevent even moderately sized transformer models from being used in production. By co-designing the compression pipeline with the underlying hardware and runtime environment, [arxiv_2211.07715] achieves meaningful throughput improvements with minimal accuracy loss, illustrating that the effectiveness of compression techniques is tightly coupled to the software and hardware stack on which they are executed.

Despite the maturity of weight-centric compression methods, a significant limitation shared by most existing approaches is their exclusive focus on optimizing model weights while neglecting the exploration of model architecture itself [arxiv_2409.17372]. This observation motivates a complementary line of research into architecture search for LLMs. However, traditional neural architecture search methods face severe scalability challenges when applied to models with billions of parameters, limiting their practical utility in this domain. To address this, [arxiv_2409.17372] proposes a training-free architecture search framework designed to identify optimal subnets that preserve the core capabilities of the original LLM while achieving inference acceleration. By inheriting weights from the pretrained model rather than training subnets from scratch, this approach sidesteps the prohibitive computational costs that have historically constrained architecture search at scale.

Taken together, these works reveal a multi-dimensional optimization landscape in which weight compression and architectural search are increasingly recognized as complementary rather than competing strategies. While [arxiv_2408.03130] and [arxiv_2211.07715] underscore the practical maturity and deployability of quantization and pruning pipelines, [arxiv_2409.17372] highlights the underexplored potential of structural optimization as an additional axis of efficiency gain. The convergence of these perspectives suggests that future progress in efficient LLM inference will likely require integrated approaches that simultaneously address weight redundancy, architectural suboptimality, and hardware-software co-design.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation — evidence cluster A

Post-training quantization and memory optimization have emerged as two of the most actively investigated directions in the broader effort to make large language model (LLM) inference computationally tractable. These approaches respond to a fundamental tension identified in the literature: the models that achieve the highest accuracy are also the most expensive to deploy, and the hardware constraints governing real-world inference systems do not relax in proportion to the growth of model scale.

An important early insight into this tension comes from work on training dynamics. Research on Transformer models for natural language processing tasks demonstrated that, counterintuitively, training extremely large models for fewer iterations is often more compute-efficient than training smaller models to full convergence [arxiv_2002.11794]. Wider and deeper Transformers converge in significantly fewer steps, and this acceleration in convergence typically outpaces the additional per-iteration computational overhead of the larger architecture. While this finding optimizes for training efficiency, it simultaneously sharpens the inference problem: the models that are cheapest to train to a given accuracy level are also the largest, and therefore the most burdensome to serve at inference time. This observation motivates the subsequent body of work on compression and memory management as post-hoc or deployment-time interventions.

Quantization is among the most widely adopted of these interventions. Post-training quantization (PTQ) reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations without requiring retraining, making it particularly attractive for practitioners who lack the resources to fine-tune billion-parameter models. However, PTQ introduces two interrelated challenges that have proven difficult to resolve simultaneously. First, reducing bit-width—especially to very low precisions—degrades model performance in ways that can be difficult to predict or bound. Second, the integer computing units available on contemporary GPUs support only a limited set of precision formats, which means that quantized matrix operations at non-standard bit-widths cannot straightforwardly exploit hardware acceleration [arxiv_2408.08554]. The ABQ-LLM framework addresses both challenges by introducing an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm paired with an inference framework capable of executing mixed-precision matrix operations efficiently on GPU hardware. By decoupling the choice of quantization precision from the constraints of fixed hardware data types, this approach expands the design space available to practitioners seeking to balance accuracy and throughput.

A complementary bottleneck arises not from weight precision but from the memory consumed by the key-value (KV) cache during autoregressive generation. As context lengths increase, the KV cache grows quadratically, creating a memory bottleneck that limits both the batch sizes and the sequence lengths that can be handled in practice [arxiv_2508.06297]. A recent systematic review of KV cache compression techniques categorizes the available strategies into selective token retention, quantization of cached activations, and attention-level compression, evaluating each in terms of its impact on memory usage, inference speed, and output quality. The review highlights that these methods carry distinct trade-offs and that compatibility with different model architectures and serving frameworks remains an open challenge.

Taken together, these three lines of work illuminate the multi-dimensional nature of the inference efficiency problem. The training-time insight that large models are paradoxically efficient [arxiv_2002.11794] establishes why inference compression is necessary in the first place. Quantization frameworks such as ABQ-LLM [arxiv_2408.08554] address weight-level memory and compute costs, while KV cache compression techniques [arxiv_2508.06297] target the dynamic memory overhead that accumulates during generation. Efficient inference thus requires coordinated solutions across the model lifecycle, from training strategy through deployment-time memory management.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation — evidence cluster B

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary natural language processing research, driven by the enormous computational and memory demands these systems impose during both training and inference. Addressing these demands requires a multifaceted understanding of where bottlenecks arise and which optimization strategies are best suited to resolving them.

A foundational concern is the sheer scale of resources required to run modern LLMs. As [arxiv_2405.11704] notes, the Transformer architecture and its derivatives, while highly effective at capturing long-range dependencies, introduce significant computational inefficiencies that manifest across both the training and inference phases. During training, techniques such as adaptive optimization algorithms like AdamW, massively parallel computing, and mixed-precision training have proven effective at accelerating convergence and reducing memory consumption. However, the challenge does not end once a model is trained; deployment introduces its own set of constraints, particularly when models must operate under real-world resource limitations. [arxiv_2405.11704] systematically surveys compression strategies including quantization and pruning as key mechanisms for alleviating inference-time overhead, situating these approaches within a broader framework of efficiency optimization.

One of the most prominent inference-time bottlenecks is the key-value (KV) cache, whose memory footprint grows linearly with sequence length and can quickly become the dominant cost in autoregressive generation. [arxiv_2404.15949] directly addresses this problem through CORM, a cache eviction policy that dynamically retains only the most essential key-value pairs during inference. The method is grounded in two empirical observations about Transformer attention behavior: adjacent token query vectors exhibit high similarity, and the attention computation for a current query can be adequately approximated using only a small subset of preceding queries' attention information. By exploiting these properties, CORM substantially reduces the memory footprint of the KV cache without requiring modifications to model weights or retraining. This approach represents a practical, deployment-oriented intervention that complements weight-level compression techniques by targeting the dynamic memory structures generated at inference time.

While KV cache optimization addresses runtime memory, a parallel line of research focuses on reducing the static parameter count and representational complexity of models themselves. [arxiv_2501.16372] examines the intersection of low-rank adaptation and Neural Architecture Search (NAS), arguing that these two methodologies are mutually reinforcing. Low-rank adapters, originally developed for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, constrain weight updates to low-dimensional subspaces, thereby reducing both the number of trainable parameters and the memory required for gradient storage. When combined with weight-sharing super-networks from the NAS literature, this approach enables the simultaneous search over architectural configurations and the training of compressed, task-adapted models. The resulting systems exhibit reduced memory footprints and faster inference times, and [arxiv_2501.16372] frames this combination explicitly as a means of democratizing LLM deployment in resource-constrained environments.

Taken together, these works illuminate the complementary nature of different efficiency strategies. [arxiv_2405.11704] provides a broad theoretical and empirical grounding for understanding where inefficiencies originate, while [arxiv_2404.15949] and [arxiv_2501.16372] offer targeted solutions operating at different levels of the inference pipeline—dynamic cache management and static model compression, respectively. Notably, the approaches are not mutually exclusive: a model compressed via low-rank NAS techniques [arxiv_2501.16372] could further benefit from KV cache eviction policies [arxiv_2404.15949] during deployment, suggesting that the most effective inference optimization frameworks will likely combine multiple complementary strategies rather than relying on any single technique in isolation.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Speculative decoding has emerged as one of the most actively investigated paradigms for reducing the latency of large language model (LLM) inference without sacrificing output quality. The core intuition is straightforward: a smaller, faster draft model generates a sequence of candidate tokens, which the target LLM then verifies in a single forward pass, yielding wall-clock speedups whenever the acceptance rate is sufficiently high. Despite its conceptual elegance, practical deployment of speculative decoding at scale exposes several non-trivial engineering challenges that have only recently begun to receive systematic attention.

A comprehensive treatment of the broader inference optimization landscape is provided by [arxiv_2407.09111], which surveys complementary techniques applicable to foundation models running on AI accelerators. That work situates speculative decoding within a wider taxonomy that includes quantization, pruning, and hardware-aware scheduling, noting that as model parameter counts reach hundreds of billions, the combined pressure of inference cost and latency makes no single technique sufficient on its own. The tutorial underscores that real-world deployments must balance throughput and latency simultaneously, a tension that motivates the search for more holistic optimization frameworks.

It is precisely this holistic perspective that motivates the design of SPIN [arxiv_2503.15921]. The authors identify three limitations that afflict current speculative decoding systems: the use of homogeneous small speculative models (SSMs) that cannot adapt to requests of varying difficulty, inadequate support for batched inference, and the absence of joint optimization across both the speculation and verification phases. SPIN addresses these gaps through heterogeneous SSM ensembles, pairing a learning-based selection algorithm that chooses among draft models at runtime without requiring prior knowledge of request characteristics. By allowing different requests within the same batch to be served by different SSMs, SPIN avoids the inefficiency that arises when a single draft model is either too weak for easy requests or too slow for hard ones. This adaptive strategy represents a meaningful departure from prior work, which typically fixes the draft model at deployment time and relies on a single speculation policy regardless of input complexity.

The quantization dimension of inference efficiency is addressed from a complementary angle by [arxiv_2305.14152], which focuses on the memory bottleneck that persists even after parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods reduce optimizer-state overhead. The authors observe that most quantization proposals target the deployment phase exclusively, leaving a gap when practitioners need to fine-tune an already-compressed model for a downstream task. Their proposed method, PEQA, closes this gap by updating only the quantization scales during adaptation, making it directly applicable to sub-4-bit integer quantized LLMs. Compared to standard PEFT approaches, PEQA achieves significant memory reduction while preserving task-transfer flexibility. Taken together with the observations in [arxiv_2407.09111], this work reinforces the view that memory efficiency and computational efficiency are deeply intertwined concerns: reducing the footprint of model weights through aggressive quantization directly affects the feasibility of batched inference and, by extension, the conditions under which speculative decoding can be profitably applied.

Across these contributions, a consistent theme emerges: efficient LLM inference is not a single-axis problem. [arxiv_2407.09111] frames the challenge at the system level, cataloguing the full space of accelerator-aware optimizations. [arxiv_2503.15921] demonstrates that even within the relatively mature subfield of speculative decoding, substantial gains remain available through heterogeneity-aware scheduling and joint phase optimization. [arxiv_2305.14152] shows that the memory constraints shaping inference costs begin upstream, during fine-tuning, and that quantization-aware adaptation can propagate efficiency benefits all the way through to deployment. Together, these works suggest that future progress will increasingly depend on co-designing compression, scheduling, and hardware utilization strategies rather than optimizing each dimension in isolation.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The memory footprint associated with the key-value (KV) cache represents one of the most pressing bottlenecks in deploying large language models (LLMs) at scale, particularly as sequence lengths and batch sizes grow. Two complementary lines of research have emerged to address this challenge: dynamic management of the KV cache during inference and architectural modifications that reduce the number of layers for which KV entries are computed and stored.

InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] tackles the problem from a runtime management perspective, proposing a framework specifically designed for long-text generation scenarios in which the KV cache can grow to prohibitive sizes. The central insight of InfiniGen is that only a small subset of tokens is truly essential for computing attention in any given layer, and that this subset can be identified speculatively by performing a lightweight rehearsal using inputs from the current layer together with partial query weights and key cache entries from the subsequent layer. By prefetching only these critical KV entries, InfiniGen integrates naturally with offloading-based inference systems, reducing the volume of data that must reside in high-bandwidth memory at any moment. This speculative prefetching strategy is notable because it avoids the need for exhaustive attention computation to determine token importance, instead approximating relevance at low cost before the full forward pass proceeds.

A structurally different approach is taken by the Layer-Condensed KV Cache method [arxiv_2405.10637], which addresses memory consumption not through selective retention of tokens within a layer but through selective retention of layers themselves. Rather than computing and caching KV pairs for every transformer layer, this method restricts caching to a small number of layers, with the remaining layers reusing or deriving representations from those cached entries. The reported throughput gains are substantial, reaching up to 26× compared to standard transformer inference, and the authors demonstrate that language modeling quality and downstream task performance remain competitive despite the reduction in cached state. Importantly, the method is described as orthogonal to other memory-saving techniques, meaning it can be combined with approaches such as quantization or sparse attention without fundamental incompatibility. Compared to InfiniGen, which operates dynamically at the token level within a fixed layer structure, the Layer-Condensed approach modifies the static computation graph itself, suggesting that the two strategies could in principle be applied jointly for compounded memory savings.

While both of the above works focus on the KV cache and assume GPU-centric deployment, a distinct but related challenge arises when inference must be performed on hardware with more constrained memory bandwidth, such as CPUs. The work on efficient LLM inference on CPUs [arxiv_2311.00502] addresses this setting through INT4 weight-only quantization combined with highly optimized runtime kernels. By quantizing model weights to four-bit integers while preserving activations at higher precision, this approach dramatically reduces the memory capacity and bandwidth demands that make CPU deployment otherwise impractical. The framework is demonstrated on widely used models including Llama2, Llama, and GPT-NeoX, establishing its generality across architectures. In contrast to the KV cache-focused methods, this work targets the static parameter memory rather than the dynamic inference-time state, reflecting the different dominant bottleneck on CPU hardware where both capacity and bandwidth are more severely limited than on GPU clusters.

Taken together, these three works illustrate that efficient LLM inference requires addressing memory pressure at multiple levels simultaneously: the dynamic KV cache through speculative prefetching [arxiv_2406.19707] or layer-level condensation [arxiv_2405.10637], and the static model weights through aggressive quantization tailored to the target hardware [arxiv_2311.00502]. The orthogonality noted in [arxiv_2405.10637] suggests that combining these strategies is a promising direction for achieving deployable inference across diverse hardware environments.
