# Multimodal Large Language Models (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs — evidence cluster A

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, fundamentally reshaping how computational systems perceive and reason about heterogeneous data. At its core, this paradigm is driven by the recognition that connecting textual and visual modalities plays an essential role in generative intelligence, motivating substantial research investment in architectures capable of seamlessly integrating these complementary information streams [arxiv_2402.12451]. The resulting body of work spans a wide spectrum, from narrowly specialized vision-language systems to increasingly general-purpose assistants capable of handling diverse real-world tasks.

A foundational distinction in the literature concerns the evolutionary trajectory from specialist to generalist models. Early multimodal foundation models were predominantly pre-trained for specific purposes, including dedicated vision backbone learning for visual understanding and text-to-image generation pipelines. More recent work has shifted decisively toward general-purpose assistants, pursuing unified vision models inspired by large language models, end-to-end multimodal training regimes, and the chaining of multimodal tools with language model backbones [arxiv_2309.10020]. This transition reflects a broader ambition: rather than deploying separate systems for discrete tasks, researchers now seek unified architectures that can fluidly navigate across problem types within a single dialogue-based interface.

Architectural choices and multimodal alignment strategies constitute a central axis of differentiation among contemporary MLLMs. Surveys of the field reveal that models vary considerably in how they bridge the representational gap between visual encoders and language model decoders, with alignment strategies ranging from lightweight projection layers to more elaborate cross-attention mechanisms [arxiv_2402.12451]. Training techniques similarly diverge, with some approaches relying on large-scale contrastive pre-training followed by instruction fine-tuning, while others pursue end-to-end optimization from the outset [arxiv_2309.10020]. These architectural and training decisions carry significant downstream consequences, influencing performance across tasks such as visual grounding, image generation and editing, and open-ended visual question answering [arxiv_2402.12451].

A particularly important dimension of recent progress concerns the expansion of modality coverage beyond the canonical vision-language dyad. While much foundational work concentrated on image-text pairs, the frontier has broadened to encompass audio, video, sensor data, and other heterogeneous input types [arxiv_2410.05608]. This expansion introduces new technical challenges, including the need for modality-specific encoders, unified representation spaces that can accommodate diverse signal types, and instruction tuning strategies optimized for multi-domain performance. Practical applications enabled by this broader multimodal capability include visual storytelling, visual question answering, and a range of domain-specific deployments [arxiv_2410.05608].

The maturation of the field is also reflected in the growing infrastructure surrounding model evaluation. Researchers have compiled and systematically described training datasets and evaluation benchmarks, enabling more rigorous comparisons across model families and task categories [arxiv_2402.12451]. This benchmarking infrastructure is essential for tracking genuine progress, particularly as the diversity of model architectures and training paradigms makes informal comparisons unreliable. Collectively, these developments underscore that the study of MLLMs is no longer confined to a narrow set of vision-language tasks but has expanded into a broad research program encompassing foundational modeling questions, scalable training methodologies, and the principled evaluation of general-purpose multimodal intelligence [arxiv_2309.10020; arxiv_2410.05608].

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs — evidence cluster B

The development of multimodal large language models is deeply rooted in the broader trajectory of vision-language pre-training (VLP), which has emerged as a foundational paradigm for enabling machines to jointly reason over visual and textual information. A comprehensive survey of VLP methods identifies three principal categories of tasks that these models have been designed to address: image-text tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and visual grounding; core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation; and video-text tasks encompassing video captioning and video question answering [arxiv_2210.09263]. This tripartite taxonomy reflects the expanding scope of multimodal intelligence, as researchers have progressively moved beyond narrow task-specific architectures toward large-scale foundation models capable of generalizing across diverse modalities and domains. The survey also highlights persistent challenges in the field, including the difficulty of aligning heterogeneous representations and the computational demands of training at scale, while pointing to big foundation models as a promising direction for future progress [arxiv_2210.09263].

A critical but often underexplored dimension of multimodal model development concerns interpretability. While the NLP community has benefited from a growing ecosystem of visualization and analysis tools for transformer-based models, the internal mechanisms of vision-language transformers have remained comparatively opaque. This asymmetry poses significant risks, as the deployment of powerful yet inscrutable multimodal systems in high-stakes applications demands a deeper understanding of how these models integrate and attend to cross-modal information. To address this gap, VL-InterpreT has been proposed as an interactive visualization tool specifically designed for multimodal transformers, offering novel capabilities for examining attention patterns and hidden representations across both visual and linguistic components [arxiv_2203.17247]. The tool tracks statistics across attention heads throughout all layers and visualizes both cross-modal and intra-modal attention, providing researchers with a task-agnostic framework for probing model behavior [arxiv_2203.17247]. This work underscores the argument that advances in model capability must be accompanied by commensurate advances in interpretability, particularly as multimodal transformers are increasingly integrated into real-world systems.

The practical deployment of multimodal models in specialized domains introduces additional considerations that go beyond general-purpose benchmarks. One notable example is the application of vision-language instruction tuning to semiconductor electron microscopy image analysis, a domain characterized by highly technical visual content and limited labeled data [arxiv_2409.07463]. This work proposes a teacher-student framework in which large pre-trained multimodal models such as GPT-4 are used to generate instruction-following data, which is then used to fine-tune smaller, more deployable multimodal models for zero-shot visual question answering and classification tasks [arxiv_2409.07463]. The approach effectively transfers domain-specific knowledge from larger to smaller models while substantially reducing the burden of human annotation, offering a secure and cost-effective pathway for domain adaptation. Compared to the general VLP landscape surveyed in [arxiv_2210.09263], this work illustrates how the foundational pre-training paradigms can be operationalized in constrained, specialized settings through targeted instruction tuning strategies.

Taken together, these contributions reveal a field in active maturation. The theoretical foundations laid by large-scale VLP research [arxiv_2210.09263] are being complemented by tools that make model behavior more transparent [arxiv_2203.17247] and by methodologies that extend multimodal capabilities into specialized application domains [arxiv_2409.07463]. The interplay between scale, interpretability, and domain specificity represents one of the central tensions shaping current research directions in multimodal large language models.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language — evidence cluster A

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has proceeded along several distinct but interrelated trajectories, each addressing the fundamental challenge of integrating heterogeneous perceptual signals with the powerful generative and reasoning capabilities of large-scale language models. A central concern across this body of work is how to align representations from different modalities—vision, audio, and language—in ways that are both computationally tractable and broadly generalizable.

One of the foundational contributions to this space is PaLI, which demonstrates that joint scaling of vision and language components is critical to achieving strong multimodal performance [arxiv_2209.06794]. PaLI generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, enabling a unified interface for a wide range of vision-language and multilingual tasks. A notable architectural decision in PaLI is the training of a large 4-billion parameter Vision Transformer (ViT-e), motivated by the observation that existing language transformers substantially outscale their vision counterparts. By leveraging pre-trained encoder-decoder language models alongside large-scale ViTs, PaLI capitalizes on prior investment in unimodal pretraining while extending capabilities into the multimodal domain [arxiv_2209.06794]. This work thus establishes an important precedent: that scaling vision encoders to match the capacity of language models yields measurable gains, and that a flexible text-generation interface can unify diverse multimodal tasks.

While PaLI addresses the question of scale and joint pretraining, a complementary line of research focuses on the efficiency of multimodal adaptation. CROME proposes a vision-language instruction tuning framework designed to reduce the cost of training and adaptation without sacrificing performance [arxiv_2408.06610]. Its central innovation is a gated cross-modal adapter that combines visual and textual representations before they are passed into a frozen language model. Because the LLM itself is not retrained, CROME requires only a small number of trainable parameters, making it substantially more resource-efficient than approaches that necessitate full language model retraining. Importantly, CROME also addresses a gap identified in prior work: the insufficient guidance that zero-shot-oriented methods provide for task-specific fine-tuning. By demonstrating strong performance on both zero-shot visual question answering benchmarks and instruction-following tasks, as well as yielding effective fine-tuning behavior, CROME bridges the divide between general-purpose and task-specific multimodal adaptation [arxiv_2408.06610]. Compared to PaLI's emphasis on scale, CROME's contribution is architectural efficiency—showing that lightweight adapter modules can serve as effective cross-modal bridges when the underlying language model is sufficiently capable.

A third direction extends the scope of multimodal integration beyond the vision-language dyad to encompass audio as well. VALOR introduces an omni-perception pretraining framework that jointly models vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end architecture [ss_478472]. The model employs three separate unimodal encoders alongside a multimodal decoder for conditional text generation, and is trained using two pretext tasks: Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA), which projects all three modalities into a shared representational space, and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC), which trains the model to generate text conditioned on any combination of visual, auditory, or audiovisual inputs [ss_478472]. This trimodal approach distinguishes VALOR from both PaLI and CROME, which operate exclusively within the vision-language paradigm. By constructing a large-scale, high-quality dataset to support this pretraining regime, VALOR also contributes to the research infrastructure necessary for advancing audio-visual-language understanding.

Taken together, these works illuminate a productive tension in the field between scale and efficiency, and between bimodal and richer multimodal architectures. PaLI argues for the importance of matched scaling across modalities [arxiv_2209.06794]; CROME demonstrates that frozen large models can be effectively adapted through lightweight cross-modal modules [arxiv_2408.06610]; and VALOR pushes the boundaries of modality coverage by incorporating audio into the pretraining objective [ss_478472]. Collectively, they reflect the field's ongoing effort to build models that are simultaneously capable, adaptable, and perceptually comprehensive.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language — evidence cluster B

The rapid convergence of language and vision modalities has catalyzed a new generation of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), which extend the representational power of large language models (LLMs) to encompass visual information. This integration has enabled systems capable of performing a diverse array of tasks, including image captioning, visual question answering (VQA), and visual grounding [arxiv_2403.08773]. The promise of these models lies in their ability to bridge the semantic gap between textual and visual domains, yet realizing this promise in practice has required sustained methodological innovation alongside rigorous evaluation frameworks.

Early efforts in multimodal representation learning established the foundational architectures that subsequent work would build upon. Models such as InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4, and LLaVA demonstrated that instruction-tuned vision-language models could generalize across a broad range of multimodal tasks, suggesting that alignment between modalities could be achieved through carefully designed training regimes [arxiv_2306.09265]. These developments marked a significant departure from task-specific pipelines, moving the field toward unified models capable of open-ended visual reasoning.

Despite these advances, a persistent challenge has been the accurate interpretation of complex visual scenes, particularly in real-world scenarios where images contain ambiguous or densely overlapping information [arxiv_2403.08773]. Existing vision-language models (VLMs) frequently struggle with fine-grained visual understanding, leading to errors in tasks that demand precise spatial reasoning or nuanced object recognition. In response to these limitations, Veagle was proposed as a novel approach to enhance multimodal capabilities by addressing the representational bottlenecks observed in prior MLLMs [arxiv_2403.08773]. Such targeted architectural improvements reflect a broader trend in the field: rather than scaling models indiscriminately, researchers have increasingly sought to identify and remedy specific failure modes in cross-modal understanding.

Parallel to these modeling efforts, the community has recognized the need for comprehensive and standardized evaluation methodologies. The LVLM-eHub benchmark was introduced to address this gap, providing a holistic assessment of publicly available large multimodal models across multiple capability dimensions [arxiv_2306.09265]. In its initial formulation, LVLM-eHub evaluated eight representative LVLMs across six categories of multimodal capabilities—including VQA and embodied artificial intelligence—using 47 standard text-related visual benchmarks, and complemented quantitative evaluation with an online arena platform for user-level, open-world question answering [arxiv_2306.09265]. A subsequent and expanded version of this benchmark extended coverage to 13 representative LVLMs evaluated across five capability categories and 42 in-domain text-related visual benchmarks, while also investigating how specific model configurations and modality alignment strategies influence overall performance [ss_478371]. Comparing these two iterations reveals a maturing evaluation ecosystem: as the number of available models grew and their capabilities diversified, benchmark design itself evolved to capture a more nuanced portrait of model strengths and weaknesses.

Collectively, these contributions illuminate the dual trajectory of the field. On one hand, architectural innovations such as Veagle seek to push the representational frontier by improving how models fuse and interpret multimodal inputs [arxiv_2403.08773]. On the other hand, systematic evaluation frameworks like LVLM-eHub provide the empirical scaffolding necessary to situate these advances within a broader comparative context [arxiv_2306.09265; ss_478371]. Together, they underscore that progress in multimodal large language models depends not only on building more capable systems, but also on developing the diagnostic tools required to understand where and why current models succeed or fall short.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Visual Question Answering (VQA) has emerged as one of the central benchmark tasks for evaluating multimodal large language models (MLLMs), precisely because it demands the simultaneous comprehension of visual content and natural language. As surveyed in [arxiv_2411.17558], VQA sits at the intersection of natural language processing and computer vision, requiring models not only to parse and understand image-question pairs but also to perform knowledge reasoning that draws on both internal representations and externally retrieved information. The survey traces the evolution of VQA from early pipeline-based systems toward the current generation of vision-language pretraining models and MLLMs, highlighting how the field has progressively unified feature extraction and cross-modal fusion within single end-to-end architectures. This trajectory reflects a broader shift in the community's understanding of what it means for a model to "understand" an image: rather than treating visual and linguistic signals as parallel but separate streams, contemporary approaches seek deeply integrated representations that support complex inferential reasoning.

A critical bottleneck in realizing this integration, however, is the availability of high-quality instruction-tuning data for vision-language tasks. [arxiv_2308.12714] identifies this scarcity as a persistent challenge and notes that the dominant paradigm exemplified by systems such as LLaVA circumvents it by using a language-only version of GPT-4 to generate training data from pre-annotated captions and bounding boxes. While pragmatically useful, this approach is fundamentally limited because it relies on textual proxies for visual content rather than on direct image understanding, making it prone to missing fine-grained visual details. As a corrective, [arxiv_2308.12714] proposes leveraging existing MLLMs themselves to generate and iteratively correct visual instruction data, acknowledging at the same time that current MLLMs are weaker than their text-only counterparts and tend to produce inadequate or factually incorrect responses. This tension—using imperfect multimodal models to bootstrap better multimodal training—underscores a fundamental circularity in the field that has yet to be fully resolved.

Underlying both the data-generation challenge and the broader question of cross-modal reasoning is a more foundational issue: whether vision-and-language pretraining actually produces richer linguistic representations than text-only pretraining. [arxiv_2109.10246] addresses this directly by comparing the semantic representations learned by VL models against those of text-only baselines across clustering, probing, and commonsense question-answering analyses conducted in a language-only setting. Contrary to the intuitive expectation that grounding language in visual experience should enrich lexical semantics, the study finds that multimodal models fail to significantly outperform their text-only counterparts on these measures. This result is consequential for the interpretation of VQA performance gains reported in [arxiv_2411.17558]: strong benchmark scores may reflect improvements in cross-modal alignment and fusion mechanisms rather than genuinely deeper linguistic grounding.

Taken together, these three perspectives reveal a layered set of challenges facing MLLMs in the VQA domain. At the data level, [arxiv_2308.12714] highlights the difficulty of constructing instruction-tuning corpora that faithfully capture visual detail. At the representational level, [arxiv_2109.10246] raises doubts about whether multimodal pretraining substantively transforms internal linguistic semantics. And at the task-evaluation level, [arxiv_2411.17558] documents the rapid architectural progress that has nonetheless been achieved, suggesting that practical performance and theoretical grounding do not always advance in lockstep. Future work in this area will need to address not only how to scale multimodal training data but also how to design evaluation protocols and representational analyses that can distinguish genuine cross-modal understanding from sophisticated pattern matching.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The integration of visual capabilities into large language models through instruction tuning has emerged as a central research direction in multimodal learning, with a growing body of work examining how best to align visual and linguistic representations for effective zero-shot generalization. A foundational concern in this area is the architectural question of how vision encoders are grafted onto pretrained language models and subsequently fine-tuned. [arxiv_2310.03211] addresses this directly by conducting a comparative analysis of different multimodal instruction tuning approaches, benchmarking their performance across a diverse set of tasks including complex reasoning, conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification. Through rigorous ablation experiments, the study reveals key insights for guiding architectural choices when incorporating multimodal capabilities into language models, while also acknowledging that current approaches retain notable limitations in their generalization capacity.

A recurring critique of standard visual instruction tuning pipelines is their reliance on coarse image-level alignment, which may be insufficient for tasks requiring detailed spatial or regional understanding. [arxiv_2308.13437] identifies precisely this gap, arguing that existing methods utilize only image-language instruction data and therefore lack fine-grained cross-modal alignment. In response, the authors propose Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT), which augments the standard architecture with an additional region-level vision encoder. This design promotes more detailed comprehension of images by enabling the model to attend to sub-image regions rather than treating the entire image as a single undifferentiated input. To support this fine-grained alignment, PVIT introduces multiple data generation strategies for constructing an image-region-language instruction dataset, combining quantitative benchmarks with qualitative evaluation to demonstrate the benefits of region-aware supervision. Compared to the broader architectural survey in [arxiv_2310.03211], the PVIT work takes a more targeted approach, proposing a concrete modification to the encoder pipeline rather than evaluating existing systems in aggregate.

A distinct but related limitation concerns the ability of multimodal models to comprehend textual content embedded within images, such as signs, documents, or captions rendered as visual elements. [arxiv_2406.06730] highlights that a majority of visually-tuned models struggle with this capability, attributing the deficiency primarily to the composition of training data rather than architectural constraints per se. To address this, the authors introduce TRINS, a Text-Rich image INStruction dataset built upon LAION using hybrid annotation strategies that combine machine-assisted and human-assisted processes. The dataset comprises 39,153 text-rich images alongside 102,437 questions, and is distinguished by notably longer per-annotation word counts compared to related datasets, thereby posing new challenges for reading comprehension in visual contexts. Alongside the dataset, the paper presents a Language-vision Reading Assistant architecture designed to leverage this resource effectively.

Taken together, these three works illuminate complementary dimensions of the visual instruction tuning problem. [arxiv_2310.03211] provides a broad empirical foundation for understanding how architectural choices affect multimodal performance across task types. [arxiv_2308.13437] addresses the granularity of visual grounding by introducing region-level supervision, while [arxiv_2406.06730] shifts attention to a largely underexplored modality within images, namely embedded text, and argues that data composition is as consequential as model design. Collectively, they suggest that progress in multimodal instruction tuning requires simultaneous attention to encoder architecture, alignment granularity, and the diversity and richness of training data.
