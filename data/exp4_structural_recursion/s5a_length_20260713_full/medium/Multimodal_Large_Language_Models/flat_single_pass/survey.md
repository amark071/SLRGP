# Multimodal Large Language Models (medium)

The landscape of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past several years, evolving from narrowly scoped specialist systems into increasingly general-purpose assistants capable of reasoning across text, images, audio, video, and other sensory modalities. This evolution reflects a broader intellectual ambition within artificial intelligence research: to build systems that perceive and reason about the world in ways that more closely approximate human cognition, which is inherently multimodal in nature. Understanding the current state of this field requires tracing the conceptual and architectural lineage from early vision-language pre-training methods through to the sophisticated instruction-tuned models that now dominate the research frontier.

The foundational work in this domain emerged from vision-language pre-training (VLP), which sought to learn joint representations of images and text by training on large-scale paired datasets. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2210.09263], VLP methods can be broadly grouped into three categories: those targeting image-text tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and image-text retrieval; those addressing core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation; and those handling video-text tasks such as video captioning and video question answering. This taxonomy is instructive because it reveals how the field initially approached multimodality as a collection of distinct problem settings, each requiring specialized architectures and training objectives, rather than as a unified challenge amenable to a single general framework. The survey by [arxiv_2210.09263] documents the substantial progress made within each of these categories while also identifying persistent challenges, particularly around compositional reasoning, fine-grained grounding, and the generalization of pre-trained representations to novel tasks and domains.

The transition from specialist VLP models to general-purpose multimodal assistants is perhaps the most consequential development in the recent history of the field. [arxiv_2309.10020] provides a detailed account of this transition, framing it as a shift in the fundamental design philosophy: whereas earlier models were pre-trained for specific purposes and evaluated on fixed benchmarks, newer systems aspire to serve as flexible assistants capable of following open-ended instructions across a wide variety of tasks. This shift was catalyzed in large part by the success of large language models (LLMs), which demonstrated that scale, combined with instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback, could yield systems with impressive zero-shot and few-shot generalization capabilities. The natural extension of this insight to the multimodal domain prompted researchers to explore how visual information could be integrated into LLM-based architectures without sacrificing the language understanding capabilities that made these models so powerful. [arxiv_2309.10020] identifies several distinct approaches to this integration, including end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs and the chaining of specialized multimodal tools with LLMs, each with its own trade-offs in terms of computational cost, flexibility, and performance.

Architectural choices lie at the heart of MLLM design, and a substantial portion of the literature is devoted to understanding how visual and linguistic representations can be most effectively combined. A prominent paradigm involves using a pre-trained vision encoder, such as a Vision Transformer (ViT), to extract visual features, which are then projected into the embedding space of a pre-trained LLM. The PaLI model [arxiv_2209.06794] exemplifies this approach at scale, jointly training large encoder-decoder language models with a 4-billion parameter ViT to demonstrate that scaling the vision component alongside the language component yields substantial performance gains. PaLI's ability to perform a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages underscores the importance of joint scaling and flexible task interfaces. The finding that existing language transformers were substantially larger than their vision counterparts, and that closing this gap through a larger ViT improved performance, has had lasting influence on subsequent architectural decisions in the field.

More recent work has focused on making the integration of visual and linguistic modalities more efficient and adaptable. CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] proposes a gated cross-modal adapter that combines visual and textual representations before they are fed into a frozen LLM, enabling effective cross-modal understanding with minimal additional parameters. This approach addresses a key practical concern in the field: the computational expense of retraining or fine-tuning large language models. By keeping the LLM frozen and training only a lightweight adapter, CROME achieves competitive zero-shot performance on visual question answering and instruction-following benchmarks while also enabling efficient task-specific fine-tuning. This stands in contrast to approaches that require full model retraining, and the comparison highlights a fundamental tension in MLLM development between maximizing performance and minimizing computational cost. Similarly, the Veagle model [arxiv_2403.08773] introduces a novel approach to enhancing multimodal representation learning by addressing limitations observed in existing vision-language models, particularly in the accurate interpretation of images and the answering of visually grounded questions. These efforts collectively reflect a growing recognition that the field must move beyond simply scaling up models and instead develop more principled methods for cross-modal alignment and efficient adaptation.

The role of instruction tuning in enabling MLLMs to follow open-ended user instructions has received considerable attention. [arxiv_2310.03211] conducts a comparative analysis of different multimodal instruction tuning approaches, evaluating their performance across tasks including complex reasoning, conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification. The study reveals important insights about architectural choices when incorporating multimodal capabilities into LLMs, noting that while instruction-tuned multimodal models demonstrate promising zero-shot generalization, current approaches still face significant limitations in handling complex reasoning tasks. This finding resonates with broader concerns in the field about whether MLLMs genuinely understand the visual content they process or whether they rely on statistical shortcuts and language priors. Position-Enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT) [arxiv_2308.13437] addresses one specific limitation by integrating a region-level vision encoder alongside the standard image-level encoder, enabling finer-grained cross-modal alignment. By constructing an image-region-language instruction dataset through multiple data generation strategies, PVIT promotes more detailed comprehension of images, which is particularly important for tasks requiring spatial reasoning and precise localization.

The quality and diversity of instruction-tuning data have emerged as critical determinants of MLLM performance. [arxiv_2308.12714] identifies the scarcity of high-quality instruction-tuning data as a significant bottleneck, noting that the dominant paradigm of using language-only GPT-4 to generate training data suffers from an inability to understand image details, since it relies on pre-annotated captions and bounding boxes rather than direct visual perception. In response, the Visual Instruction Generation and Correction (VIGC) framework proposes using available MLLMs to generate instruction data for vision-language tasks, while incorporating a correction mechanism to address the tendency of current MLLMs to produce inadequate or factually incorrect responses. This self-improvement loop represents an interesting methodological development, though it also raises questions about the propagation of errors and biases from teacher models to student models. A related challenge is addressed by TRINS [arxiv_2406.06730], which focuses specifically on the ability of MLLMs to read and comprehend textual content embedded within images. The authors observe that most visually-tuned models struggle with this capability due to limitations in training data, and they introduce a large-scale text-rich image instruction dataset to address this gap. The dataset contains over 100,000 questions associated with text-rich images, with annotations that are substantially longer and more detailed than those in related datasets, providing new challenges for models that must integrate visual text recognition with broader language understanding.

Domain-specific applications of MLLMs have also attracted growing interest, as researchers seek to leverage the general capabilities of these models for specialized tasks that require expert knowledge. [arxiv_2409.07463] presents a framework for analyzing electron microscopy images in semiconductor manufacturing using vision-language instruction tuning. The framework employs a teacher-student approach in which a large pre-trained MLLM such as GPT-4 generates instruction-following data for zero-shot visual question answering and classification tasks, which is then used to fine-tune smaller multimodal models. This approach addresses practical concerns around data privacy, computational cost, and the need for domain-specific expertise, demonstrating that the knowledge encoded in large general-purpose models can be distilled into smaller, more deployable systems. The success of this approach in a highly specialized domain suggests that instruction tuning with domain-specific data can substantially extend the applicability of MLLMs beyond the general-purpose tasks on which they are typically evaluated.

Beyond the vision-language dyad, a growing body of work seeks to incorporate additional modalities such as audio into multimodal pre-training frameworks. The VALOR model [ss_478472] represents a significant step in this direction, jointly modeling the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, with two pre-training tasks designed to build alignment across all three modalities simultaneously. The Multimodal Grouping Alignment task projects vision, language, and audio into a shared common space, while the Multimodal Grouping Captioning task trains the model to generate text conditioned on any combination of visual and auditory inputs. This omni-perceptual approach contrasts with the predominantly vision-language focus of most MLLM research and points toward a more comprehensive form of multimodal intelligence. [arxiv_2410.05608] similarly emphasizes the importance of extending multimodal models beyond vision and language, covering recent advances in models that integrate text, images, audio, and video, and highlighting the key technical challenges involved in processing and aligning such diverse data forms. The tutorial-style overview provided by [arxiv_2410.05608] is particularly valuable for situating instruction tuning strategies within the broader context of multimodal pre-training, noting that the choice of tuning strategy can have a substantial impact on task-specific performance.

The evaluation of MLLMs presents its own set of challenges, as the diversity of tasks and capabilities these models are expected to handle makes it difficult to construct benchmarks that are both comprehensive and meaningful. [arxiv_2306.09265] and its updated version [ss_478371] address this challenge by constructing the LVLM-eHub, a comprehensive evaluation benchmark that assesses publicly available large multimodal models across multiple categories of capability including visual question answering, object hallucination, and embodied artificial intelligence. The benchmark evaluates models both through quantitative capability assessment on standardized benchmarks and through an online arena platform that captures user-level evaluation in open-world question-answering scenarios. The comparison between the two versions of this benchmark is instructive: the later version [ss_478371] expands the evaluation to 13 representative LVLMs and 42 in-domain benchmarks, reflecting the rapid proliferation of new models in the intervening period. Among the key findings reported across both versions is that instruction-tuned LVLMs with stronger language model backbones tend to perform better across most capability categories, and that modality alignment strategies have a significant impact on performance, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding.

The visual question answering task deserves particular attention as a benchmark for MLLM capabilities, given its requirement for integrated visual perception, language understanding, and knowledge reasoning. [arxiv_2411.17558] provides a detailed survey of VQA in the context of MLLMs, covering the evolution of models from early pipeline-based approaches to end-to-end systems that leverage vision-language pre-training. The survey distinguishes between tasks requiring internal knowledge, which the model must have acquired during pre-training, and those requiring external knowledge, which must be retrieved or provided at inference time. This distinction is important for understanding the limitations of current MLLMs, which may perform well on questions that can be answered from visual content alone but struggle with questions that require world knowledge or commonsense reasoning beyond what is directly observable in the image. The survey also highlights the role of multimodal fusion strategies in determining VQA performance, noting that models that achieve deeper integration of visual and linguistic representations tend to outperform those that treat the two modalities more independently.

Interpretability and transparency represent another important dimension of MLLM research, particularly as these models are deployed in high-stakes applications. [arxiv_2203.17247] introduces VL-InterpreT, an interactive visualization tool designed to illuminate the internal mechanisms of vision-language transformers. The tool tracks attention statistics across all layers for both vision and language components, visualizes cross-modal and intra-modal attention patterns, and provides interactive interfaces for exploring hidden representations. The authors motivate this work by observing that while interpretability tools have become available for NLP models, the internal workings of multimodal transformers remain largely opaque. This opacity is particularly concerning given the tendency of MLLMs to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect outputs, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as hallucination. Understanding the mechanisms by which these models integrate visual and linguistic information is a prerequisite for developing more reliable and trustworthy systems.

The question of whether vision-language pre-training genuinely improves the grounding of linguistic representations has been examined critically by [arxiv_2109.10246], which compares the semantic representations learned through VL pre-training versus text-only pre-training. Using clustering, probing, and commonsense question answering tasks in a language-only setting, the study finds that multimodal models fail to significantly outperform their text-only counterparts, suggesting that the visual signal does not straightforwardly improve the quality of linguistic representations in the ways that grounding theory would predict. This finding has important implications for the design of MLLMs, as it suggests that simply exposing a language model to visual data during pre-training may not be sufficient to produce genuinely grounded representations. More sophisticated alignment strategies, such as those involving contrastive learning, cross-modal attention, and region-level supervision, may be necessary to achieve the kind of deep semantic grounding that would enable MLLMs to reason reliably about the visual world.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here paints a picture of a field that has made extraordinary progress in a short period of time, moving from narrow specialist models to general-purpose assistants capable of handling a wide range of vision-language tasks. The comprehensive surveys provided by [arxiv_2402.12451] and [arxiv_2309.10020] document this trajectory in detail, analyzing architectural choices, alignment strategies, training techniques, and evaluation methodologies across a broad range of models and tasks. At the same time, the literature reveals persistent challenges around data quality, computational efficiency, fine-grained visual understanding, multimodal grounding, and the extension of these models to additional modalities and specialized domains. These challenges define the frontier of current research and motivate the continued development of new architectures, training paradigms, and evaluation frameworks for multimodal large language models.

The extension of MLLMs beyond the canonical vision-language dyad to encompass audio, video, and sensor modalities represents one of the most active frontiers in the field. [arxiv_2304.08345] and its companion publication [ss_478472] introduce VALOR, a Vision-Audio-Language Omni-perception pretraining model that jointly models relationships among all three modalities in an end-to-end manner. VALOR employs three separate unimodal encoders alongside a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, and introduces two pretext tasks—Multimodal Grouping and Multimodal Captioning—to encourage the model to discover correspondences across modalities without requiring densely annotated trimodal data. The significance of this work lies in its demonstration that audio provides complementary semantic information that neither vision nor language alone can supply, and that models trained to integrate all three modalities outperform their bimodal counterparts on tasks such as video question answering and audio captioning. This observation is reinforced by [arxiv_2410.05608], which surveys the broader landscape of multimodal pretrained models capable of processing text, images, audio, and video, and identifies the absence of large-scale trimodal datasets as a principal bottleneck for further progress. Together, these contributions underscore the importance of moving beyond the image-text paradigm that dominates much of the MLLM literature and investing in data collection and architectural innovations that can accommodate the full diversity of perceptual modalities.

Efficiency and deployability have emerged as critical concerns as MLLM architectures grow in scale and complexity. [arxiv_2408.06610] proposes CROME, a cross-modal adapter framework that enables efficient vision-language instruction tuning without requiring full retraining of the underlying language model. By inserting lightweight adapter modules at strategic points in the network, CROME achieves competitive zero-shot and task-specific performance at a fraction of the computational cost of full fine-tuning. Complementing this approach, [arxiv_2410.16236] introduces LLaVA-KD, a knowledge distillation framework specifically designed for MLLMs, in which a large-scale teacher model transfers multimodal knowledge to a smaller student model through a combination of token-level and feature-level distillation objectives. The authors demonstrate that small-scale MLLMs trained with this framework substantially close the performance gap with their larger counterparts on standard benchmarks, suggesting that distillation is a viable path toward deploying capable multimodal models in resource-constrained environments. [arxiv_2408.12902] takes a complementary approach by proposing an Inner-Adaptor Architecture that inserts multiple modality-specific adapters directly into the frozen layers of a large language model, thereby preserving the model's original natural language processing capabilities while endowing it with multimodal competencies. This strategy addresses a well-documented tension in MLLM training: fine-tuning on vision-language data tends to degrade performance on purely linguistic tasks, a form of catastrophic forgetting that limits the practical utility of the resulting models. The convergence of these efficiency-oriented contributions reflects a broader recognition that the field must develop methods that are not only performant but also practically deployable.

The challenge of fine-grained visual understanding—distinguishing between objects, attributes, and spatial relationships at a level of detail that supports precise reasoning—has motivated several targeted architectural and data-centric innovations. [arxiv_2308.13437] proposes Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT), which augments standard image-language instruction data with region-level position information, enabling the model to develop more precise cross-modal alignment between linguistic expressions and specific image regions. Similarly, [arxiv_2406.15768] introduces MR-MLLM, a framework that establishes mutual reinforcement between high-level multimodal comprehension and low-level visual perception by jointly training the model on both understanding and detection objectives. The authors argue that these two capabilities are mutually beneficial: richer semantic understanding improves the model's ability to localize objects, while more precise localization in turn supports more accurate semantic reasoning. This bidirectional coupling contrasts with the more common pipeline approach in which perception and comprehension are treated as sequential stages, and the empirical results reported suggest that joint training yields consistent improvements across both types of tasks. [arxiv_2403.08773] further explores multimodal representation learning through the Veagle framework, which integrates dynamic high-resolution image processing with a flexible visual connector to improve performance on tasks requiring detailed spatial understanding, such as visual grounding and document comprehension.

The quality and diversity of instruction-tuning data have been identified across multiple studies as a primary determinant of MLLM capability, motivating substantial investment in data construction methodologies. [arxiv_2307.04087] scales up visual instruction tuning by constructing a dataset of 3.2 million instruction-following samples derived from COCO image annotations, covering a broader range of task types and question formats than earlier datasets. The authors demonstrate that scaling instruction data yields consistent performance improvements across a range of multimodal benchmarks, and that diversity of task type is at least as important as raw data volume. [arxiv_2308.12714] addresses a complementary limitation by introducing VIGC, a Visual Instruction Generation and Correction framework that uses a multimodal model to iteratively generate and refine instruction-following data directly from images, bypassing the reliance on pre-annotated captions and bounding boxes that characterizes earlier data generation pipelines such as LLaVA. This approach produces instruction data that is more tightly grounded in actual image content, reducing the risk of hallucinated or inaccurate annotations. [arxiv_2406.06730] targets the specific limitation of text comprehension within images by introducing TRINS, a text-rich image instruction dataset built upon LAION with hybrid annotation strategies, demonstrating that targeted data augmentation can substantially improve a model's ability to read and reason about embedded textual content—a capability that is increasingly important for document understanding and scene text applications.

Benchmarking and evaluation methodology have themselves become active areas of research, as the community grapples with the difficulty of constructing assessments that are both comprehensive and resistant to gaming. [arxiv_2408.08632] surveys over 200 benchmarks and evaluation protocols for MLLMs, organizing them along dimensions of perception, cognition, reasoning, and domain-specific competence, and identifies systematic gaps in current evaluation practice, including insufficient coverage of compositional reasoning, temporal understanding, and robustness to distribution shift. [arxiv_2311.17092] introduces SEED-Bench-2, which extends earlier benchmarks by evaluating not only comprehension of single image-text inputs but also the generative capabilities of models that can produce both text and images, reflecting the emergence of unified multimodal generation models. [arxiv_2403.13164] examines multimodal in-context learning specifically, constructing VL-ICL Bench to probe the ability of vision-language models to adapt to novel tasks from few-shot multimodal exemplars, and finds that current models exhibit substantially weaker in-context learning capabilities in the multimodal setting than their text-only counterparts do in the purely linguistic setting. This finding points to an important asymmetry in the generalization capabilities of current MLLMs and motivates further research into the mechanisms by which visual and linguistic context interact during inference.

Interpretability research has advanced alongside architectural development, with recent work moving beyond visualization tools toward systematic mechanistic analyses. [arxiv_2412.02104] provides a comprehensive survey of explainability and interpretability methods for MLLMs, categorizing approaches along the dimensions of attribution, probing, mechanistic analysis, and natural language explanation, and identifying the cross-modal attribution problem—determining which visual regions and linguistic tokens jointly contribute to a given output—as a particularly underexplored challenge. [arxiv_2411.18620] takes a mechanistic approach by tracing the flow of cross-modal information through the layers of autoregressive MLLMs, finding that visual information is predominantly integrated into linguistic representations in the early-to-middle layers of the network, with later layers primarily performing linguistic processing. This layered integration pattern has implications for the design of efficient adaptation strategies, suggesting that interventions targeting the early integration layers may be more effective than those applied uniformly across the network. Collectively, these interpretability contributions complement the architectural and data-centric work reviewed above by providing the analytical tools necessary to understand why certain design choices succeed and to diagnose the failure modes that remain.

Domain-specific adaptation of MLLMs has also attracted growing attention, as general-purpose models trained on broad web-scale data often lack the specialized knowledge required for high-stakes professional applications. [arxiv_2409.07463] demonstrates this in the context of semiconductor manufacturing, where a teacher-student framework leverages GPT-4-generated instruction data to fine-tune smaller multimodal models for electron microscopy image analysis, achieving strong performance on domain-specific VQA and classification tasks despite the scarcity of labeled training data. [arxiv_2402.08360] pursues a related strategy for domain-specific visual tasks more broadly, developing a method to transform domain-specific visual datasets into instruction-following format and demonstrating that this approach enables MLLMs to handle tasks such as object detection and segmentation that are not naturally expressed as language generation problems. [arxiv_2502.09906] extends this line of work to precision agriculture by constructing a large multimodal dataset for insect understanding and training a foundation model capable of supporting vision-language dialogue about insect species, behavior, and agricultural impact. These domain-specific contributions collectively illustrate both the promise and the practical requirements of adapting general-purpose MLLMs to specialized settings: while the underlying architectures transfer well, meaningful performance in specialized domains typically requires targeted data collection, domain-aware instruction tuning, and evaluation protocols designed to capture the nuances of expert knowledge.
