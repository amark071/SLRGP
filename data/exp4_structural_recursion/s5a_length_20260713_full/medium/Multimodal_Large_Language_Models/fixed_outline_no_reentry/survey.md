# Multimodal Large Language Models (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, fundamentally redefining how computational systems perceive, reason about, and generate content that spans multiple sensory modalities. At its core, this research trajectory is motivated by the observation that human cognition is inherently multimodal, and that language alone is insufficient for capturing the full richness of real-world information. As [arxiv_2402.12451] articulates, connecting text and visual modalities plays an essential role in generative intelligence, and the success of large language models has catalyzed substantial research investment in systems capable of seamlessly integrating visual and textual information within a unified, dialogue-capable framework.

The intellectual lineage of MLLMs can be traced through a longer history of vision-language pre-training (VLP), which laid the groundwork for the more capable generalist systems that followed. [arxiv_2210.09263] provides a systematic account of this foundational period, organizing VLP methods into three broad categories: those targeting image-text tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and image-text retrieval; those addressing core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation; and those designed for video-text tasks such as video captioning and video question answering. This tripartite taxonomy is useful for appreciating the breadth of the pre-MLLM landscape and for understanding which capabilities were subsequently absorbed and extended by large-scale multimodal systems. The challenges identified in that earlier generation of work—including the difficulty of aligning heterogeneous modalities and the limited generalizability of specialist models—directly motivated the architectural innovations that define contemporary MLLMs.

The transition from specialist vision-language models to general-purpose multimodal assistants is perhaps the defining narrative of the field's recent evolution. [arxiv_2309.10020] frames this transition explicitly, tracing a trajectory from models pre-trained for specific purposes—such as dedicated visual understanding backbones and text-to-image generation systems—toward unified architectures capable of serving as general-purpose assistants. The survey identifies three particularly active research directions in this latter category: unified vision models inspired by LLMs, end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs, and the chaining of multimodal tools with LLMs. This progression reflects a broader paradigm shift in which the goal is no longer to optimize performance on a single, narrowly defined task, but to develop systems with flexible, instruction-following capabilities that can generalize across diverse visual and linguistic challenges. The contrast between these two generations of models is instructive: whereas earlier specialist systems required task-specific fine-tuning and were often brittle outside their training distribution, contemporary MLLMs leverage the representational power of large language model backbones to achieve a degree of compositional generalization that was previously unattainable.

A comprehensive treatment of the architectural and training dimensions of MLLMs is offered by [arxiv_2402.12451], which analyzes models along three principal axes: architectural choices, multimodal alignment strategies, and training techniques. The alignment problem—how to bridge the representational gap between visual encoders and language model decoders—is identified as a central technical challenge, and the survey documents a range of approaches, from lightweight projection layers to more elaborate cross-attention mechanisms. The paper also examines MLLMs across a wide range of downstream tasks, including visual grounding, image generation and editing, and domain-specific applications, underscoring the versatility that distinguishes this generation of models from their predecessors. Importantly, [arxiv_2402.12451] also compiles and describes training datasets and evaluation benchmarks, reflecting the field's growing awareness that progress requires not only architectural innovation but also rigorous, standardized evaluation infrastructure.

The scope of multimodality itself has expanded considerably beyond the image-text dyad that dominated early research. [arxiv_2410.05608] situates this expansion within a broader tutorial framework, noting that state-of-the-art multimodal models are now capable of integrating and processing diverse data forms including text, images, audio, and video. This extension beyond vision and language introduces new technical challenges related to temporal modeling, cross-modal synchronization, and the heterogeneous statistical properties of different signal types. The tutorial also emphasizes the role of instruction tuning as a mechanism for optimizing multimodal models for specific tasks, a technique that has proven particularly effective in adapting large pretrained models to downstream applications without requiring full retraining. The practical demonstrations highlighted in [arxiv_2410.05608]—including visual storytelling and visual question answering—illustrate how these technical advances translate into real-world capabilities.

The application of MLLMs to specialized domains represents another significant dimension of the literature. [arxiv_2409.07463] presents a framework for analyzing electron microscopy images in semiconductor manufacturing, employing a teacher-student approach in which a large pretrained multimodal model such as GPT-4 generates instruction-following data that is subsequently used to fine-tune a smaller, domain-adapted model. This work exemplifies a broader trend toward knowledge distillation from large to small multimodal models, addressing practical constraints related to computational cost, data security, and the scarcity of domain-specific labeled data. The framework described in [arxiv_2409.07463] merges knowledge engineering with machine learning, integrating domain expertise into the fine-tuning process in a manner that reduces reliance on extensive human annotation. This approach stands in productive contrast to the general-purpose orientation described in [arxiv_2309.10020] and [arxiv_2402.12451], illustrating that the field must simultaneously pursue both breadth of capability and depth of domain specialization.

A less frequently foregrounded but increasingly important concern in the MLLM literature is interpretability. [arxiv_2203.17247] addresses this gap by proposing VL-InterpreT, an interactive visualization tool designed to illuminate the internal mechanisms of vision-language transformers. The tool tracks attention head statistics across all layers for both visual and linguistic components, and visualizes both cross-modal and intra-modal attention patterns. The authors argue that as multimodal transformers become more capable and more widely deployed, understanding their inner workings becomes correspondingly more critical, both for improving model design and for ensuring trustworthiness. This concern for interpretability is notably underrepresented in the broader survey literature reviewed here, suggesting that while architectural and benchmark-oriented research has advanced rapidly, the interpretability of MLLMs remains a relatively open frontier.

Taken together, the literature reviewed in this section reveals a field characterized by rapid architectural innovation, expanding modality coverage, growing attention to domain-specific adaptation, and an emerging but still underdeveloped focus on interpretability. The progression from task-specific VLP models [arxiv_2210.09263] through the specialist-to-generalist transition [arxiv_2309.10020] to the current generation of instruction-tuned, dialogue-capable MLLMs [arxiv_2402.12451] reflects both the ambition and the technical maturity of the research community. At the same time, the diversity of approaches—from large general-purpose assistants to small domain-adapted models [arxiv_2409.07463], and from performance-oriented training to interpretability-focused analysis [arxiv_2203.17247]—underscores that multimodal large language model research is not a monolithic enterprise but a richly pluralistic one, animated by complementary and sometimes competing priorities.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together the representational power of large-scale language models with the perceptual richness of visual and auditory modalities. This subsection surveys the foundational architectures, training strategies, and evaluation frameworks that have shaped the current landscape of multimodal learning, drawing attention to both the remarkable capabilities these systems exhibit and the persistent challenges that motivate ongoing research.

A central concern in the development of MLLMs is how to effectively integrate visual and linguistic representations without incurring prohibitive computational costs. The PaLI model [arxiv_2209.06794] offers an influential early demonstration of what joint scaling across vision and language components can achieve. By coupling large pre-trained encoder-decoder language models with Vision Transformers (ViTs), PaLI capitalizes on the existing capabilities of both modalities and leverages the substantial investment already made in training them independently. Crucially, the authors find that scaling the vision component is not merely supplementary but essential: because existing language transformers are considerably larger than their vision counterparts, they train a 4-billion parameter ViT (ViT-e) to quantify the benefits of increased visual capacity. The resulting model generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, enabling it to perform a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages. PaLI thus establishes a template in which the joint scaling of heterogeneous components, rather than the scaling of language alone, drives multimodal performance.

While PaLI pursues scale as a primary lever, other work has focused on the efficiency of cross-modal adaptation. CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] addresses a practical limitation that affects many MLLMs: existing approaches often require expensive retraining of the language model backbone and offer limited adaptability to downstream tasks. In response, CROME introduces a gated cross-modal adapter that combines visual and textual representations before they are passed into a frozen LLM. This lightweight adapter is trained with a minimal number of parameters, yet it enables effective cross-modal understanding without modifying the underlying language model. The authors report superior zero-shot performance on standard visual question answering and instruction-following benchmarks, as well as strong results under task-specific fine-tuning. Compared to the full-scale joint training paradigm exemplified by PaLI, CROME represents a complementary philosophy: rather than scaling all components simultaneously, it seeks to achieve competitive performance through targeted, parameter-efficient adaptation. This distinction is practically significant, as it lowers the barrier to deploying and customizing MLLMs in resource-constrained settings.

The scope of multimodal integration has also expanded beyond the vision-language dyad to encompass audio. The VALOR model [ss_478472] proposes an omni-perceptual pretraining framework that jointly models the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. VALOR employs three separate encoders to produce single-modality representations and a decoder for multimodal conditional text generation. Two pretext tasks drive pretraining: Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA), which projects vision, language, and audio into a shared common space while simultaneously building pairwise and trimodal alignment, and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC), which trains the model to generate text conditioned on vision, audio, or both. By treating audio as a first-class modality rather than an afterthought, VALOR extends the representational ambitions of models like PaLI into a genuinely trimodal regime. This is a meaningful departure from the dominant vision-language paradigm and raises important questions about how alignment objectives should be designed when more than two modalities must be reconciled simultaneously.

Alongside architectural innovation, the field has increasingly recognized the need for rigorous and comprehensive evaluation. The LVLM-eHub benchmark [arxiv_2306.09265] addresses this need by providing a systematic evaluation of publicly available large vision-language models across six categories of multimodal capabilities, including visual question answering and embodied artificial intelligence, assessed over 47 standard text-related visual benchmarks. The benchmark evaluates eight representative LVLMs, including InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, through both quantitative capability assessment and an online arena platform that captures user-level evaluation in open-world question-answering scenarios. Among the findings, the study reveals that instruction-tuned LVLMs demonstrate notable advantages, underscoring the importance of instruction-following as a training objective. A subsequent and expanded version of this benchmark [ss_478371] scales the evaluation to 13 representative LVLMs and 42 in-domain text-related visual benchmarks, adding object hallucination as an explicit evaluation category and investigating how specific model configurations and modality alignment strategies influence performance. Together, these two works chart the evolution of evaluation methodology in the field, moving from a relatively contained benchmark toward a more comprehensive hub that can accommodate the rapidly growing ecosystem of multimodal models.

The Veagle model [arxiv_2403.08773] situates itself explicitly within the challenges identified by such evaluations. Noting that current vision-language models and MLLMs frequently struggle to accurately interpret images and answer questions in real-world scenarios, the authors propose a novel approach to enhance multimodal representation learning. Veagle responds to the limitations observed in existing models by introducing architectural modifications aimed at improving the fidelity with which visual information is encoded and integrated with language. This work is representative of a broader trend in which evaluation findings directly motivate architectural refinement, creating a productive feedback loop between benchmarking and model development.

Taken together, the works reviewed here illuminate several defining tensions in the MLLM literature. The first is the tension between scale and efficiency: PaLI demonstrates that joint scaling of vision and language yields powerful models, while CROME shows that frozen backbones combined with lightweight adapters can achieve competitive results at a fraction of the cost. The second tension concerns modality coverage: most models focus on vision and language, but VALOR argues compellingly that audio constitutes an equally important perceptual channel whose omission leaves significant capabilities unrealized. The third tension is between capability and evaluation: the LVLM-eHub benchmarks [arxiv_2306.09265, ss_478371] reveal that impressive zero-shot performance on standard tasks can coexist with systematic failures such as object hallucination, suggesting that aggregate metrics may obscure important weaknesses. Models like Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] and CROME [arxiv_2408.06610] represent attempts to address these weaknesses through targeted architectural innovation. Collectively, this body of work establishes the conceptual and empirical foundations upon which more specialized investigations into multimodal large language models must build.

### Evidence theme 3

Visual Question Answering (VQA) has emerged as one of the most consequential benchmark tasks for evaluating multimodal large language models (MLLMs), precisely because it demands the simultaneous exercise of natural language understanding, visual perception, and knowledge-based reasoning. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2411.17558], the field has progressively moved from relatively narrow image-caption alignment objectives toward sophisticated architectures capable of integrating vision-language pretraining with external knowledge retrieval, reflecting a broader maturation in how researchers conceptualize the relationship between linguistic and visual representations. This trajectory is not merely one of incremental performance gains; it represents a fundamental rethinking of how modalities should be fused, grounded, and jointly reasoned over.

A foundational tension in this literature concerns whether training on paired visual and linguistic data genuinely improves the semantic quality of the resulting representations, or whether the benefits of multimodal pretraining are largely confined to task-specific benchmarks. [arxiv_2109.10246] addresses this question directly, comparing the internal linguistic representations of vision-and-language models against their text-only counterparts through clustering, probing, and commonsense question answering evaluations. Their findings are notably sobering: multimodal models fail to significantly outperform text-only baselines in purely linguistic settings, suggesting that the grounding signal provided by visual co-training does not straightforwardly propagate into richer lexical semantics. This result has important implications for the broader MLLM research agenda, as it implies that architectural integration of visual encoders does not automatically resolve the grounding problem that has long been identified as a limitation of language models trained exclusively on text. Future work, the authors argue, must more carefully examine how visual information is encoded and transferred into the linguistic component of these systems.

The architectural question of how best to integrate visual encoders with large language models has consequently attracted substantial research attention. [arxiv_2310.03211] conducts a systematic comparative analysis of multimodal instruction tuning approaches, evaluating them across a diverse task suite that includes complex reasoning, open-ended conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification. Through rigorous benchmarking and ablation experiments, the study reveals that while instruction-tuned multimodal variants demonstrate promising zero-shot generalization, current approaches still exhibit meaningful limitations, particularly on tasks requiring fine-grained visual understanding and multi-step reasoning. The study's contribution lies in its provision of principled guidance for architectural choices when grafting vision encoders onto pretrained language models, a process sometimes described as model grafting, which has become a dominant paradigm for constructing MLLMs without training from scratch.

One specific limitation that has been identified within this paradigm is the coarseness of cross-modal alignment achieved through standard visual instruction tuning. Existing methods typically rely on image-level and language-level instruction data, which, while effective for global scene understanding, fall short when tasks require localized, region-specific comprehension. [arxiv_2308.13437] addresses this gap by proposing Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT), which augments the standard MLLM architecture with an additional region-level vision encoder. By constructing a dedicated image-region-language instruction dataset through multiple data generation strategies, PVIT achieves finer-grained alignment between visual and linguistic representations. Both quantitative and qualitative evaluations reported in that work demonstrate improvements in tasks that require the model to reason about specific spatial regions within an image, a capability that standard instruction tuning pipelines systematically underserve. This work thus represents a targeted response to the architectural critique raised by [arxiv_2310.03211], demonstrating that the granularity of the alignment objective is a critical design variable.

The quality and diversity of instruction-tuning data constitute a parallel axis of concern. [arxiv_2308.12714] identifies a practical bottleneck in the prevailing data generation paradigm: leading approaches such as LLaVA rely on language-only GPT-4 to produce instruction data, a process that requires pre-annotated captions and bounding boxes and consequently suffers from an inability to reason about fine-grained image details. As a remedy, the authors propose Visual Instruction Generation and Correction (VIGC), a framework that leverages existing MLLMs to iteratively generate and self-correct instruction-tuning data for vision-language tasks. The key insight is that even imperfect MLLMs can be harnessed productively if their outputs are subjected to a correction mechanism that filters or refines hallucinated or inadequate responses. This approach acknowledges a well-documented weakness of current MLLMs—their tendency to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content—and converts it from an obstacle into a manageable component of the data pipeline.

A related but distinct challenge concerns the capacity of MLLMs to read and interpret text that is embedded within images, a capability sometimes referred to as scene text understanding or, more broadly, text-rich image comprehension. [arxiv_2406.06730] observes that the majority of visually-tuned models struggle with this modality despite its practical importance, attributing the deficiency primarily to the scarcity of appropriate training data. In response, the authors introduce TRINS, a Text-Rich image INStruction dataset constructed from LAION using hybrid machine-assisted and human-assisted annotation strategies. Containing over 39,000 text-rich images and more than 102,000 questions, TRINS is notable not only for its scale but also for the length and complexity of its annotations, which substantially exceed those of comparable datasets and thereby introduce new challenges for model comprehension. Alongside the dataset, the authors present a Language-vision Reading Assistant architecture designed to exploit the richer annotation structure. Taken together, [arxiv_2406.06730] and [arxiv_2308.12714] converge on the conclusion that data quality and coverage are at least as consequential as architectural innovation in determining MLLM capability, a perspective that complements the architectural focus of [arxiv_2308.13437] and [arxiv_2310.03211].

Collectively, these works illuminate a set of interrelated challenges that define the current frontier of MLLM research. The grounding problem identified by [arxiv_2109.10246] motivates the architectural refinements proposed by [arxiv_2308.13437], while the data scarcity and quality issues foregrounded by [arxiv_2308.12714] and [arxiv_2406.06730] underscore that even well-designed architectures are constrained by the instruction-tuning corpora available to them. The comprehensive benchmarking of [arxiv_2310.03211] provides an empirical scaffold for situating these contributions relative to one another, and the survey perspective of [arxiv_2411.17558] contextualizes them within the longer developmental arc of VQA research. Taken together, these contributions suggest that progress in MLLMs will require coordinated advances across representation learning, architectural design, and data curation rather than improvements along any single dimension in isolation.
