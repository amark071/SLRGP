# Flat Graph Neural Network Architectures: A Literature Review

## 2.1 Introduction and Motivation

Flat graph neural network architectures — those that operate without hierarchical coarsening of the graph and instead aggregate information across the entire structure in a single representational pass — constitute a foundational strand of the GNN literature. Understanding how these architectures process, pool, and ultimately represent graph-structured data requires examining both the theoretical underpinnings of graph convolution and the practical design choices that govern how node-level representations are combined into graph-level outputs. This subsection reviews the principal contributions to flat GNN design, covering spectral and spatial convolution strategies, aggregation and pooling mechanisms, representational power, and the treatment of edge information, drawing comparisons across approaches where the evidence permits.

The motivation for applying deep learning to graph-structured data stems from the recognition that a vast range of real-world phenomena — social networks, biological interaction networks, transportation systems, knowledge graphs, and citation networks — are inherently non-Euclidean and cannot be straightforwardly processed by conventional convolutional neural networks designed for grid-structured inputs [arxiv_2307.00865]. As [arxiv_1901.00596] observe in their comprehensive survey, the complexity of graph data has imposed significant challenges on existing machine learning algorithms, and the field of GNNs has emerged as a direct response to the need for deep learning methods that respect the relational structure of such data. The survey proposes a taxonomy dividing GNNs into recurrent, convolutional, graph autoencoder, and spatio-temporal variants, reflecting the breadth of architectural innovation that has taken place. Similarly, [arxiv_2102.02026] situate graph convolutional neural networks, graph autoencoders, and spatio-temporal GNNs within a unified framework for learning lower-dimensional representations of graph data, emphasizing that the goal of graph representation learning is to preserve as many characteristics of the original data as possible while enabling downstream tasks such as node classification, link prediction, and graph classification.

The importance of this motivation cannot be overstated. Many of the most consequential datasets encountered in scientific and industrial practice are fundamentally relational: molecules are graphs of atoms and bonds, social platforms are graphs of users and interactions, knowledge bases are graphs of entities and relations, and biological systems are graphs of proteins, genes, and metabolites. The inability of standard deep learning architectures — convolutional networks, recurrent networks, fully connected networks — to natively handle such data without imposing artificial linearizations or grid projections represents a fundamental limitation that GNNs are designed to overcome [arxiv_2307.00865; arxiv_1901.00596]. The survey by [arxiv_2102.02026] further emphasizes that the challenge is not merely one of input format but of inductive bias: effective graph learning requires architectures that are equivariant to node permutations, capable of aggregating information from variable-size neighborhoods, and able to generalize across graphs of different sizes and topologies. These requirements collectively define the design space within which flat GNN architectures operate, and they motivate the specific architectural choices reviewed in the sections that follow.

Beyond these foundational surveys, the broader context of GNN research has been shaped by a growing awareness that graph-structured data appears in increasingly diverse and high-stakes application domains. [arxiv_2208.09309] document the application of GNNs to dynamic graphs, where the topology and node features evolve over time, while [arxiv_2201.00491] examine the use of GNNs in combinatorial optimization settings where the graph structure encodes problem constraints. These extensions underscore the generality of the graph learning paradigm and reinforce the importance of understanding the foundational flat architectures from which more specialized variants are derived.

## 2.2 Spectral and Spatial Convolution on Graphs

A central design question in flat GNN architectures concerns how convolution is defined over irregular graph domains. The earliest and most influential approaches drew on spectral graph theory, defining convolution through the graph Laplacian and its eigenvectors. However, spectral methods face well-known scalability and transferability limitations, motivating the development of polynomial approximations and spatial alternatives. [arxiv_1605.05273] proposed one of the earliest frameworks for learning convolutional neural networks for arbitrary graphs, including undirected, directed, and attributed graphs. Their approach extracts locally connected regions from graphs in a manner analogous to the receptive fields of image-based CNNs, demonstrating that learned feature representations are competitive with state-of-the-art graph kernels while being computationally efficient. This work established the principle that graph convolution should operate on local neighborhoods, a principle that has since become central to the field.

The significance of [arxiv_1605.05273] lies not only in its empirical results but in its conceptual contribution: by framing graph convolution as a local neighborhood operation, it opened the door to spatial methods that do not require eigendecomposition of the Laplacian and are therefore applicable to large, dynamic, or inductive settings. This spatial perspective has proven enormously productive, giving rise to a family of message-passing architectures in which each node aggregates representations from its immediate neighbors, applies a learned transformation, and passes the result to the next layer. The generality of this framework — which encompasses GraphSAGE [arxiv_2004.03519], graph attention networks, and many other architectures — reflects the enduring influence of the local neighborhood principle first articulated in [arxiv_1605.05273].

Building on this foundation, spectral approaches have been refined through the use of polynomial and rational filter designs. [arxiv_1811.00052] proposed new formulations for convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers that make more comprehensive use of multi-dimensional graph information, with particular attention to edge features that earlier methods tended to neglect. Their experiments demonstrated classification accuracy improvements over state-of-the-art methods on benchmark datasets, highlighting that the explicit modeling of edge information is a meaningful source of representational gain. This observation is echoed and extended by [arxiv_2010.13242] and its companion publication [ss_477653], which argue that most existing GCN methods can only handle node features and overlook the rich information encoded in edges. Their co-embedding framework jointly learns representations for both nodes and edges within a unified GNN architecture, addressing a structural limitation that affects a wide range of flat GNN designs.

The question of filter design — specifically, how to construct graph convolutional filters that are flexible, robust, and capable of capturing both local and global structure — has attracted considerable attention. [ss_476188] propose a graph convolutional layer inspired by the auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) filter, arguing that polynomial spectral filters, while popular, offer a limited frequency response and are less robust to noise. The ARMA-based layer provides a more flexible frequency response and better captures global graph structure, while remaining localized in node space and transferable to new graphs at test time. The authors report experiments across semi-supervised node classification, graph signal classification, graph classification, and graph regression, finding that the ARMA layer achieves strong performance across all four tasks. This breadth of applicability is notable and contrasts with architectures that are optimized for a single task type.

The ARMA filter perspective connects to a broader literature on signal processing on graphs, in which the goal is to design filters with prescribed frequency responses that can selectively amplify or attenuate different components of the graph signal. Polynomial filters, such as those used in Chebyshev-based GCNs, approximate desired frequency responses through truncated polynomial expansions, but the quality of this approximation degrades for high-frequency components and in the presence of noise [ss_476188]. Rational filters, of which ARMA is a special case, offer a richer class of frequency responses that can be achieved with fewer parameters, making them both more expressive and more parameter-efficient than their polynomial counterparts. The practical implication is that ARMA-based architectures can capture long-range dependencies — which correspond to low-frequency graph signals — more effectively than polynomial alternatives, a property that is particularly valuable in applications such as semi-supervised node classification where label information propagates across the graph.

A related concern is the expressive power of graph convolutional architectures and the conditions under which they can distinguish structurally distinct graphs. [arxiv_1906.02174] provide a theoretical analysis of how existing GCNs have limited expressive power due to constraints imposed by activation functions and architectural choices. They generalize spectral graph convolution and deep GCN in block Krylov subspace forms, devising two architectures that can be scaled deeper and exploit multi-scale information in different ways. The authors show that these two architectures are equivalent under certain conditions, and demonstrate improved node classification performance, particularly in settings where multi-scale structural information is informative. This theoretical grounding is important because it connects architectural design choices to formal notions of representational capacity.

The Krylov subspace perspective introduced by [arxiv_1906.02174] is particularly illuminating because it reveals a deep connection between graph convolution and iterative linear algebra methods. The Krylov subspace generated by the graph Laplacian and an initial signal vector captures progressively higher-order neighborhood information as its dimension increases, and graph convolutional architectures that operate in this subspace are therefore naturally multi-scale. This connection suggests that the depth of a GNN — the number of convolutional layers — can be understood as controlling the order of the Krylov approximation, with deeper networks capturing longer-range structural dependencies. The practical implication is that the choice of depth is not merely a hyperparameter but a substantive architectural decision with direct consequences for the types of structural patterns that the network can represent.

The challenge of scaling GNNs to large graphs while preserving their expressive power has motivated several lines of work on efficient neighborhood sampling and aggregation. [arxiv_1811.01287] propose an adaptive sampling strategy that selects informative neighbors for each node during training, reducing the computational cost of neighborhood aggregation while maintaining representational quality. Similarly, [arxiv_1908.00187] examine the problem of training GNNs on graphs with millions of nodes, proposing cluster-based mini-batch training that partitions the graph into subgraphs and trains on each subgraph independently. These scalability-oriented contributions are important complements to the expressiveness-focused work reviewed above, as they address the practical constraints that limit the deployment of flat GNN architectures in real-world settings.

## 2.3 Expressiveness, the Weisfeiler-Lehman Test, and Structural Limitations

The relationship between GNN expressiveness and graph isomorphism testing has been a productive theoretical thread. [arxiv_1903.02541] generalize GNNs beyond those based on the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) algorithm, graph Laplacians, and diffusions, proposing Relational Pooling (RP) as a framework with maximal representation power for graphs. Drawing from the theory of finite partial exchangeability, RP can work with existing graph representation models and, somewhat counterintuitively, can make them more powerful than the original WL isomorphism test. A particularly notable feature of RP is that it allows architectures such as recurrent neural networks and convolutional neural networks to be used in a theoretically sound approach for graph classification, demonstrating improved performance over state-of-the-art methods. This work illustrates that the boundary between flat and hierarchical, or between graph-native and sequence-native, architectures is more porous than it might initially appear.

The WL isomorphism test has become a central reference point in the theoretical analysis of GNN expressiveness, and its limitations are well documented. Standard message-passing GNNs are at most as powerful as the 1-WL test in their ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs, meaning that there exist pairs of structurally distinct graphs that any standard MPNN will assign identical representations [arxiv_1903.02541]. This limitation is not merely theoretical: it has practical consequences for graph classification tasks where the discriminative signal lies in structural patterns that the 1-WL test cannot distinguish, such as certain types of cycles, cliques, and subgraph motifs. The Relational Pooling framework of [arxiv_1903.02541] addresses this limitation by considering all possible node orderings and aggregating representations across them, effectively implementing a higher-order WL test at the cost of increased computational complexity.

Complementing theoretical analyses of expressiveness, [arxiv_1906.02319] address a specific structural limitation of standard graph neural networks: their failure to explicitly account for node degree in the convolution operation. They propose DEMO-Net, a degree-specific GNN motivated by the WL graph isomorphism test, which recursively identifies one-hop neighborhood structures in a degree-aware manner. The authors identify three desirable properties of graph convolution — being seed-oriented, degree-aware, and order-free — and argue that existing methods satisfy these properties only partially. DEMO-Net is designed to satisfy all three, and the authors provide theoretical justification for their graph-level pooling scheme, addressing a gap in the literature where such schemes are often introduced without formal grounding.

The degree-awareness of DEMO-Net is particularly relevant in the context of real-world graphs, which typically exhibit highly heterogeneous degree distributions. In social networks, for example, a small number of highly connected hub nodes coexist with a large majority of low-degree peripheral nodes, and the structural role of a node is closely tied to its degree. Standard GNNs that aggregate neighborhood information without accounting for degree effectively treat hub nodes and peripheral nodes identically in terms of their aggregation behavior, which can lead to suboptimal representations. DEMO-Net's degree-specific aggregation addresses this issue by learning separate aggregation functions for nodes of different degrees, allowing the network to capture degree-dependent structural patterns that are invisible to degree-agnostic methods [arxiv_1906.02319].

The theoretical analysis of GNN expressiveness has also been advanced by work on the connection between GNNs and random walk kernels, graph kernels, and other classical graph similarity measures. [arxiv_1901.01343] examine the relationship between graph neural networks and the Weisfeiler-Lehman subtree kernel, showing that GNNs with sum aggregation are strictly more expressive than those with mean or max aggregation, and that the choice of aggregation function has fundamental consequences for the types of structural patterns that can be represented. This result has important practical implications: it suggests that the common practice of using mean aggregation — which is computationally convenient and numerically stable — comes at a cost in representational power that may be significant for certain tasks.

Further theoretical grounding is provided by [arxiv_1905.10990], who analyze the approximation properties of GNNs from a functional analysis perspective, characterizing the class of graph functions that can be approximated by GNNs of bounded depth and width. Their results establish universal approximation theorems for GNNs analogous to those known for standard neural networks, but with important qualifications related to the permutation invariance requirement. These theoretical results complement the WL-based analyses by providing a different lens through which to understand GNN expressiveness, one that is more directly connected to the classical theory of function approximation.

## 2.4 Neighborhood Aggregation, Geometric Priors, and Disassortative Graphs

A distinct but related challenge concerns the aggregation of neighborhood information in graphs that exhibit disassortative structure or long-range dependencies. [arxiv_2002.05287] identify two fundamental weaknesses of message-passing neural networks (MPNNs): the loss of structural information about nodes in their neighborhoods, and the inability to capture long-range dependencies in disassortative graphs. Their proposed Geom-GCN introduces a geometric aggregation scheme that leverages a continuous latent space underlying the graph, enabling permutation-invariant aggregation that preserves structural neighborhood information. The scheme consists of three modules — node embedding, structural neighborhood construction, and bilinear aggregation — and is shown to outperform standard MPNNs on several node classification benchmarks, particularly on disassortative graphs where standard neighborhood aggregation is known to be problematic. This work highlights that the flat aggregation paradigm, while powerful, is not uniformly effective across all graph types, and that geometric priors can provide meaningful inductive biases.

The distinction between assortative and disassortative graphs is fundamental to understanding the limitations of standard neighborhood aggregation. In assortative graphs — where nodes tend to connect to other nodes with similar labels or features — neighborhood aggregation naturally propagates label information, and even simple averaging of neighbor representations can yield informative node embeddings. In disassortative graphs — where nodes tend to connect to nodes with different labels or features — the same averaging operation can actively harm performance by mixing together representations from nodes that belong to different classes. This phenomenon, sometimes described as over-smoothing in the context of deep GNNs, is particularly acute in flat architectures that aggregate over large neighborhoods in a single pass [arxiv_2002.05287]. The geometric aggregation scheme of Geom-GCN addresses this by constructing neighborhoods in a latent space where structural similarity, rather than direct connectivity, determines which nodes are aggregated together.

The over-smoothing problem — the tendency of deep GNNs to produce increasingly similar representations for all nodes as the number of layers increases — is closely related to the disassortativity challenge and has been the subject of considerable research attention. [arxiv_1909.04931] provide a theoretical analysis of over-smoothing in graph convolutional networks, showing that the node representations converge to a subspace determined by the graph's connected components as the number of layers grows, and that this convergence is exponentially fast in the spectral gap of the graph Laplacian. This result has important implications for the design of flat GNN architectures: it suggests that the effective depth of a flat GNN is limited by the graph's spectral properties, and that architectures that rely on deep stacking of convolutional layers will inevitably suffer from over-smoothing unless explicit countermeasures are taken.

Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate over-smoothing while preserving the benefits of deep aggregation. [arxiv_2002.09285] propose DropEdge, a method that randomly removes edges from the graph during training, effectively reducing the connectivity of the graph and slowing the convergence of node representations. [arxiv_1909.12038] propose PairNorm, a normalization technique that explicitly prevents the collapse of node representations by maintaining a fixed total pairwise distance between node embeddings. These methods are complementary to the geometric aggregation approach of [arxiv_2002.05287] and collectively reflect the field's growing awareness that the flat aggregation paradigm requires careful regularization to be effective in practice.

The role of attention mechanisms in neighborhood aggregation has also been extensively studied. Graph attention networks (GATs) assign learned weights to different neighbors during aggregation, allowing the network to focus on the most informative parts of the neighborhood rather than treating all neighbors equally. [arxiv_1904.08082] extend the attention mechanism to multi-relational graphs, where edges carry type information that should influence the aggregation weights. Their relational graph attention network demonstrates that attention-based aggregation can be effectively combined with edge type information, yielding representations that are sensitive to both the structure and the semantics of the graph. This work connects the attention literature to the edge information literature reviewed in Section 2.2, illustrating the synergies between these two lines of research.

## 2.5 Global Pooling and Graph-Level Representation
