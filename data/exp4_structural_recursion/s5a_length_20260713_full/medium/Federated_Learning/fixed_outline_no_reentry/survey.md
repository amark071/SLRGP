# Federated Learning (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm in machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across distributed data sources without requiring the centralization of raw data. This approach addresses a fundamental tension in modern data-driven systems: the need to harness large, diverse datasets for effective model training while simultaneously respecting legal constraints, organizational boundaries, and individual privacy expectations. As [arxiv_2104.14362] observe, data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across end-user devices, geographic regions, and organizations, and regulatory frameworks often prohibit the direct sharing of such resources. Federated learning resolves this tension by allowing participants to collaboratively train models while keeping their data local, thereby obeying applicable laws and preserving data security and privacy.

The conceptual foundations of federated learning can be understood as an evolution from classical distributed machine learning. In conventional distributed settings, data or gradients are exchanged freely among nodes and a central server, enabling efficient optimization but exposing sensitive information. Federated learning introduces a more constrained communication protocol in which only locally trained model parameters or computed gradients are transmitted, rather than the underlying data itself [arxiv_2301.01299]. This architectural distinction is not merely technical; it reflects a principled commitment to privacy-by-design, wherein the system's structure itself limits the exposure of sensitive information. [arxiv_2104.14362] formalize this by proposing a functional architecture for federated learning systems and a taxonomy of related techniques, covering distributed training procedures, data communication strategies, and security mechanisms, thereby providing a structured lens through which the field's development can be assessed.

Despite its conceptual elegance, federated learning faces a range of practical challenges that have motivated substantial research effort. One of the most extensively studied problems concerns the statistical heterogeneity of data across participating clients. In idealized settings, federated learning assumes that local datasets are independent and identically distributed (IID), but in practice this assumption is routinely violated. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a detailed analysis of the influence of non-IID data on both parametric and non-parametric machine learning models, examining the problem across both horizontal and vertical federated learning configurations. Their survey demonstrates that models trained under federated conditions with non-IID data consistently underperform relative to centralized baselines, and they systematically review the strategies proposed to mitigate this degradation. The non-IID problem is thus not a peripheral concern but a central obstacle to the practical deployment of federated learning, and its resolution remains an active area of inquiry.

Communication efficiency constitutes another major axis of challenge. Because federated learning typically involves a large number of clients—potentially millions of edge devices—the volume of model updates transmitted per training round can be enormous, and the communication overhead can become a bottleneck that limits scalability and increases latency. [arxiv_2208.01200] provide a contemporary survey of communication challenges in federated learning, organizing their analysis around three perspectives: communication efficiency, communication environment, and communication resource allocation. Their work highlights that the practical deployment of federated learning requires not only algorithmic innovations but also careful attention to network conditions, bandwidth constraints, and the heterogeneous connectivity of participating devices. This multi-dimensional framing of communication challenges complements the broader architectural surveys by situating federated learning within the realities of networked systems.

Privacy, while a founding motivation for federated learning, is not automatically guaranteed by the framework's basic design. The exchange of model updates or gradients can itself leak sensitive information about local datasets through inference attacks, gradient inversion, and membership inference, among other threat vectors. Several lines of work have sought to strengthen privacy guarantees through formal mechanisms. [arxiv_2003.13761] address the risk of privacy leakage arising from information exchange between edge devices and a central server, particularly in settings where the server is not fully trusted. They develop a federated learning approach based on concentrated differential privacy, aiming to provide rigorous privacy guarantees without substantially degrading model utility or convergence behavior. This work highlights a recurring tension in privacy-preserving federated learning: stronger privacy guarantees, such as those provided by differential privacy, typically impose costs on model accuracy and training efficiency, and managing this trade-off is a central concern of the field.

An alternative approach to privacy protection eschews differential privacy in favor of controlling the communication topology among participants. [arxiv_2012.07183] develop a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that formulates the distributed aggregation problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) and proposes a communication pattern that minimizes privacy leakage by limiting which participants can observe each other's updates in a given aggregation round. Unlike [arxiv_2003.13761], who rely on noise injection to achieve differential privacy, [arxiv_2012.07183] achieve privacy guarantees against honest-but-curious adversaries through structural constraints on communication, drawing inspiration from combinatorial design principles. This contrast illustrates the diversity of approaches to privacy in federated learning and the absence of a single dominant solution, with different methods offering different trade-offs between privacy strength, computational overhead, and model quality.

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals federated learning as a rich and multifaceted research domain. [arxiv_2301.01299] situate recent advances within a systematic framework, noting that while numerous surveys exist, many focus on specific perspectives or lack coverage of the latest developments, motivating continued synthesis efforts. The field's challenges—statistical heterogeneity, communication overhead, and privacy leakage—are deeply interconnected: for instance, techniques that reduce communication rounds to improve efficiency may exacerbate the effects of non-IID data, while privacy mechanisms that add noise to gradients can interact adversely with convergence under heterogeneous data distributions. Understanding these interactions is essential for designing federated learning systems that are simultaneously efficient, accurate, and privacy-preserving, and it is this integrative challenge that defines the frontier of current research.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed participants without requiring the direct exchange of raw data. While this architecture provides an inherent degree of privacy by design, it does not in itself offer formal privacy guarantees, and a substantial body of research has therefore focused on augmenting federated learning with stronger cryptographic and statistical privacy mechanisms. The two most prominent such mechanisms are differential privacy (DP) and secure aggregation, and much of the recent literature has been concerned with how these techniques can be combined effectively, what trade-offs they introduce, and how the underlying communication topology—centralized versus decentralized—shapes both the privacy guarantees and the practical utility of the resulting models.

A foundational tension in this space concerns the relationship between privacy and model utility. Differential privacy, which introduces calibrated noise into the learning process to bound the information that can be inferred about any individual training sample, necessarily degrades model quality when noise levels are high. This trade-off is well documented and has motivated considerable effort to identify parameter settings and algorithmic designs that preserve acceptable utility while maintaining meaningful privacy budgets [arxiv_2311.16008]. Experimental investigations using benchmark datasets have confirmed that the choice of DP parameters—including the noise multiplier and clipping threshold—is central to navigating this trade-off, and that poorly chosen configurations can render the resulting model practically useless even when the formal privacy guarantee is technically satisfied [arxiv_2311.16008]. These findings underscore that differential privacy in federated learning is not merely a theoretical concern but a practical engineering challenge requiring careful calibration.

Secure aggregation addresses a complementary vulnerability: even when raw data is never shared, a malicious or curious aggregation server may be able to infer sensitive information from the model updates submitted by individual participants. Cryptographic secure aggregation protocols ensure that the server learns only the aggregate of all submitted updates, not the individual contributions. However, combining secure aggregation with differential privacy introduces additional complexity, particularly when participants perform multiple local optimization steps between communication rounds—a common practice adopted to reduce communication overhead. Prior work has generally required participants to communicate after each local step in order to fully exploit the privacy amplification benefits of secure aggregation at fine-grained protection granularities such as the sample level. Recent analysis has shown that this constraint can be relaxed: a refined analytical approach allows participants to perform multiple local optimization steps while still benefiting from the formal privacy guarantees that secure aggregation enables, yielding higher-utility models without sacrificing the privacy accounting [arxiv_2407.19286]. This result is significant because it reconciles two previously competing desiderata—communication efficiency and strong privacy guarantees—within a single coherent framework.

The design of the aggregation infrastructure itself is another axis along which federated learning systems vary substantially. The dominant paradigm assumes a centralized coordinator or parameter server that collects updates from all participants and computes the global model. This architecture is operationally convenient but introduces a single point of failure and concentrates trust in the server, which may itself be adversarial or compromised. Decentralized federated learning, in which participants communicate directly with one another according to a peer-to-peer topology rather than routing all updates through a central node, has been proposed as a structural remedy to this vulnerability [arxiv_2306.02570]. The shift to decentralized communication, however, introduces its own set of challenges: convergence behavior depends on the spectral properties of the communication graph, data heterogeneity across nodes complicates aggregation, and the absence of a coordinator makes it harder to enforce consistent privacy mechanisms across all participants. A systematic treatment of these challenges identifies the model, data, and communication dimensions as the three primary axes along which decentralized federated learning must be analyzed, and argues that understanding these dimensions is essential for translating decentralized optimization theory into practical federated learning systems [arxiv_2306.02570].

Privacy-preserving decentralized federated learning is particularly challenging in dynamic environments where the communication graph between participants changes over successive rounds of aggregation. A consensus-based algorithm designed for this setting integrates the Metropolis-Hastings method to update the weighted adjacency matrix in response to changes in the communication topology, and incorporates Shamir's secret sharing scheme to ensure that no participant learns the individual updates of its neighbors during the consensus process [arxiv_2210.00325]. This approach provides formal correctness and privacy guarantees even under high mobility, where the set of available communication links may vary unpredictably between rounds. The use of secret sharing as the cryptographic primitive here is notable because it distributes trust across multiple parties rather than relying on a single trusted aggregator, which aligns with the broader motivation for decentralization.

Secret sharing also forms the cryptographic foundation of SCOTCH, a multi-party secure computation framework specifically designed for federated aggregation [arxiv_2201.07730]. SCOTCH addresses the concern that even a decentralized aggregation protocol may be vulnerable if a subset of participants collude or if the aggregation nodes themselves are adversarial. By deploying MPC primitives based on secret sharing, SCOTCH provides strict privacy guarantees against both curious aggregators and colluding data owners, while maintaining communication overheads that are competitive with existing state-of-the-art approaches. The emphasis on communication efficiency is important because cryptographic protocols for secure aggregation have historically been criticized for their substantial overhead, which can make them impractical in bandwidth-constrained federated settings.

Beyond differential privacy and cryptographic secure aggregation, alternative privacy mechanisms have also been explored. One line of work proposes the use of chaotic maps-based encryption as a substitute for or complement to differential privacy, arguing that chaotic encryption can provide a strong layer of privacy protection while also addressing the non-IID data challenge that arises when participants hold data drawn from heterogeneous distributions [arxiv_2402.10145]. Experimental comparisons between differential privacy and chaotic-based encryption in federated deep learning settings suggest that both approaches can improve privacy without catastrophically degrading model performance, even when the underlying data distributions differ substantially across participants. This comparison is instructive because it situates differential privacy within a broader landscape of privacy-enhancing technologies rather than treating it as the sole available option.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals a field grappling with the simultaneous demands of strong formal privacy guarantees, high model utility, communication efficiency, and robustness to adversarial participants and infrastructure failures. The convergence of differential privacy, secure aggregation, and decentralized communication topologies represents the current frontier of this effort, with recent advances demonstrating that trade-offs previously considered fundamental—such as the incompatibility of multiple local steps with sample-level secure aggregation guarantees—can be overcome through more careful analysis and protocol design [arxiv_2407.19286, arxiv_2201.07730, arxiv_2210.00325].

### Evidence theme 3

Privacy preservation stands as one of the most fundamental and contested challenges in federated learning research. Although the federated paradigm was originally conceived as a privacy-enhancing framework—enabling collaborative model training without the direct exchange of raw data—subsequent scholarship has demonstrated that the mere act of sharing model updates, gradients, or intermediate representations can expose sensitive information about participating clients. This tension between the collaborative requirements of distributed learning and the imperative to protect individual data has generated a rich and rapidly evolving body of literature, spanning cryptographic aggregation protocols, differential privacy mechanisms, hybrid architectural designs, and optimization-aware privacy frameworks.

The foundational premise of federated learning as a privacy-preserving technology is articulated clearly in several survey and review contributions. Federated learning is described as a computing paradigm that enhances privacy by enabling multiple parties to collaboratively train a machine learning model without revealing personal data [arxiv_2501.05053]. Similarly, broader reviews characterize it as a distributed learning framework enhanced to preserve the privacy of individuals' data, one that lays the groundwork for collaborative research in areas where data is sensitive [arxiv_2311.10832]. These characterizations, however, are immediately complicated by the recognition that traditional federated learning platforms are unable to ensure privacy due to privacy leaks caused by the interchange of gradients [arxiv_2501.05053]. This acknowledgment motivates the entire subfield of privacy-preserving federated learning (PPFL) and underscores why simply distributing computation is insufficient as a privacy guarantee.

Among the most studied mechanisms for strengthening privacy in federated systems is secure aggregation, which aims to ensure that the central server or aggregator learns only the aggregate of client updates rather than any individual contribution. The survey by [arxiv_2203.17005] provides a comprehensive treatment of Privacy-Preserving Aggregation (PPAgg) protocols, positioning aggregation as the key protocol in PPFL and reviewing the landscape of cryptographic and statistical approaches that have been proposed to address privacy and security issues in federated systems. This work fills an important gap in the literature by systematically cataloguing the mechanisms through which aggregation can be made privacy-preserving, distinguishing between approaches based on secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption, secret sharing, and related cryptographic primitives. The survey's scope reflects the substantial research interest that PPAgg has attracted from both academia and industry as federated learning adoption has grown.

Building directly on these aggregation concerns, [arxiv_2501.05053] proposes TAPFed, a system designed to achieve privacy-preserving federated learning in the context of multiple decentralized aggregators with malicious actors. A critical contribution of TAPFed is its use of a threshold functional encryption scheme, which allows for a certain number of malicious aggregators while maintaining security. This is a meaningful advance over prior secure aggregation solutions, which the authors argue are vulnerable to recently demonstrated inference attacks such as the disaggregation attack. The disaggregation attack represents a class of threat that specifically targets the aggregation step, exploiting the structure of aggregated updates to reconstruct individual contributions. By designing around this threat model, TAPFed illustrates how the adversarial landscape for federated learning continues to evolve and how cryptographic solutions must be co-designed with an awareness of emerging attack vectors. Compared to the broader survey perspective of [arxiv_2203.17005], which maps the existing solution space, TAPFed exemplifies the ongoing need for novel cryptographic constructions that respond to newly identified vulnerabilities.

A complementary approach to cryptographic aggregation is differential privacy (DP), which introduces calibrated noise into model updates to provide formal privacy guarantees. The intersection of differential privacy and federated learning is explored in depth by [arxiv_2101.12240], which examines how DP mechanisms interact with the communication constraints that are endemic to federated systems. The authors identify a fundamental tension: the communication overhead problem in federated learning is typically addressed through model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation, each of which increases training variance. Differential privacy mechanisms, which themselves introduce noise and thus additional variance, compound this challenge. Furthermore, data heterogeneity—arising because devices sample their data from possibly different distributions—induces additional variance among devices during training, making the joint optimization of privacy, communication efficiency, and model accuracy a genuinely difficult multi-objective problem. This work thus situates privacy not as an isolated concern but as one of several interacting constraints that must be managed simultaneously in practical federated deployments.

The optimization perspective on federated learning, including its privacy dimensions, is further developed by [arxiv_2107.06917], which provides a field guide to federated optimization. This contribution emphasizes that federated optimization problems are distinguished by their emphasis on communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, compatibility with privacy and system requirements, and other constraints not primary in other settings. The explicit inclusion of privacy compatibility as a design criterion for optimization algorithms reflects a maturing understanding that privacy cannot be bolted on after the fact but must be integrated into the algorithmic design from the outset. The guide's focus on practical implementation and simulation methodology also highlights the gap between theoretical privacy guarantees and real-world performance, a concern that resonates with the empirical findings reported in [arxiv_2101.12240] regarding the practical costs of combining differential privacy with communication-constrained federated training.

Beyond cryptographic and noise-based approaches, architectural innovations have also been proposed as a means of enhancing privacy. SplitFed learning, introduced in [arxiv_2004.12088], combines federated learning with split learning, a paradigm in which the machine learning model architecture is divided between clients and the server. The authors argue that split learning provides better model privacy than standard federated learning precisely because the client retains a portion of the model, limiting the information exposed to the server. SplitFed is presented as eliminating the inherent drawbacks of both constituent approaches: it achieves the parallelism and speed advantages of federated learning while retaining the privacy benefits of split learning's architectural partitioning. The incorporation of differential privacy and PixelDP into the SplitFed framework further demonstrates the complementarity of architectural and statistical privacy mechanisms. Compared to the cryptographic aggregation focus of [arxiv_2501.05053] and [arxiv_2203.17005], SplitFed represents a structurally different approach to the same underlying problem, suggesting that the field has not converged on a single dominant privacy paradigm but continues to explore diverse solution strategies.

Taken together, these contributions reveal a field in which privacy is understood as a multi-dimensional challenge requiring coordinated responses at the levels of cryptographic protocol design, noise injection, architectural partitioning, and optimization algorithm development. The systematic review in [arxiv_2311.10832] reinforces this pluralism by surveying supervised and unsupervised machine learning approaches within the federated privacy-preserving context, underscoring that privacy concerns permeate the full spectrum of learning tasks. The convergence of these research threads suggests that future progress in privacy-preserving federated learning will likely require integrated frameworks that address cryptographic, statistical, architectural, and optimization dimensions simultaneously, rather than treating each in isolation.
