# Federated Learning (medium)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning — evidence cluster A

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across multiple parties without requiring the direct sharing of raw data. This approach has gained considerable traction in response to growing concerns over data privacy, security, and the increasingly stringent regulatory frameworks that govern data usage across organizations and jurisdictions. As [arxiv_2104.14362] observe, data and computing resources are now typically distributed across end-user devices, geographic regions, and organizations, and existing laws and regulations often prohibit the direct aggregation of such data for machine learning purposes. Federated learning addresses this challenge by allowing participants to collaboratively train models while keeping their data local, thereby obeying legal constraints and preserving data security and privacy.

At its core, federated learning departs from the traditional centralized learning paradigm by exchanging only locally trained models or computed gradients rather than the underlying data itself. As [arxiv_2301.01299] explain, this design choice provides a degree of privacy protection that centralized approaches cannot offer, since raw data never leaves the local devices or organizational boundaries. This architectural distinction has made federated learning an effective framework for privacy-preserving collaborative learning among diverse parties, and it has spurred a rapidly expanding body of research spanning methodology, systems design, and real-world applications.

Several comprehensive surveys have sought to map the landscape of federated learning research. [arxiv_2104.14362] propose a functional architecture for federated learning systems alongside a taxonomy of related techniques, covering distributed training, data communication, and system security, while also identifying key limitations and open research directions. In a complementary effort, [arxiv_2301.01299] provide a more recent and systematic survey that reviews advanced federated methods and applications from multiple perspectives, explicitly addressing the gap left by earlier surveys that tended to focus on specific aspects or lacked coverage of the latest developments. Together, these works illustrate how the field has matured from early conceptual formulations toward a rich ecosystem of methods addressing diverse practical challenges.

One of the most significant and well-documented challenges in federated learning is the heterogeneity of data distributions across participating clients. In standard centralized learning, training data are typically assumed to be independent and identically distributed (IID); however, in federated settings, local datasets are almost invariably non-IID, reflecting the distinct behaviors, preferences, and contexts of individual users or organizations. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a detailed analysis of how non-IID data negatively affects model performance in federated learning, demonstrating that this degradation applies to both parametric and non-parametric models and manifests in both horizontal and vertical federated learning configurations. Their survey reviews existing approaches to mitigating the non-IID problem and critically discusses the trade-offs associated with each strategy, offering a nuanced perspective on the current state of solutions.

Taken together, these surveys highlight that federated learning is not a monolithic solution but rather a broad framework encompassing a range of design choices and trade-offs. The transition from distributed machine learning to federated learning, as traced by [arxiv_2104.14362], reflects a fundamental shift in how the research community conceptualizes the relationship between data ownership, computational resources, and model training. Meanwhile, the persistent challenge of non-IID data, as foregrounded by [arxiv_2106.06843], and the ongoing proliferation of advanced methods documented by [arxiv_2301.01299], underscore that federated learning remains an active and evolving area of inquiry with substantial open problems yet to be resolved.

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning — evidence cluster B

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a compelling paradigm for distributed machine learning, enabling multiple clients to collaboratively train a shared model without transmitting raw data to a central server. As articulated in [arxiv_2208.01200], the fundamental motivation behind FL is to balance the competing demands of data privacy and joint model training, allowing multi-party collaborative computing without exposing original data. This architectural choice directly addresses the privacy risks inherent in traditional distributed machine learning, where private user data would otherwise traverse the network between clients and a central aggregator. However, while FL's decentralized structure provides an initial layer of privacy protection, it does not eliminate privacy vulnerabilities entirely, and a substantial body of research has consequently focused on strengthening privacy guarantees within the FL framework.

A central concern in this literature is the risk of privacy leakage during the information exchange that occurs at each communication round. In standard FL protocols, edge devices perform local stochastic gradient descent and upload their computed updates to the server for model aggregation. As [arxiv_2003.13761] observes, this exchange introduces a privacy challenge, particularly when the server cannot be fully trusted, since the uploaded gradients or model parameters may inadvertently reveal sensitive information about local training data. To address this, [arxiv_2003.13761] proposes a federated learning approach grounded in concentrated differential privacy (CDP), which aims to provide formal privacy guarantees without substantially degrading model utility or convergence. This represents an important contribution because earlier privacy-preserving mechanisms, while applicable to FL in principle, typically imposed high costs on algorithmic convergence and the quality of the learned model. By carefully calibrating the privacy-utility tradeoff, the CDP-based approach in [arxiv_2003.13761] demonstrates that meaningful privacy protection need not come at the expense of model performance.

An alternative line of inquiry moves away from differential privacy and cryptographic techniques such as homomorphic encryption, instead seeking privacy through the careful control of communication patterns among participants. [arxiv_2012.07183] develops a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol formulated using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), in which the structure of inter-participant communication is engineered to minimize privacy leakage. Rather than adding noise to model updates or encrypting them, this approach restricts which participants can observe which intermediate values during each aggregation round, providing privacy guarantees against an honest-but-curious adversary. The contrast with [arxiv_2003.13761] is instructive: while both works target privacy leakage in the aggregation process, they differ fundamentally in their mechanisms—one relying on statistical noise injection and the other on topological control of communication. [arxiv_2012.07183] further proposes an efficient algorithm for constructing the required communication patterns, suggesting that the approach is practically viable beyond its theoretical guarantees.

Cutting across both privacy-focused contributions is the broader challenge of communication efficiency, which [arxiv_2208.01200] identifies as one of the most pressing practical obstacles in FL deployment. The survey systematically examines communication challenges from three perspectives: efficiency, environment, and resource allocation, situating privacy-preserving mechanisms within a wider landscape of communication constraints. This framing is significant because privacy techniques such as those in [arxiv_2003.13761] and [arxiv_2012.07183] inevitably interact with communication costs—noise addition may require additional rounds for convergence, while controlled communication topologies alter the volume and routing of transmitted information. Together, these works underscore that privacy and communication efficiency in FL are deeply intertwined concerns that must be addressed in concert rather than in isolation.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning — evidence cluster A

The intersection of federated learning with privacy-preserving mechanisms has generated substantial scholarly attention, particularly as researchers seek to reconcile the competing demands of data utility and formal privacy guarantees. A central challenge in this space concerns how differential privacy (DP) and secure aggregation can be effectively combined within federated settings, and how architectural choices—such as centralized versus decentralized coordination—shape the resulting privacy-utility trade-offs.

Differential privacy has emerged as the predominant formal framework for quantifying and bounding privacy leakage in federated learning pipelines. [arxiv_2311.16008] situates this concern within the broader context of mobile and peer-to-peer networks, where the proliferation of distributed devices intensifies risks associated with membership inference attacks. Their experimental work demonstrates that even though federated learning inherently retains raw data at local nodes, this alone is insufficient to prevent privacy breaches, and that the careful selection of DP parameters is decisive in navigating the trade-off between model utility and privacy protection. This finding underscores a recurring theme in the literature: the mere adoption of federated learning does not confer robust privacy, and supplementary mechanisms are necessary.

Building on this foundation, [arxiv_2407.19286] addresses a specific and practically significant limitation in the joint deployment of differential privacy and secure aggregation. Secure aggregation protocols are designed to ensure that a central server learns only the aggregate of client updates rather than individual contributions, thereby strengthening privacy guarantees. However, the authors identify that existing approaches operating at fine-grained protection granularities—most notably sample-level protection—typically require parties to communicate after each local optimization step in order to fully realize the privacy benefits of secure aggregation. This communication overhead is non-trivial in large-scale federated deployments. Their contribution is a novel analytical framework that permits multiple local optimization steps between communication rounds while preserving the formal privacy guarantees associated with secure aggregation. The result is a meaningful improvement in model utility without sacrificing the theoretical privacy properties that motivate the use of secure aggregation in the first place. This work thus advances the practical feasibility of privacy-preserving federated learning by relaxing a previously binding constraint on training efficiency.

A complementary line of inquiry concerns the elimination of the central coordinator altogether. Both [arxiv_2311.16008] and [arxiv_2407.19286] operate within paradigms that retain some form of central aggregation, whereas [arxiv_2210.00325] addresses the fully decentralized case. The authors of [arxiv_2210.00325] frame decentralized federated learning over time-varying communication graphs as an open problem and propose the first consensus-based algorithm capable of achieving privacy-preserving global model aggregation in a peer-to-peer environment without a trusted coordinator. Their approach integrates Shamir's secret sharing scheme to protect individual model updates during consensus rounds, and employs the Metropolis-Hastings method to dynamically update the weighted adjacency matrix as the communication topology changes between rounds. The formal correctness and privacy properties of the algorithm are established analytically, and computational efficiency is evaluated empirically. This work is particularly relevant to high-mobility environments—such as vehicular networks or ad hoc wireless systems—where a stable central server cannot be assumed.

Taken together, these three contributions illuminate distinct but interrelated dimensions of privacy-preserving federated learning. [arxiv_2311.16008] establishes the empirical stakes of the privacy-utility trade-off under DP; [arxiv_2407.19286] refines the theoretical and practical conditions under which secure aggregation can be exploited with multiple local steps; and [arxiv_2210.00325] extends the problem space to fully decentralized topologies with dynamic connectivity. Collectively, they reflect a field progressively moving toward more flexible, scalable, and formally grounded privacy guarantees across diverse federated learning architectures.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning — evidence cluster B

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed participants without requiring the direct exchange of raw data. At its core, the framework allows multiple parties holding sensitive datasets to collaboratively build a shared model by communicating only model updates, such as gradients or weights, rather than the underlying data itself [arxiv_2402.10145]. This fundamental property makes federated learning particularly attractive in domains where data privacy is paramount, including healthcare, finance, and mobile computing. Nevertheless, the approach introduces a range of technical challenges that have motivated a growing body of research addressing privacy, communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, and system robustness.

A central concern in federated learning is the protection of participant data during the aggregation process. Even when raw data is never transmitted, model parameters can leak sensitive information to a potentially malicious aggregation server [arxiv_2201.07730]. Two broad families of techniques have been explored to counter this threat: perturbation-based methods, most notably differential privacy, and cryptographic approaches. Differential privacy introduces calibrated noise into model updates to bound the information that can be inferred about any individual data point, while cryptographic techniques such as secure multi-party computation (MPC) and secret sharing provide stronger, information-theoretic guarantees without degrading model accuracy through noise injection. The SCOTCH framework exemplifies the cryptographic direction, proposing a decentralized m-party secure computation protocol that deploys MPC primitives, including secret sharing, to protect against curious aggregators or colluding data owners while maintaining minimal communication overhead compared to existing privacy-preserving alternatives [arxiv_2201.07730]. In contrast, chaotic maps-based encryption has been proposed as an alternative privacy layer that can be compared directly against differential privacy; experimental results suggest that federated learning with either mechanism can improve average model performance metrics even under non-IID data distributions, though the two approaches differ in their computational and privacy trade-off profiles [arxiv_2402.10145].

Beyond privacy, data heterogeneity represents one of the most practically significant obstacles to effective federated learning. In real-world deployments, the data held by different participants is rarely independently and identically distributed (IID); instead, local datasets often reflect the specific demographics, behaviors, or measurement conditions of each participant. This non-IID character can cause local model updates to diverge substantially, degrading the quality of the globally aggregated model. Research has demonstrated that federated learning frameworks can be adapted to handle non-IID settings, for instance through modified aggregation strategies or supplementary regularization, and that performance improvements over purely local training remain achievable even under such conditions [arxiv_2402.10145].

A further structural limitation of conventional federated learning is its reliance on a centralized aggregation server, which creates a single point of failure and concentrates trust in one entity. Decentralized federated learning addresses this vulnerability by replacing the star topology with peer-to-peer communication graphs, distributing the aggregation responsibility across participants. This architectural shift introduces new challenges related to model consistency, convergence guarantees, and communication efficiency, but also opens opportunities for greater resilience and scalability [arxiv_2306.02570]. A systematic analysis of decentralized federated learning highlights that the relevant challenges span the model, data, and communication dimensions, and that insights from classical decentralized optimization must be carefully adapted to account for the statistical heterogeneity and privacy requirements specific to federated settings [arxiv_2306.02570]. Taken together, these threads of research illustrate that federated learning is a multifaceted problem space in which advances in privacy preservation, data heterogeneity handling, and decentralized coordination are deeply interrelated.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for enabling collaborative model training across distributed participants without requiring the direct exchange of raw data. At its core, the approach is motivated by privacy protection, allowing multiple parties to jointly learn a shared model while keeping their local datasets decentralized [arxiv_2107.06917]. Despite this foundational privacy orientation, a growing body of research has demonstrated that the mere act of distributing computation does not, by itself, guarantee robust privacy guarantees. The exchange of model gradients during training has been identified as a significant vulnerability, as adversaries can exploit these intermediate updates to reconstruct sensitive information about participants' local data [arxiv_2501.05053]. This tension between the collaborative utility of federated learning and the privacy risks inherent in gradient sharing has motivated substantial work on strengthening the privacy properties of federated systems.

One prominent line of inquiry concerns the integration of secure aggregation mechanisms into federated learning pipelines. Secure aggregation protocols are designed to ensure that the central server or aggregator learns only the aggregate model update rather than any individual participant's contribution. However, as TAPFed illustrates, even these mechanisms are not universally robust: existing secure aggregation solutions have been shown to be susceptible to recently demonstrated inference attacks, including disaggregation attacks that can isolate individual updates from an ostensibly aggregated result [arxiv_2501.05053]. To address this, TAPFed proposes a threshold functional encryption scheme that distributes the aggregation role across multiple decentralized aggregators, tolerating a bounded number of malicious actors while preserving security and correctness. This represents a meaningful advance over prior single-aggregator designs, which implicitly assume a trusted central party and thus introduce a single point of failure for privacy.

A complementary architectural response to privacy and resource constraints in federated learning comes from the integration of split learning principles. Split learning divides a model's architecture between clients and a server, so that clients process only the initial layers and transmit intermediate representations rather than raw data or full gradients. This structural separation provides stronger model privacy than conventional federated learning, since the server never observes the complete model held by any client [arxiv_2004.12088]. However, split learning's relay-based sequential training across clients introduces significant latency, making it slower than standard federated learning in practice. The SplitFed framework attempts to reconcile these trade-offs by combining federated and split learning, achieving the privacy benefits of architectural splitting while recovering the parallelism that makes federated learning computationally efficient. SplitFed further incorporates differential privacy and PixelDP to bolster both data privacy and model robustness, suggesting that hybrid architectural approaches can be layered with cryptographic and statistical privacy mechanisms [arxiv_2004.12088].

Across these contributions, a common theme is that no single mechanism is sufficient to address the full spectrum of privacy threats in federated learning. The field guide to federated optimization situates these challenges within a broader framework, emphasizing that federated optimization must simultaneously contend with communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, and compatibility with privacy and system requirements—constraints that distinguish federated settings from conventional distributed machine learning [arxiv_2107.06917]. Taken together, the literature suggests that achieving meaningful privacy in federated learning requires a multi-layered approach: architectural choices such as model splitting, cryptographic tools such as threshold functional encryption, and statistical guarantees such as differential privacy must be carefully combined and evaluated in realistic simulation conditions to ensure that theoretical protections translate into practical security.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for enabling collaborative model training across distributed participants without requiring the centralized aggregation of raw data, making it particularly well-suited to domains where data sensitivity is paramount [arxiv_2311.10832]. At its core, the framework allows individual entities—whether hospitals, mobile devices, or financial institutions—to train local models on their own data, with only model updates rather than underlying records being shared with a coordinating server. This architectural choice directly addresses longstanding concerns about data sovereignty and regulatory compliance, and has consequently attracted substantial research interest from both academic and industrial communities [arxiv_2203.17005].

Despite its conceptual elegance, federated learning introduces a constellation of interrelated technical challenges that have driven a rich body of follow-on research. Among the most extensively studied is the tension between communication efficiency and model quality. Because federated systems may involve thousands of geographically dispersed participants with heterogeneous network conditions, the volume of gradient or parameter updates transmitted during training can become prohibitive. Three principal strategies have been proposed to mitigate this overhead: model compression, which reduces the bit-width or dimensionality of transmitted updates; partial device participation, which restricts each communication round to a subset of available clients; and periodic aggregation, which allows local devices to perform multiple gradient steps before synchronizing with the server [arxiv_2101.12240]. While each of these techniques reduces raw communication cost, they do so at the expense of increased training variance, thereby creating a fundamental trade-off that system designers must carefully navigate.

Compounding the communication challenge is the problem of data heterogeneity. Unlike conventional distributed learning, where data are assumed to be independently and identically distributed across nodes, federated participants typically draw their samples from markedly different local distributions. This statistical heterogeneity induces additional variance among device updates during training, which can destabilize convergence and degrade the quality of the resulting global model [arxiv_2101.12240]. Variance-reduction algorithms have been proposed to counteract these effects, yet they frequently demand supplementary control information to be exchanged between the server and clients, thereby partially offsetting the communication savings achieved by other techniques. The interplay among communication constraints, statistical heterogeneity, and variance reduction thus constitutes one of the central open problems in federated optimization.

Privacy preservation adds yet another layer of complexity. Even when raw data never leave local devices, model updates themselves can leak sensitive information through gradient inversion or membership inference attacks, motivating the integration of formal privacy guarantees into the federated pipeline [arxiv_2203.17005]. Privacy-preserving aggregation protocols—including secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption, and differential privacy mechanisms—have been developed to ensure that the server learns nothing beyond the aggregate model update. However, differential privacy in particular introduces a noise-accuracy trade-off: the stronger the privacy guarantee, the greater the perturbation applied to updates, and the more pronounced the degradation in model utility [arxiv_2101.12240]. Surveys of privacy-preserving aggregation highlight that no single protocol dominates across all deployment scenarios; rather, the appropriate choice depends on the threat model, the number of participants, and the acceptable level of accuracy loss [arxiv_2203.17005].

Systematic reviews of the broader federated learning literature further underscore that the field spans a wide range of machine learning paradigms, including both supervised and unsupervised approaches, and that real-world applicability is constrained not only by the technical challenges described above but also by organizational and regulatory factors [arxiv_2311.10832]. Collectively, these works establish that federated learning is not a monolithic solution but rather a flexible framework whose practical deployment requires careful co-design of communication protocols, optimization algorithms, and privacy mechanisms tailored to the specific requirements of each application context.
