# Federated Learning (medium)

Federated learning (FL) has emerged over the past several years as a compelling paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed, privacy-sensitive data sources without requiring raw data to be centralized. The foundational motivation is straightforward: as data and computing resources become increasingly distributed across end-user devices, regional data centers, and organizational silos, legal frameworks and regulatory constraints—ranging from the European General Data Protection Regulation to sector-specific data governance rules—prohibit the direct aggregation of such data for centralized model training [arxiv_2104.14362]. Federated learning addresses this tension by allowing participants to collaboratively train a shared model while retaining their local data, transmitting only model updates or gradients rather than raw samples [arxiv_2301.01299]. This section reviews the landscape of flat federated learning, examining its architectural foundations, the persistent challenges of data heterogeneity and communication overhead, the spectrum of privacy-preserving mechanisms that have been proposed, and the emerging interest in decentralized topologies that eliminate reliance on a central coordinating server.

At its core, federated learning can be understood as a distributed optimization problem in which a global model is iteratively refined through rounds of local computation and aggregated updates. The canonical procedure involves a central server distributing a global model to a subset of participating clients, each of which performs several steps of stochastic gradient descent on its local dataset before returning updated parameters to the server for aggregation [arxiv_2107.06917]. This formulation, while conceptually elegant, introduces a set of interrelated challenges that distinguish federated optimization from conventional distributed machine learning. Chief among these are communication efficiency, statistical heterogeneity across clients, system heterogeneity in terms of computational and network resources, and the need to provide formal privacy guarantees [arxiv_2104.14362; arxiv_2107.06917]. Surveys of the field have consistently identified these four dimensions as the primary axes along which federated learning research is organized [arxiv_2301.01299; arxiv_2106.06843].

The problem of statistical heterogeneity, often referred to as the non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) data problem, is particularly consequential for model quality. When local datasets are drawn from heterogeneous distributions—as is almost universally the case in real-world deployments—the local model updates computed by individual clients may diverge substantially from one another and from the direction that would minimize the global objective [arxiv_2106.06843]. This divergence, sometimes called client drift, degrades convergence rates and can result in a global model that performs poorly relative to one trained on centralized data. The survey by [arxiv_2106.06843] provides a detailed taxonomy of non-IID scenarios, distinguishing between feature distribution skew, label distribution skew, quantity skew, and temporal non-stationarity, and reviews approaches that address each variant. These include data-sharing strategies, in which a small globally accessible dataset is used to regularize local training; personalization techniques that allow clients to maintain locally adapted model components; and algorithmic modifications such as FedProx, which introduces a proximal term to constrain the deviation of local updates from the global model. The work of [arxiv_2402.10145] further illustrates the practical severity of the non-IID problem by demonstrating that federated deep learning performance degrades measurably under non-IID conditions, while also proposing chaotic-map-based encryption as an alternative privacy mechanism that can be evaluated alongside differential privacy in such settings.

Communication overhead constitutes a second major bottleneck in federated learning systems. Because model updates must be transmitted between clients and the server at every aggregation round, and because modern deep learning models may contain hundreds of millions of parameters, the cumulative communication cost can be prohibitive, particularly for clients operating over bandwidth-constrained wireless links [arxiv_2208.01200]. The survey by [arxiv_2208.01200] systematically examines communication challenges from three perspectives: communication efficiency, which encompasses gradient compression, quantization, and sparsification techniques; communication environment, which addresses the heterogeneity of network conditions across clients; and communication resource allocation, which considers scheduling and bandwidth assignment strategies. The interaction between communication reduction and privacy is non-trivial: techniques such as gradient compression reduce the volume of information transmitted but may also affect the sensitivity of the transmitted updates, thereby influencing the privacy-utility trade-off [arxiv_2101.12240]. The work of [arxiv_2101.12240] specifically examines this intersection, analyzing how communication-reduction techniques including model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation interact with differential privacy mechanisms, and demonstrating that data heterogeneity introduces additional variance that complicates the joint optimization of communication cost and privacy loss.

Privacy preservation is arguably the most extensively studied dimension of federated learning, and a rich body of work has developed around two principal cryptographic and statistical paradigms: differential privacy (DP) and secure aggregation. Differential privacy, which provides a formal probabilistic bound on the information that can be inferred about any individual training example from the released model, has been widely adopted as the standard privacy guarantee in federated learning [arxiv_2203.17005]. The survey by [arxiv_2203.17005] provides a comprehensive review of privacy-preserving aggregation protocols in federated learning, covering both DP-based approaches and cryptographic techniques such as homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation. The authors observe that while DP offers strong and composable privacy guarantees, it typically requires the addition of calibrated noise to model updates, which degrades model utility, and that the magnitude of this degradation depends critically on the sensitivity of the updates and the desired privacy budget. The work of [arxiv_2003.13761] addresses this utility degradation by developing a federated learning approach based on concentrated differential privacy, which provides tighter privacy accounting than standard DP and thereby allows for less noise addition at a given privacy level, resulting in improved model convergence without sacrificing formal privacy guarantees.

Secure aggregation, which uses cryptographic protocols to ensure that the server learns only the aggregate of client updates rather than individual contributions, is frequently combined with differential privacy to provide complementary protections [arxiv_2407.19286]. The combination is motivated by the observation that DP alone, when applied at the level of individual updates, requires each client to add noise proportional to the sensitivity of its update, whereas secure aggregation allows noise to be added to the aggregate, potentially reducing the total noise required for a given privacy guarantee. However, the interaction between secure aggregation and multiple local optimization steps—a common practice in federated learning to reduce communication rounds—has been shown to be subtle. The work of [arxiv_2407.19286] demonstrates that a careful re-analysis of the privacy accounting allows clients to perform multiple local steps while still benefiting from secure aggregation, enabling higher-utility models without compromising formal privacy guarantees. This result is significant because it relaxes a previously perceived tension between communication efficiency (achieved through multiple local steps) and the privacy benefits of secure aggregation.

The SCOTCH framework [arxiv_2201.07730] represents a complementary approach to secure aggregation, proposing a decentralized multi-party computation framework based on secret sharing that provides strict privacy guarantees against curious aggregators or colluding data owners while maintaining minimal communication overhead. The authors argue that purely DP-based approaches impose unacceptable utility costs in many practical settings, and that cryptographic secure aggregation can achieve strong privacy with lower impact on model quality. SCOTCH is positioned as an improvement over prior secure aggregation schemes in terms of communication efficiency, making it more practical for deployment in bandwidth-constrained environments. Similarly, TAPFed [arxiv_2501.05053] addresses a specific vulnerability in existing secure aggregation schemes—the disaggregation attack, in which a malicious aggregator attempts to recover individual client updates from the aggregate—by proposing a threshold functional encryption scheme that tolerates a bounded number of malicious aggregators. TAPFed is notable for operating in a setting with multiple decentralized aggregators, anticipating the shift toward more distributed aggregation architectures that is discussed further below.

The combination of differential privacy with decentralized aggregation has also received attention as a means of strengthening privacy guarantees beyond what is achievable with a single trusted server. The work of [arxiv_2311.16008] deploys an experimental environment for federated learning with differential privacy in a peer-to-peer setting, demonstrating that the choice of DP parameters and techniques is central to the privacy-utility trade-off and that decentralized aggregation can provide stronger privacy than server-based aggregation under certain threat models. The privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol of [arxiv_2012.07183] takes a different approach, using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) to formulate the distributed aggregation problem and controlling the communication graph among participants to minimize privacy leakage, rather than relying on DP or homomorphic encryption. The authors establish privacy guarantees against an honest-but-curious adversary and propose an efficient algorithm for constructing the communication pattern, drawing inspiration from combinatorial design theory. This approach is particularly interesting because it achieves privacy through structural constraints on information flow rather than through noise addition or cryptographic overhead, potentially offering a more favorable utility-privacy trade-off in settings where the communication graph can be engineered.

A growing body of research has examined the extension of federated learning to fully decentralized topologies, in which there is no central server and clients communicate directly with one another in a peer-to-peer fashion. This architectural shift is motivated by concerns about single-point failure, the concentration of power in a central aggregator, and the communication bottleneck that arises when all clients must communicate with a single server [arxiv_2306.02570]. The survey by [arxiv_2306.02570] systematically investigates the challenges and opportunities that arise when decentralized optimization techniques are applied to federated learning, organizing the discussion around model, data, and communication dimensions. The authors identify several key challenges unique to the decentralized setting, including the need to achieve consensus among clients with heterogeneous data distributions, the sensitivity of convergence to the topology of the communication graph, and the difficulty of providing privacy guarantees in the absence of a trusted aggregator. The privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithm of [arxiv_2210.00325] addresses the specific challenge of decentralized federated learning over time-varying communication graphs, which arise naturally in mobile and ad-hoc network settings. The algorithm integrates the Metropolis-Hastings method for updating the weighted adjacency matrix based on the current communication topology with Shamir's secret sharing scheme to achieve privacy in the consensus process, and establishes both correctness and privacy properties against honest-but-curious adversaries.

Beyond the purely algorithmic dimensions, federated learning has been explored in conjunction with alternative model architectures and training paradigms. The SplitFed framework [arxiv_2004.12088] combines federated learning with split learning, in which the model is partitioned between clients and a server, with each client processing only the initial layers of the network before transmitting intermediate activations to the server. The authors argue that this combination inherits the privacy benefits of split learning—since raw data never leaves the client and the server never sees the full model—while also achieving the parallelism and communication efficiency of federated learning. The integration of differential privacy and PixelDP into the SplitFed architecture further enhances both data privacy and model robustness against adversarial inputs. This work illustrates the broader trend toward hybrid architectures that combine multiple privacy-enhancing technologies to address complementary threat models.

The review by [arxiv_2311.10832] provides a systematic survey of machine learning models used in federated learning, covering supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning approaches and evaluating their performance and limitations in the federated setting. The authors apply the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews, providing a structured synthesis of the literature that complements the more technically focused surveys discussed above. Their analysis highlights the diversity of application domains in which federated learning has been deployed, including healthcare, finance, and natural language processing, and identifies model complexity, communication overhead, and data heterogeneity as the primary factors limiting performance across these domains.

Taken together, the literature on flat federated learning reveals a field that has matured considerably from its initial formulation but continues to grapple with fundamental tensions between privacy, utility, communication efficiency, and robustness. The transition from centralized to decentralized aggregation architectures represents one of the most significant ongoing developments, driven by both practical concerns about single-point failure and theoretical interest in stronger privacy guarantees [arxiv_2306.02570; arxiv_2210.00325; arxiv_2012.07183]. The interaction between differential privacy and secure aggregation remains an active area of investigation, with recent work demonstrating that careful analysis can relax previously perceived constraints and enable higher-utility models [arxiv_2407.19286]. The non-IID data problem continues to motivate algorithmic innovation, with approaches ranging from proximal regularization to data sharing to personalization [arxiv_2106.06843; arxiv_2402.10145]. And the communication bottleneck, while partially addressed by gradient compression and periodic aggregation, remains a central concern, particularly as the interaction between communication reduction and privacy is better understood [arxiv_2208.01200; arxiv_2101.12240]. The field's trajectory suggests continued convergence toward systems that are simultaneously more private, more communication-efficient, and more robust to the statistical and system heterogeneity that characterizes real-world deployments [arxiv_2301.01299; arxiv_2104.14362].

The interplay between differential privacy and secure aggregation has attracted sustained theoretical and empirical attention, with researchers increasingly recognizing that the two mechanisms are not merely complementary but can be jointly designed to achieve guarantees that neither provides alone. The systematic review by [arxiv_2405.08299] categorizes the landscape of differentially private federated learning algorithms, distinguishing among central, local, and shuffled DP models and evaluating their respective utility-privacy trade-offs. The authors observe that central DP, in which noise is added by the server after aggregation, generally achieves better utility than local DP, in which each client perturbs its own update before transmission, but at the cost of requiring trust in the aggregating server. This tension motivates hybrid approaches that use secure aggregation to simulate the central DP model without requiring a trusted server, a direction explored in depth by [arxiv_2407.19286], which demonstrates that combining secure aggregation with sample-level DP protection is feasible even with multiple local gradient steps, provided that the clipping and noise calibration are carefully adapted to the multi-step setting. The comprehensive survey by [arxiv_2404.18814] further contextualizes these developments, tracing the evolution of DP-FL from early theoretical formulations to practically deployable systems and identifying remaining gaps in the understanding of composition, amplification by subsampling, and the interaction between DP and non-IID data distributions. Together, these works underscore that the integration of DP and secure aggregation is not a solved problem but an active frontier requiring continued algorithmic innovation and rigorous theoretical analysis.

The challenge of balancing privacy guarantees against model utility is particularly acute when differential privacy is applied in the local optimization step rather than at the level of gradient aggregation. The work of [arxiv_2503.21154] proposes a utility-enhanced approach to federated learning with differential privacy that modifies the local optimization objective to reduce the sensitivity of gradient updates, thereby allowing the privacy budget to be spent more efficiently. The authors demonstrate that their approach achieves superior utility compared to standard DP-SGD baselines across a range of benchmark datasets, particularly in the non-IID setting where gradient variance is already elevated. Similarly, [arxiv_2304.05127] investigates the trade-off between privacy and performance for private federated learning algorithms, providing both theoretical bounds and empirical evaluations that quantify the utility cost of different DP mechanisms as a function of the privacy budget, the number of clients, and the degree of data heterogeneity. The empirical benchmarking study of [arxiv_2402.02230] complements these theoretical contributions by systematically varying the number of participating clients and the DP noise level, finding that the performance degradation attributable to DP is substantially larger in settings with few clients or highly heterogeneous data, which has direct implications for the design of practical deployments. The momentum gradient descent approach of [arxiv_2209.14086] addresses the local DP setting specifically, proposing a mechanism that incorporates momentum into the noisy gradient updates to accelerate convergence and partially offset the utility loss introduced by local perturbation.

Secure aggregation protocols represent a cryptographic complement to differential privacy, ensuring that the server learns only the aggregate of client updates rather than individual contributions. The e-SeaFL protocol of [arxiv_2304.03841] advances the state of the art by reducing the aggregation phase to a single communication round while supporting verifiability, meaning that clients can detect a malicious server that attempts to deviate from the prescribed aggregation function. This verifiability property addresses a limitation of many earlier secure aggregation schemes, which assumed a semi-honest server and provided no mechanism for clients to audit the aggregation outcome. The SCOTCH framework of [arxiv_2201.07730] takes a complementary approach, combining secret sharing with lightweight cryptographic primitives to reduce the communication and computational overhead of secure aggregation, and demonstrates that the resulting overhead is practical even for large neural network models. The TAPFed protocol of [arxiv_2501.05053] introduces threshold secure aggregation, in which a minimum quorum of clients must participate for the aggregation to succeed, providing robustness against client dropout while maintaining privacy guarantees even when a subset of clients collude with the server. The communication-computation efficient secure aggregation scheme of [arxiv_2012.05433] further reduces overhead by exploiting the algebraic structure of the aggregation operation, achieving significant reductions in both uplink communication and server-side computation relative to prior schemes. Collectively, these protocols illustrate a trajectory toward secure aggregation mechanisms that are simultaneously more efficient, more robust to dropout and collusion, and more amenable to integration with DP mechanisms.

The communication efficiency of federated learning has been studied extensively, with researchers proposing a variety of compression, quantization, and sparsification techniques to reduce the volume of data exchanged between clients and the server. The survey of [arxiv_2208.01200] provides a comprehensive taxonomy of communication reduction techniques, distinguishing among gradient compression, model pruning, knowledge distillation, and asynchronous aggregation approaches, and evaluating their compatibility with privacy mechanisms. The two-bit aggregation scheme of [arxiv_2110.03017] demonstrates that extreme quantization of gradient updates to two bits per parameter can be combined with differential privacy noise addition without significant additional utility loss, because the quantization error and the DP noise interact in a way that partially cancels at the aggregate level. The stochastic coded federated learning framework of [arxiv_2201.10092] addresses the straggler problem—in which slow or unreliable clients delay the completion of each communication round—by introducing coded redundancy into the local computation, allowing the server to recover the aggregate from a subset of client responses. The authors establish convergence guarantees for their scheme and demonstrate that it achieves a favorable trade-off among convergence speed, communication cost, and robustness to stragglers. The CorBin-FL mechanism of [arxiv_2409.13133] introduces correlated binary stochastic quantization as a means of simultaneously achieving differential privacy and communication efficiency, exploiting common randomness shared among clients to reduce the variance of the quantized updates and thereby improve the privacy-utility-communication trade-off relative to schemes that treat quantization and privacy noise as independent sources of perturbation.

The non-IID data problem, which arises because the local datasets of different clients are drawn from heterogeneous distributions, has been identified as one of the most significant obstacles to achieving high model quality in federated learning [arxiv_2106.06843]. The FedPDC approach of [arxiv_2302.12503] addresses this challenge by leveraging a small public dataset to correct the bias introduced by heterogeneous local distributions during aggregation, demonstrating that even a modest amount of publicly available data can substantially improve convergence and final model accuracy in non-IID settings. The causal federated learning framework of [arxiv_2104.06557] takes a fundamentally different perspective, arguing that the non-IID problem can be partially mitigated by learning causal rather than purely correlational representations, which are more likely to generalize across clients with different data distributions. The chaotic maps-based approach of [arxiv_2402.10145] combines a novel privacy mechanism based on chaotic encryption with a data augmentation strategy for addressing non-IID distributions, providing an empirical comparison with differential privacy that suggests chaotic encryption can achieve comparable privacy protection with lower utility degradation in certain settings, though the authors acknowledge that formal privacy guarantees for chaotic encryption remain less well-established than those for DP. The field guide to federated optimization of [arxiv_2107.06917] provides a unifying theoretical framework for analyzing convergence in the presence of data heterogeneity, communication constraints, and partial client participation, establishing conditions under which standard optimization algorithms converge and identifying the modifications required to handle the federated setting's unique constraints.

Beyond these core algorithmic and theoretical contributions, the federated learning literature has increasingly engaged with questions of system design, evaluation methodology, and application-specific adaptation. The PrivacyFL simulator of [arxiv_2002.08423] provides a configurable software environment for evaluating privacy-preserving federated learning protocols under realistic network conditions, supporting a range of DP mechanisms, secure aggregation schemes, and adversary models. The Sherpa.ai FL framework described in [arxiv_2007.00914] similarly provides a practical platform for combining federated learning with differential privacy, and includes methodological guidelines for practitioners seeking to deploy privacy-preserving FL systems in real-world settings. The comparative assessment of [arxiv_2202.01529] evaluates federated and centralized machine learning across a range of tasks and datasets, finding that the performance gap between the two paradigms is highly sensitive to the degree of data heterogeneity and the number of communication rounds, and that the gap can be substantially reduced by appropriate choice of aggregation algorithm and local training hyperparameters. The study of performance limitations in [arxiv_2501.03477] provides a systematic analysis of the factors that constrain model quality in federated learning, including hardware heterogeneity, network latency, and the interaction between local training epochs and global convergence, offering practical guidance for system designers. The emerging intersection of federated learning with quantum computing, explored in [arxiv_2602.03052], points toward longer-term directions in which hybrid classical-quantum models may offer computational advantages in the federated setting, though the authors acknowledge that non-IID data remains a challenge even for quantum-enhanced architectures. The security and privacy survey of [arxiv_2508.13730] provides a broad synthesis of attack and defense mechanisms in federated learning, cataloguing threats ranging from gradient inversion and membership inference to Byzantine poisoning and backdoor attacks, and evaluating the effectiveness of defenses including DP, secure aggregation, anomaly detection, and robust aggregation rules. This comprehensive threat modeling perspective is essential for understanding the limits of privacy guarantees provided by any single mechanism and motivates the layered defense strategies that characterize the most robust contemporary federated learning systems.
