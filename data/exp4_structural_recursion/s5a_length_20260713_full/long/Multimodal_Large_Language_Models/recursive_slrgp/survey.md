# Multimodal Large Language Models (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs — evidence cluster A

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing, computer vision, and cross-modal representation learning into unified architectures capable of reasoning across heterogeneous data types. The field has evolved rapidly from narrowly scoped specialist systems toward increasingly general-purpose assistants, a trajectory that several comprehensive surveys have sought to document and analyze [arxiv_2309.10020, arxiv_2402.12451]. Understanding this evolution requires situating MLLMs within the broader history of vision-language pre-training, examining the architectural and training innovations that have enabled multimodal integration, and acknowledging the expanding scope of modalities that contemporary systems now address.

The intellectual foundations of MLLMs lie in vision-language pre-training (VLP), which established the core methodological principles for aligning visual and textual representations. Early VLP research concentrated on a well-defined set of image-text tasks, including image captioning, visual question answering, image-text retrieval, and visual grounding, before extending to core computer vision tasks such as open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation, and eventually to video-text tasks involving temporal reasoning [arxiv_2210.09263]. This tripartite taxonomy reflects the progressive broadening of scope that characterizes the field: researchers first demonstrated that pre-training on large-scale image-text pairs could yield transferable representations for downstream tasks, then showed that the same paradigm could be adapted to dense prediction problems in computer vision, and finally extended it to the temporal domain. The challenges identified in this earlier literature, including the difficulty of grounding language in visual scenes and the computational cost of pre-training at scale, directly motivated the architectural choices that would later define MLLMs [arxiv_2210.09263].

The transition from specialist VLP models to general-purpose multimodal assistants is perhaps the defining narrative of recent progress in the field. Specialist models were pre-trained for specific purposes, with separate systems optimized for visual understanding and for text-to-image generation, and their capabilities did not transfer easily across task boundaries [arxiv_2309.10020]. The emergence of large language models as powerful general-purpose reasoners created an opportunity to use language as a unifying interface, enabling the construction of systems that could handle diverse visual and linguistic inputs within a single framework. This shift is characterized by two complementary research directions: the development of unified vision models inspired by LLMs, and the end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs that integrate vision encoders with language model backbones [arxiv_2309.10020]. A third direction, chaining multimodal tools with LLMs, represents a more modular approach in which pre-existing specialist models are orchestrated by a language model rather than jointly trained, offering flexibility at the cost of tight integration [arxiv_2309.10020].

Contemporary MLLMs are distinguished by their ability to seamlessly integrate visual and textual modalities while providing dialogue-based interfaces and instruction-following capabilities, features that mark a qualitative departure from earlier VLP systems [arxiv_2402.12451]. Surveys of these models have examined their architectural choices in detail, focusing on how visual encoders are connected to language model backbones, the multimodal alignment strategies used to bridge the representational gap between modalities, and the training techniques, including instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback, that enable models to follow complex, open-ended instructions [arxiv_2402.12451]. The range of tasks addressed by these systems is correspondingly broad, spanning visual grounding, image generation and editing, visual understanding, and a growing array of domain-specific applications in areas such as medicine, science, and document analysis [arxiv_2402.12451]. This breadth distinguishes MLLMs from their VLP predecessors and underscores the importance of comprehensive evaluation frameworks capable of measuring performance across heterogeneous task types.

A further dimension of recent progress concerns the expansion of multimodality beyond the vision-language dyad. While the dominant paradigm has centered on integrating text and images, there is increasing recognition that real-world intelligence requires the processing of diverse data forms including audio, video, sensor streams, and other modalities [arxiv_2410.05608]. This broader conception of multimodality introduces new technical challenges related to the alignment and fusion of modalities with very different statistical properties and temporal structures. Instruction tuning strategies, which adapt pre-trained multimodal models to specific tasks through supervised fine-tuning on curated datasets of instruction-response pairs, have emerged as a particularly important tool for optimizing performance across this expanded modality space [arxiv_2410.05608]. The availability of large-scale multimodal datasets and standardized benchmarks has been essential to progress in this area, enabling systematic comparison of models and identification of capability gaps [arxiv_2410.05608, arxiv_2402.12451].

Taken together, the literature reflects a field in rapid transition, moving from the careful construction of specialist pre-trained models toward the development of general-purpose systems capable of perceiving, reasoning about, and generating content across multiple modalities. The architectural innovations that have enabled this transition, including scalable vision encoders, cross-modal alignment modules, and instruction-tuned language model backbones, build directly on the foundations laid by earlier VLP research [arxiv_2210.09263, arxiv_2309.10020]. At the same time, the expansion of scope to include audio, video, and sensor data signals that the field is moving toward an even more ambitious conception of multimodal intelligence, one in which the boundaries between modalities are increasingly fluid and the demand for robust, generalizable representations continues to grow [arxiv_2410.05608]. The following sections examine the specific architectural and training choices that have shaped this trajectory in greater detail.

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs — evidence cluster B

The rapid advancement of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has generated a rich body of research addressing their architecture, evaluation, interpretability, and domain-specific adaptation. This subsection surveys key contributions across these dimensions, highlighting both the progress achieved and the persistent challenges that motivate ongoing inquiry.

A foundational concern in the development of vision-language transformers is interpretability. As transformer-based architectures have come to dominate not only natural language processing but also multimodal systems, the opacity of their internal mechanisms has become an increasingly pressing issue. Addressing this gap, VL-InterpreT was proposed as an interactive visualization tool specifically designed to illuminate the inner workings of multimodal transformers [arxiv_2203.17247]. The tool provides novel visualizations of attention heads and hidden representations across all layers, tracking statistics for both vision and language components and rendering cross-modal as well as intra-modal attention patterns accessible to researchers. Crucially, VL-InterpreT is described as task-agnostic and integrated, meaning it can be applied across a range of vision-language tasks without modification [arxiv_2203.17247]. The authors argue that unraveling these black-box models is not merely an academic exercise but a prerequisite for building more capable and trustworthy systems. This perspective underscores a broader consensus in the field: as MLLMs are deployed in increasingly consequential settings, the demand for interpretable and auditable models grows correspondingly urgent.

Complementing interpretability research, a parallel strand of work has focused on the systematic evaluation of MLLMs. The LVLM-eHub benchmark represents one of the most comprehensive efforts in this direction, assembling 13 representative large vision-language models—including InstructBLIP and LLaVA—and subjecting them to rigorous quantitative and qualitative assessment [ss_478371]. The benchmark evaluates five categories of multimodal capabilities, encompassing visual question answering and object hallucination detection, across 42 in-domain text-related visual benchmarks. Beyond quantitative metrics, LVLM-eHub incorporates an online arena platform that enables user-level evaluation in open-world question-answering scenarios, thereby capturing dimensions of model performance that standardized benchmarks may miss [ss_478371]. The study also investigates how specific model configurations and modality alignment strategies influence overall performance, providing actionable insights for future model design. The scale and rigor of LVLM-eHub reflect a maturing field that recognizes the insufficiency of narrow, task-specific evaluations and the need for holistic assessments that can guide both research and deployment decisions.

While evaluation frameworks illuminate what models can and cannot do, efficiency in training and adaptation remains a critical practical concern. The CROME framework addresses this challenge directly, proposing an efficient vision-language instruction tuning approach centered on a novel gated cross-modal adapter [arxiv_2408.06610]. Unlike many existing methods that require expensive retraining of the underlying language model, CROME combines visual and textual representations prior to input into a frozen large language model, training only a lightweight adapter with minimal parameters. This design philosophy prioritizes cost-effective adaptation without sacrificing performance, and the framework demonstrates superior zero-shot results on standard visual question answering and instruction-following benchmarks [arxiv_2408.06610]. The authors also highlight that current approaches in the field have tended to focus disproportionately on zero-shot performance, offering insufficient guidance for task-specific fine-tuning—a gap that CROME explicitly aims to address. Comparing CROME with the broader landscape of MLLM adaptation, it is notable that the efficiency imperative it embodies is not merely a technical preference but a response to real-world constraints around computational cost and accessibility.

The tension between large-scale generalist models and the need for specialized, domain-adapted systems is further illustrated by work on semiconductor electron microscopy analysis. This research presents a framework for interpreting electron microscopy images in semiconductor manufacturing, leveraging a teacher-student approach in which pre-trained MLLMs such as GPT-4 generate instruction-following data that is subsequently used to fine-tune smaller multimodal models (SMMs) [arxiv_2409.07463]. The resulting instruction-tuned assistant is designed for zero-shot visual question answering and classification tasks within this highly specialized domain. A distinctive feature of this approach is its integration of knowledge engineering with machine learning, enabling domain-specific expertise to be transferred from larger to smaller models while greatly reducing the need for extensive human labeling [arxiv_2409.07463]. The authors emphasize that their framework is secure, cost-effective, and customizable—qualities that are particularly salient in industrial contexts where data privacy and deployment constraints are paramount. This work stands in productive contrast to CROME [arxiv_2408.06610]: while both prioritize efficient adaptation of multimodal models, the semiconductor microscopy framework is explicitly domain-driven and relies on knowledge distillation from larger models, whereas CROME pursues a more general-purpose adapter-based solution applicable across diverse tasks.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate several interconnected themes in the current MLLM literature. First, the interpretability of vision-language transformers remains an open and important problem, with tools like VL-InterpreT [arxiv_2203.17247] representing early but significant steps toward transparency. Second, comprehensive evaluation frameworks such as LVLM-eHub [ss_478371] are essential for understanding the true capabilities and limitations of these models across diverse tasks and conditions. Third, the efficiency of training and adaptation is a central concern, addressed through lightweight adapter architectures [arxiv_2408.06610] and knowledge distillation strategies [arxiv_2409.07463]. Finally, the application of MLLMs to specialized domains demonstrates both the versatility of these models and the continued importance of domain-specific customization. Collectively, these threads define a research agenda in which capability, efficiency, interpretability, and applicability are pursued in tandem.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language — evidence cluster A

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has emerged as one of the most active frontiers in artificial intelligence research, driven by the recognition that real-world understanding requires the integration of multiple sensory modalities rather than reliance on text alone. Early work in this space focused primarily on the pairing of vision and language, establishing foundational architectures and training paradigms that subsequent research has built upon, extended, and critically evaluated.

A landmark contribution to vision-language modeling is PaLI (Pathways Language and Image model), which demonstrated that the principles of scaling and flexible task interfaces that proved transformative for unimodal language models could be effectively extended to joint vision-language settings [arxiv_2209.06794]. PaLI generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs, enabling it to perform a wide range of tasks spanning vision, language, and multimodal understanding across many languages. Crucially, the authors found that joint scaling of both the vision and language components is essential for strong performance, a finding that challenged the prevailing tendency to invest disproportionately in scaling language model parameters while leaving vision encoders comparatively underdeveloped. To address this imbalance, the PaLI team trained a large 4-billion parameter Vision Transformer (ViT-e), capitalizing on existing pre-trained encoder-decoder language models and ViTs to leverage the substantial prior investment in their training [arxiv_2209.06794]. This work established an important methodological precedent: that the vision backbone must be treated as a first-class component in multimodal architectures, not merely an auxiliary feature extractor appended to a language model.

Building on the vision-language paradigm, subsequent research has sought to extend multimodal modeling beyond the dyadic pairing of vision and text to encompass additional modalities, most notably audio. The VALOR (Vision-Audio-Language Omni-peRception pretraining) model represents a significant step in this direction, proposing an end-to-end framework that jointly models the relationships among vision, audio, and language simultaneously [ss_478472]. Unlike the majority of vision-language pretraining models that treat audio as an afterthought or ignore it entirely, VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations alongside a decoder for multimodal conditional text generation. The model is trained using two purpose-designed pretext tasks: Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA), which projects vision, language, and audio into a shared common space to build pairwise and joint alignment, and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC), which trains the model to generate text conditioned on vision, audio, or both modalities together [ss_478472]. This trimodal approach reflects a growing consensus in the field that the richness of human perception cannot be adequately captured by vision-language models alone, and that audio provides complementary semantic signals that improve both understanding and generation tasks. Compared to PaLI, which focuses on scaling within the vision-language domain, VALOR prioritizes breadth of modality coverage, illustrating a productive tension in the field between depth of integration within a limited set of modalities and the ambition to incorporate the full spectrum of perceptual inputs.

While architectural innovation has driven much of the progress in MLLMs, a parallel and equally important strand of research has focused on rigorous evaluation. The LVLM-eHub benchmark addresses a critical gap in the literature by providing a comprehensive, systematic evaluation of publicly available large vision-language models [arxiv_2306.09265]. The benchmark encompasses eight representative LVLMs, including InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, and evaluates them across six categories of multimodal capabilities—including visual question answering and embodied artificial intelligence—using 47 standard text-related visual benchmarks, supplemented by an online arena platform for open-world user-level evaluation [arxiv_2306.09265]. This dual-track evaluation methodology, combining quantitative capability assessment with more naturalistic human-in-the-loop evaluation, reflects an important methodological maturation in the field. Among the key findings reported is that instruction-tuned LVLMs demonstrate notable advantages, underscoring the importance of alignment techniques in translating raw multimodal capabilities into practically useful behavior. The existence of such a benchmark is significant not only for the comparative insights it provides but also because it establishes a shared evaluation infrastructure that enables more principled progress tracking across the rapidly evolving landscape of multimodal models.

The challenge of accurately interpreting visual content and answering questions grounded in real-world images remains a persistent bottleneck, even for state-of-the-art systems. The Veagle model directly targets this limitation, proposing a novel approach to enhance the multimodal capabilities of existing vision-language models in response to observed deficiencies in both VLMs and MLLMs [arxiv_2403.08773]. Veagle positions itself as an extension of large language models into the multimodal domain, aiming to address the gap between the impressive benchmark performance of current systems and their frequent failures in accurately interpreting images and answering questions in realistic scenarios [arxiv_2403.08773]. This work is representative of a broader trend in which researchers take stock of the limitations revealed by comprehensive evaluations such as LVLM-eHub and respond with targeted architectural or training innovations. The interplay between evaluation-driven diagnosis and model development is increasingly characteristic of the field's maturation.

Taken together, these works illuminate several defining tensions and trajectories in the development of multimodal large language models. The question of how to scale vision and language components in a balanced and mutually reinforcing manner, raised prominently by PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], remains open and consequential. The aspiration to move beyond vision-language dyads toward richer, omni-perceptual systems, exemplified by VALOR [ss_478472], points toward a future in which models engage with the full complexity of multimodal human experience. Meanwhile, the establishment of rigorous evaluation frameworks such as LVLM-eHub [arxiv_2306.09265] and the targeted model improvements proposed in work like Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773] reflect the field's growing emphasis on accountability and practical utility alongside raw capability. These threads collectively define the current state of multimodal large language model research and set the agenda for ongoing investigation.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language — evidence cluster B

Visual Question Answering (VQA) has emerged as one of the most consequential benchmark tasks for evaluating multimodal large language models (MLLMs), precisely because it demands the simultaneous exercise of natural language understanding, visual perception, and knowledge-based reasoning. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2411.17558], VQA sits at the intersection of natural language processing and computer vision, and its progressive adoption as a standard evaluation protocol reflects the broader ambition of the field to develop systems capable of grounded, cross-modal inference. The survey traces the evolution of VQA from early pipeline-based architectures toward contemporary models that leverage vision-language pretraining and large-scale instruction tuning, offering a detailed account of how modal information is extracted, fused, and reasoned over. Crucially, [arxiv_2411.17558] distinguishes between the extraction of internal knowledge—encoded within model parameters during pretraining—and the introduction of external knowledge sources, a distinction that proves important when evaluating the limits of purely parametric approaches to complex visual reasoning.

The architectural backbone of modern MLLMs typically involves coupling a pretrained visual encoder with a large language model, a design paradigm that has gained considerable traction following the success of models such as LLaVA. [arxiv_2310.03211] provides a systematic comparative analysis of this class of multimodal instruction-tuning approaches, evaluating their zero-shot generalization across a diverse battery of tasks including complex reasoning, open-ended conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification. The study's ablation experiments yield actionable insights regarding architectural choices—particularly the mechanisms by which visual encoders are grafted onto language model backbones—and reveal that while instruction-tuned multimodal models demonstrate promising zero-shot capabilities, current approaches remain limited in important respects. This finding resonates with a broader pattern in the literature: the integration of visual and linguistic modalities does not automatically produce the kind of deep semantic alignment one might expect.

This concern is taken up directly by [arxiv_2109.10246], which investigates whether vision-and-language pretraining actually improves the lexical grounding of internal linguistic representations. The paper's central motivation is the longstanding criticism that text-only language models lack grounding—that is, a meaningful connection between words and their referents in the physical world. VL models trained jointly on text and image or video data have been proposed as a remedy, on the assumption that exposure to visual context during pretraining would enrich the semantic content of learned representations. However, [arxiv_2109.10246] challenges this assumption through a suite of analyses including clustering, probing, and performance on commonsense question answering, all conducted in a language-only evaluation setting. The results are sobering: multimodal models fail to significantly outperform their text-only counterparts on these measures, suggesting that the benefits of visual pretraining may be largely confined to tasks that explicitly require cross-modal processing at inference time, rather than producing more grounded linguistic representations in general. This finding stands in productive tension with the optimistic framing of VL pretraining found in [arxiv_2411.17558], which documents substantial empirical gains on VQA benchmarks without necessarily interrogating whether those gains reflect genuine semantic grounding or more superficial statistical associations.

A separate but related challenge concerns the quality and availability of instruction-tuning data for vision-language tasks. [arxiv_2308.12714] identifies the scarcity of high-quality multimodal instruction data as a significant bottleneck in the development of capable MLLMs. The dominant paradigm at the time of writing, exemplified by LLaVA, addresses this scarcity by using a language-only version of GPT-4 to generate instruction-following data from pre-annotated image captions and bounding box descriptions. While pragmatically effective, this approach is inherently limited: because the generative model never directly observes the images, it cannot capture fine-grained visual details, and the resulting data may fail to reflect the full complexity of real visual scenes. [arxiv_2308.12714] proposes an alternative framework—Visual Instruction Generation and Correction (VIGC)—that leverages existing MLLMs to generate instruction data directly from images, thereby circumventing the need for language-only proxies. The authors acknowledge, however, that currently available MLLMs are themselves imperfect generators, prone to producing inadequate or factually incorrect responses, which motivates the correction component of their proposed pipeline.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate a set of interrelated tensions that characterize the current state of multimodal large language model research. On one hand, [arxiv_2411.17558] and [arxiv_2310.03211] document the rapid empirical progress enabled by vision-language pretraining and multimodal instruction tuning, with VQA benchmarks serving as a primary site of evaluation. On the other hand, [arxiv_2109.10246] raises fundamental questions about whether the internal representations produced by these models are genuinely grounded in visual meaning or whether performance gains are achieved through other means. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2308.12714] draws attention to the practical constraints imposed by data quality and the circularity risks inherent in using imperfect MLLMs to generate training data for future MLLMs. The architectural insights offered by [arxiv_2310.03211] regarding encoder grafting and instruction tuning complement the data-centric perspective of [arxiv_2308.12714], together suggesting that progress in the field will require advances on both the modeling and data fronts simultaneously. The grounding question raised by [arxiv_2109.10246] further implies that benchmark performance alone may be an insufficient criterion for evaluating the maturity of multimodal systems, and that more diagnostic, representation-level analyses will be necessary to understand what these models have actually learned from their exposure to paired visual and linguistic data.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing and computer vision to enable systems capable of reasoning across text, images, and other sensory modalities. This subsection reviews foundational contributions to the field, examining the methodological innovations that have shaped how MLLMs are trained, aligned, and evaluated, as well as the persistent challenges that remain open problems in the literature.

A seminal contribution to the field is the work introducing LLaVA (Large Language and Vision Assistant), which presented one of the first systematic attempts to apply instruction tuning to the multimodal domain [arxiv_2304.08485]. Prior to this work, instruction tuning had demonstrated considerable success in improving the zero-shot generalization of large language models on purely textual tasks, yet its application to vision-language settings remained largely unexplored. The authors addressed this gap by leveraging a language-only version of GPT-4 to generate multimodal instruction-following data, pairing image content with diverse linguistic instructions to create a training corpus capable of teaching a model to respond to open-ended visual queries. The resulting system connected a vision encoder to a large language model in an end-to-end fashion, enabling general-purpose visual and language understanding. Empirically, LLaVA achieved an 85.1% relative score compared with GPT-4 on a synthetic multimodal instruction-following benchmark and demonstrated strong performance when fine-tuned on Science QA, establishing a compelling proof of concept for the visual instruction tuning paradigm [arxiv_2304.08485]. The significance of this work lies not only in its empirical results but in its methodological framing: by treating multimodal alignment as an instruction-following problem amenable to data-driven solutions, it opened a productive research direction that subsequent work has sought to refine and extend.

One important limitation of the original visual instruction tuning framework is its reliance on image-level alignment between visual and linguistic representations, which can be insufficiently granular for tasks requiring detailed spatial or regional understanding. This concern motivates the Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT) approach, which extends the MLLM framework by integrating an additional region-level vision encoder alongside the standard image-level encoder [arxiv_2308.13437]. The core insight is that coarse image-language alignment, while effective for many tasks, leaves a gap in fine-grained cross-modal understanding that can be addressed by explicitly grounding language to sub-image regions. To support this architectural extension, the authors designed multiple data generation strategies to construct an image-region-language instruction dataset, enabling the model to learn correspondences between linguistic expressions and specific spatial locations within images. Both quantitative experiments and qualitative analyses are presented to validate the approach [arxiv_2308.13437]. Compared to the foundational LLaVA framework [arxiv_2304.08485], PVIT represents a targeted refinement: rather than reconceiving the overall training paradigm, it augments the visual processing pipeline to capture information at multiple levels of granularity, thereby promoting more detailed image comprehension. This distinction between image-level and region-level alignment is an important axis along which MLLM architectures can be differentiated, and it anticipates broader discussions in the literature about the resolution and specificity of visual representations required for different downstream tasks.

A complementary challenge concerns the ability of MLLMs to process and reason about text that appears visually within images, a capability sometimes referred to as scene text understanding or visual reading. Despite the impressive general capabilities demonstrated by models such as LLaVA, a majority of visually tuned models struggle to comprehend textual content embedded in images, a deficiency attributed primarily to limitations in training data [arxiv_2406.06730]. To address this, the TRINS dataset was introduced as a text-rich image instruction resource built upon the LAION corpus using hybrid annotation strategies combining machine-assisted and human-assisted processes [arxiv_2406.06730]. Containing over 39,000 text-rich images and more than 102,000 questions, TRINS is notable for the length and complexity of its annotations, which are significantly longer than those found in related datasets and thereby pose new challenges for model comprehension. Alongside the dataset, the authors proposed a Language-vision Reading Assistant (LaRA) architecture designed to enhance reading ability in multimodal models [arxiv_2406.06730]. This work highlights an important dimension of multimodal capability that is orthogonal to the spatial grounding concerns addressed by PVIT [arxiv_2308.13437]: whereas the latter focuses on where visual content is located, TRINS and its associated model address what written language within images says. Together, these contributions suggest that the training data and architectural choices required for robust multimodal understanding are multifaceted, with different aspects of visual comprehension demanding distinct solutions.

Cutting across these specific methodological contributions is a broader concern about the interpretability and explainability of MLLMs, which has attracted increasing scholarly attention as these systems are deployed in consequential settings. A comprehensive survey of explainable and interpretable MLLMs situates the field within the wider trajectory of AI development, noting that the convergence of large language models and computer vision has catalyzed the rise of multimodal AI capable of spanning text, vision, audio, and video modalities [arxiv_2412.02104]. The survey acknowledges the impressive capabilities of MLLMs in tasks such as image-text generation, visual question answering, and cross-modal retrieval, while emphasizing that the complexity and scale of these systems introduce significant challenges for transparency, trustworthiness, and reliability, particularly in high-stakes applications [arxiv_2412.02104]. This framing is consequential for evaluating the contributions reviewed above: the instruction tuning paradigm pioneered by LLaVA [arxiv_2304.08485], the region-level alignment of PVIT [arxiv_2308.13437], and the reading-focused training of TRINS [arxiv_2406.06730] all advance capability, but the survey literature reminds researchers that capability gains must be accompanied by progress in understanding why and how these models produce their outputs. The interpretability challenge is thus not peripheral to MLLM research but central to its responsible development, shaping the criteria by which architectural and data-centric innovations should ultimately be assessed.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, building directly upon the transformative success of large language models (LLMs) in natural language processing. At their core, LLMs constitute a class of deep learning architectures characterized by transformer-based designs, billions to trillions of parameters, and training on vast corpora, enabling them to perform tasks ranging from text generation and language translation to question answering and code analysis [arxiv_2412.03220]. MLLMs extend this foundational capability by incorporating the ability to process and interpret multiple data modalities simultaneously, including images, audio, and video, thereby empowering systems with capacities such as video editing and cross-modal reasoning [arxiv_2412.03220]. This architectural evolution from unimodal to multimodal processing has opened a broad research frontier, with scholars investigating how best to train, evaluate, distill, and specialize these systems across an increasingly diverse range of tasks and deployment contexts.

A central concern in the MLLM literature is the question of how effectively these models can be adapted to tasks that require genuine visual understanding rather than superficial pattern recognition. Early MLLM applications concentrated heavily on vision-language tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering (VQA), which, while demonstrating the models' capacity to integrate textual and visual signals, do not necessarily probe the full depth of multimodal reasoning [arxiv_2403.13164]. Research has shown that benchmarks focused narrowly on few-shot VQA and captioning neither exploit the genuine strengths of in-context learning nor adequately test its limitations, suggesting that the broader capabilities of multimodal in-context learning (ICL) remain substantially under-explored [arxiv_2403.13164]. This observation has motivated the development of more comprehensive evaluation frameworks. The VL-ICL Bench, for instance, was introduced as a benchmark encompassing a broad spectrum of tasks involving both images and text as inputs and outputs, designed to surface the nuanced challenges that standard benchmarks obscure [arxiv_2403.13164]. Such work underscores a growing consensus that the field requires richer, more demanding evaluation protocols if it is to accurately characterize the capabilities and failure modes of MLLMs.

Alongside evaluation challenges, the question of domain specialization has attracted considerable scholarly attention. While general-purpose MLLMs have demonstrated impressive performance on standard vision-language benchmarks, their extension to domain-specific visual tasks—those requiring more explicit and precise understanding of visual information—has proven considerably more difficult [arxiv_2402.08360]. To address this gap, researchers have proposed methods for transforming domain-specific visual and vision-language datasets into unified question-answering formats. The Visual Question Answering Instruction (VQA-IN) approach exemplifies this direction, converting heterogeneous domain-specific datasets into a standardized format that can be used to fine-tune multiple MLLM architectures, including those built on smaller large language models (sLLMs) [arxiv_2402.08360]. Experimental results from this line of work indicate that such instruction-tuning strategies can achieve strong performance on domain-specific visual tasks while preserving general vision-language capabilities, suggesting that the VQA format serves as a productive interface between the generalist pretraining of LLMs and the specialized demands of domain applications [arxiv_2402.08360]. This finding is particularly significant because it implies that the bottleneck in domain adaptation may lie less in model architecture than in the structure and format of training supervision.

A third major strand of research concerns the computational costs associated with deploying large-scale MLLMs. As model sizes have grown to accommodate increasingly complex multimodal reasoning, the computational and memory requirements of state-of-the-art systems have become prohibitive for resource-constrained environments such as mobile devices or edge computing platforms [arxiv_2410.16236]. Small-scale MLLMs (s-MLLMs) have been proposed as a practical alternative, but these models typically suffer from meaningful performance degradation relative to their large-scale counterparts (l-MLLMs), creating a tension between efficiency and capability [arxiv_2410.16236]. Knowledge distillation has emerged as a promising strategy for resolving this tension, transferring the representational richness of large teacher models into smaller student architectures. The LLaVA-KD framework illustrates this approach in detail, introducing Multimodal Distillation (MDist) to transfer robust cross-modal representations from teacher to student and Relation Distillation (RDist) to convey the teacher model's capacity to capture relationships among visual tokens [arxiv_2410.16236]. A three-stage training scheme further structures the distillation process to maximize knowledge transfer [arxiv_2410.16236]. Compared to the domain-adaptation work discussed above, which focuses on what MLLMs are trained to do, distillation research focuses on how the knowledge encoded in large models can be made accessible at reduced computational cost—a complementary concern that reflects the practical pressures shaping the field.

Taken together, these research directions reveal a field grappling simultaneously with questions of scope, specialization, and scalability. The recognition that standard benchmarks inadequately capture multimodal ICL capabilities [arxiv_2403.13164] motivates more rigorous evaluation, while the development of instruction-tuning strategies [arxiv_2402.08360] and distillation frameworks [arxiv_2410.16236] addresses the practical challenges of deploying capable MLLMs across diverse domains and hardware constraints. The architectural survey perspective reinforces this picture, situating MLLMs as an advanced subset of LLMs whose multimodal extensions are still being systematically characterized and refined [arxiv_2412.03220]. Collectively, this body of work establishes that the trajectory of MLLM research is defined not merely by scaling but by the interplay of evaluation rigor, task-specific adaptation, and efficient deployment—themes that continue to organize the field's most active lines of inquiry.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) has emerged as one of the most active frontiers in artificial intelligence research, driven by the convergence of advances in large language models, vision encoders, and cross-modal alignment techniques. This body of work reflects a sustained effort to enable machines to perceive, reason about, and generate content across multiple sensory modalities, most prominently vision and language, but increasingly extending to audio and beyond.

A foundational concern in the field is the architecture through which different modalities are represented and integrated. Early approaches to multimodal representation learning relied on separate encoders for each modality, with learned projection layers or attention mechanisms facilitating cross-modal interaction. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2302.00389], transformer-based architectures have proven particularly effective across textual, visual, and audio feature spaces, with cross-modal interaction and complementary information from different modalities identified as crucial for tasks such as Visual Question Answering (VQA), Natural Language for Visual Reasoning (NLVR), and Vision Language Retrieval (VLR). This survey traces the evolution of deep learning multimodal architectures and underscores that the capacity to model correlations between modalities—not merely their individual representations—is what distinguishes high-performing systems. The diversity of transformer variants proposed for multimodal tasks reflects the field's recognition that no single architectural choice is universally optimal, and that the nature of the downstream task often dictates the most appropriate design.

Building on this architectural foundation, recent work has sought to extend multimodal pretraining beyond the dominant vision-language paradigm to incorporate audio as a third modality. [arxiv_2304.08345] introduces VALOR, a Vision-Audio-Language Omni-perception pretraining model that jointly models the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation. Two pretext tasks—Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA) and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC)—are designed to project all three modalities into a common representational space and to condition text generation on any combination of visual, auditory, or joint inputs. This trimodal approach represents a meaningful departure from the predominantly bimodal (vision-language) paradigm and highlights the potential for richer world understanding when acoustic information is incorporated alongside visual and linguistic signals. The construction of a large-scale high-quality dataset to support this pretraining further underscores the data-centric demands of omni-perceptual models.

The question of data quality and scale is indeed central to the advancement of MLLMs, and several recent contributions have directly addressed the scarcity of high-quality instruction tuning data as a limiting factor. [arxiv_2307.04087] presents SVIT (Scaling up Visual Instruction Tuning), which constructs a dataset of 4.2 million visual instruction tuning examples, encompassing 1.6 million conversation question-answer pairs, 1.6 million complex reasoning QA pairs, 1.0 million referring QA pairs, and 106,000 detailed image descriptions. Crucially, the dataset is generated by prompting GPT-4 with rich manual image annotations, ensuring not only volume but also quality and diversity. The authors argue that while existing multimodal models demonstrate impressive visual understanding and reasoning, their limits remain underexplored due to insufficient instruction tuning data. SVIT's approach of leveraging GPT-4 as a data synthesis engine, conditioned on structured human annotations, represents a scalable strategy for overcoming the annotation bottleneck that has historically constrained supervised multimodal learning. This work implicitly positions data scale and diversity as co-equal with architectural innovation in determining model capability.

While scaling instruction tuning data addresses one dimension of MLLM limitation, a complementary line of research has focused on the persistent gap between high-level semantic understanding and fine-grained visual perception. MLLMs trained primarily on image-text pairs tend to excel at holistic scene interpretation and commonsense reasoning but struggle with precise localization, detection, and segmentation tasks. Conversely, dedicated vision perception models, though strong on structured perception benchmarks, are vulnerable to open-world distribution shifts due to their limited model capacity and narrow training objectives. [arxiv_2406.15768] directly confronts this dichotomy by proposing MR-MLLM (Mutually Reinforced Multimodal Large Language Model), a framework designed to synergistically enhance both visual perception and multimodal comprehension. Central to this framework is a shared query fusion mechanism that harmonizes detailed visual inputs from specialized vision models with the linguistic depth of language models. By enabling mutual reinforcement between the two subsystems, MR-MLLM aims to overcome the complementary weaknesses of each: the MLLM gains access to fine-grained spatial information it would otherwise miss, while the perception model benefits from the semantic grounding provided by language. This bidirectional enhancement strategy contrasts with earlier pipeline approaches in which perception and language understanding were treated as sequential rather than interactive processes.

Taken together, these works illuminate several converging themes in MLLM research. First, architectural flexibility—whether through trimodal encoders as in [arxiv_2304.08345] or mutual reinforcement mechanisms as in [arxiv_2406.15768]—is increasingly recognized as essential for handling the heterogeneous nature of real-world multimodal inputs. Second, the quality and diversity of training data, as emphasized by both [arxiv_2307.04087] and the broader survey in [arxiv_2302.00389], remain critical determinants of model generalization. Third, the tension between holistic semantic reasoning and fine-grained perceptual precision, foregrounded by [arxiv_2406.15768], represents a fundamental challenge that architectural and data-centric innovations must jointly address. The field is thus moving toward systems that are simultaneously broader in their modality coverage, richer in their training supervision, and more tightly integrated in their perceptual and linguistic processing pipelines.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing and computer vision into unified architectures capable of reasoning across heterogeneous data modalities. Understanding the trajectory of this field requires examining both the foundational modeling strategies that have shaped how vision and language are jointly represented, and the evaluation frameworks that have been developed to assess the capabilities of these increasingly complex systems.

The conceptual groundwork for modern MLLMs was substantially laid by the paradigm shift initiated by large pre-trained language models such as BERT and the GPT series, which demonstrated that general-purpose representations could be acquired through large-scale pre-training and subsequently adapted to specific tasks through fine-tuning [arxiv_2306.07198]. This paradigm was extended into the joint vision-language space through models such as CLIP, which showed that contrastive pre-training on image-text pairs could yield representations with strong transferability to downstream tasks including image captioning and visual question answering [arxiv_2306.07198]. However, as [arxiv_2306.07198] notes, the application of these vision-language pre-trained models to tasks such as multimodal machine translation—where visual context is expected to inform text-to-text generation—has remained comparatively underexplored, suggesting that the integration of visual and linguistic modalities is still far from uniform across task types.

The tutorial notes from CVPR 2023 provide a useful synthesis of the state of the field at a pivotal moment, situating the development of large multimodal models within the broader context of instruction-tuned language models [arxiv_2306.14895]. The authors describe how instruction tuning, originally developed to improve the ability of language models to follow natural language directives, was extended into the multimodal space to produce models capable of responding to visually grounded instructions. This line of work culminated in efforts to build open-source analogues of multimodal GPT-4, demonstrating that the core capabilities of proprietary systems could be approximated using publicly available resources [arxiv_2306.14895]. The tutorial framework is notable for its emphasis on the architectural prerequisites of multimodal instruction following, including the role of visual encoders, projection layers, and the alignment between visual tokens and the embedding space of the language model backbone.

A central tension in the design of MLLMs concerns the trade-off between preserving the linguistic competencies of pre-trained language models and enabling them to process and reason about visual inputs. The dominant approach in the field has involved unfreezing the language model during multimodal training, allowing its parameters to be updated in response to vision-language data [arxiv_2408.12902]. While this strategy facilitates deep visual understanding, it also introduces the risk of catastrophic forgetting, wherein the model's original natural language processing capabilities are degraded as a consequence of fine-tuning on a different data distribution [arxiv_2408.12902]. This phenomenon represents a fundamental challenge for the field, as it implies that gains in visual reasoning may come at the cost of linguistic fluency and general-purpose text understanding.

To address this limitation, an alternative strategy involves freezing the parameters of the language model entirely and instead training lightweight adapter modules to bridge the gap between visual representations and the frozen language model's input space [arxiv_2408.12902]. While conceptually appealing, prior implementations of this approach have generally failed to achieve performance levels comparable to full fine-tuning, suggesting that simply inserting adapters at the input layer of a frozen language model is insufficient to enable the depth of cross-modal interaction required for complex reasoning tasks. The Inner-Adaptor Architecture (IAA) proposed by [arxiv_2408.12902] addresses this limitation through a structural innovation: rather than placing multimodal adapters only at the interface between the visual encoder and the language model, IAA incorporates multiple adapters at varying depths within the transformer stack of the frozen language model. This design allows visual information to interact directly with the text-oriented transformer layers at multiple levels of abstraction, facilitating richer and more nuanced integration of visual and linguistic representations without modifying the underlying language model parameters. The authors report that this approach achieves satisfactory multimodal performance while preserving the model's NLP capabilities, offering a promising resolution to the trade-off that has constrained earlier frozen-model approaches.

The rapid proliferation of MLLM architectures has been accompanied by an equally rapid expansion of evaluation methodologies, reflecting the community's recognition that rigorous benchmarking is essential to meaningful progress. [arxiv_2408.08632] presents a comprehensive survey of over 200 benchmarks and evaluation frameworks for MLLMs, organized around five principal dimensions: perception and understanding, cognition and reasoning, specific domains, key capabilities, and other modalities. This taxonomy reveals the breadth of competencies that modern MLLMs are expected to demonstrate, ranging from basic visual perception tasks such as object recognition and scene understanding to higher-order cognitive functions including multi-step reasoning, commonsense inference, and domain-specific knowledge application. The survey also highlights persistent limitations in current evaluation methodologies, including the tendency of existing benchmarks to assess narrow, isolated capabilities rather than the integrated, open-ended reasoning that characterizes real-world multimodal tasks [arxiv_2408.08632]. The authors argue that evaluation should be treated as a foundational discipline in its own right, capable of actively guiding the development of more capable and reliable MLLMs rather than merely serving as a post-hoc measurement tool.

Taken together, these works illuminate the dual imperatives driving research in multimodal large language models: the architectural challenge of integrating visual and linguistic modalities in ways that are both effective and non-destructive to pre-existing capabilities [arxiv_2408.12902], and the evaluative challenge of developing benchmarks that are sufficiently comprehensive and ecologically valid to capture the full range of competencies that such models are expected to exhibit [arxiv_2408.08632]. The historical arc traced by [arxiv_2306.07198] and [arxiv_2306.14895] underscores that these challenges are not newly emergent but have been present since the earliest efforts to unify vision and language in pre-trained models, and that progress has been incremental, shaped by successive refinements in both architecture and assessment methodology.

The question of how visual and linguistic information interact within the internal representations of MLLMs has attracted growing mechanistic interest, moving beyond behavioral benchmarking toward a more principled understanding of the computational processes underlying multimodal reasoning. [arxiv_2411.18620] investigates the cross-modal information flow in autoregressive MLLMs, examining how visual tokens and linguistic tokens exchange information across the transformer layers of the language model backbone. Their findings reveal that visual information is not uniformly distributed throughout the network; rather, it tends to be progressively absorbed into linguistic representations in the middle and upper layers of the transformer stack, with the earliest layers largely preserving modality-specific representations. This asymmetric flow has important implications for architectural design, suggesting that interventions aimed at improving visual grounding should be targeted at the layers where cross-modal integration is most active rather than applied uniformly across the network. Complementing this mechanistic perspective, [arxiv_2203.17247] introduced VL-InterpreT, an interactive visualization tool specifically designed to expose the internal attention dynamics of vision-language transformers. By enabling researchers to inspect token-level attention patterns across both visual and textual streams, VL-InterpreT makes it possible to identify which image regions and linguistic concepts are being co-attended at each layer, providing a qualitative window into the alignment mechanisms that quantitative benchmarks alone cannot reveal. Together, these interpretability-oriented contributions underscore the importance of treating MLLMs not merely as black-box performance systems but as objects of scientific inquiry whose internal mechanisms warrant systematic investigation. The broader survey of explainability methods in [arxiv_2412.02104] further contextualizes these efforts, cataloguing the range of post-hoc and intrinsic interpretability techniques that have been applied to multimodal models and identifying the substantial gap that remains between the interpretability tools available for unimodal language models and those applicable to their multimodal counterparts.

The challenge of extending MLLMs beyond the canonical image-text dyad to encompass richer sensory modalities has emerged as a defining frontier in the field. [arxiv_2304.08345] and its companion publication [ss_478472] present the VALOR framework, which jointly models vision, audio, and language in a unified pretraining architecture. By designing pretext tasks that require the model to ground textual descriptions in both visual and auditory signals simultaneously, VALOR demonstrates that the complementary information carried by different perceptual channels can be leveraged to produce representations that are more robust and semantically richer than those derived from any single modality pair. This omni-perceptual approach anticipates the trajectory described in [arxiv_2410.05608], which surveys the expanding frontier of multimodal pretraining to include sensors, physiological signals, and other non-standard data streams alongside the more familiar vision-language combination. The tutorial framing of [arxiv_2410.05608] is particularly valuable for situating these developments within a unified conceptual framework, emphasizing that the core technical challenges of modality alignment, representation fusion, and instruction following generalize across sensory domains even as the specific architectural solutions differ. This perspective invites a reconsideration of the term "multimodal" itself: whereas early usage in the NLP and computer vision communities referred almost exclusively to the image-text combination, contemporary usage increasingly encompasses a far broader range of signal types, and the architectural principles developed for vision-language models are being actively adapted to accommodate this expanded scope.

The development of domain-specific MLLMs represents a particularly active area of applied research, driven by the recognition that general-purpose models trained on broad web-scale data may lack the specialized visual vocabulary and reasoning patterns required for professional or scientific applications. [arxiv_2409.07463] exemplifies this trend through its development of a small-scale vision-language assistant tailored to semiconductor electron microscopy, employing a teacher-student distillation paradigm in which a large general-purpose MLLM such as GPT-4 is used to generate instruction-following data that is subsequently used to fine-tune a compact domain-adapted model. This approach is notable for its pragmatic resolution of a fundamental tension in domain adaptation: the most capable models are typically too large and computationally expensive for deployment in specialized industrial settings, while smaller models lack the general visual reasoning capabilities needed to bootstrap domain-specific competencies from scratch. By using the large model as a knowledge source rather than a deployment target, [arxiv_2409.07463] demonstrates a scalable pathway for translating the capabilities of frontier MLLMs into resource-constrained professional contexts. A related strategy is pursued in [arxiv_2502.09906], which constructs a large multimodal dataset and foundation model for insect understanding in the context of precision agriculture, demonstrating that domain-specific visual knowledge that is systematically underrepresented in general web-scale corpora can be effectively incorporated through targeted data curation and domain-focused pretraining. These domain adaptation efforts collectively highlight the importance of data as a first-class design variable in MLLM development, complementing the architectural innovations that have dominated much of the theoretical literature.

The efficiency of MLLM training and inference has become an increasingly prominent concern as model sizes have grown and deployment contexts have diversified. [arxiv_2408.06610] proposes CROME, a cross-modal adapter framework that enables efficient vision-language instruction tuning by introducing lightweight adapter modules that mediate between a frozen visual encoder and a frozen language model, thereby avoiding the computational cost of full model retraining while maintaining competitive performance on zero-shot and task-specific benchmarks. The efficiency gains achieved by CROME are particularly relevant for the growing class of applications that require rapid adaptation to new visual domains or task specifications without access to large-scale computational infrastructure. Extending this efficiency-oriented perspective to the specific challenge of model compression, [arxiv_2410.16236] introduces LLaVA-KD, a knowledge distillation framework that transfers the multimodal reasoning capabilities of large-scale MLLMs to significantly smaller student models. The framework employs a two-stage distillation process that separately addresses the alignment of visual representations and the transfer of language generation capabilities, demonstrating that the performance gap between large and small MLLMs can be substantially reduced through carefully designed distillation objectives. Taken together, CROME and LLaVA-KD represent complementary strategies for democratizing access to MLLM capabilities: the former reduces the cost of adaptation for new tasks and domains, while the latter reduces the cost of deployment by enabling capable small models to be derived from large ones. Both contributions reflect a broader maturation of the field, in which the initial emphasis on maximizing raw performance is increasingly balanced by practical considerations of computational cost, accessibility, and deployability across heterogeneous hardware environments.

The quality and diversity of instruction-tuning data have been consistently identified as critical determinants of MLLM performance, motivating a substantial body of work on data construction methodologies. [arxiv_2304.08485] established the foundational paradigm of using language-only GPT-4 to generate multimodal instruction-following data from image captions and bounding box annotations, producing the LLaVA dataset and model that became a widely adopted baseline in the field. Subsequent work has sought to address the limitations of this approach, particularly its reliance on pre-annotated metadata rather than direct image understanding. [arxiv_2308.12714] proposes VIGC, a framework in which a multimodal model is used to iteratively generate and self-correct visual instruction data by directly processing images, thereby producing instruction-response pairs that are more tightly grounded in actual visual content.
