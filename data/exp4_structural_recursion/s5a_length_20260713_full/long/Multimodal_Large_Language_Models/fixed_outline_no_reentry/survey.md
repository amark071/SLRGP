# Multimodal Large Language Models (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, fundamentally reshaping how computational systems perceive, reason about, and generate content that spans multiple sensory modalities. At its core, this research trajectory is motivated by the observation that human cognition is inherently multimodal: people integrate visual, auditory, linguistic, and other sensory streams simultaneously and effortlessly. Replicating this capacity in machine learning systems has therefore become a central ambition of the field, and the rapid maturation of large language models (LLMs) has provided a powerful substrate upon which multimodal capabilities can be constructed [arxiv_2402.12451]. The resulting landscape of MLLMs is both expansive and rapidly evolving, encompassing diverse architectural paradigms, training strategies, evaluation methodologies, and application domains that collectively define the current frontier of multimodal AI research.

The intellectual lineage of modern MLLMs can be traced through the broader history of vision-language pre-training (VLP), which established the foundational techniques for aligning visual and textual representations. Early VLP research concentrated on specific task categories: image-text tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering (VQA), and image-text retrieval; core computer vision tasks including open-set image classification, object detection, and segmentation; and video-text tasks encompassing video captioning and video question answering [arxiv_2210.09263]. These specialist systems demonstrated that pre-training on large-scale image-text corpora could yield transferable representations with strong downstream performance, but they were fundamentally limited in scope, each model being optimized for a narrow slice of the multimodal problem space. The transition from such specialist architectures toward general-purpose multimodal assistants constitutes a defining theme in the recent literature [arxiv_2309.10020]. This shift was catalyzed by the success of instruction-tuned LLMs, which demonstrated that language models could be repurposed as flexible, dialogue-capable agents through relatively lightweight fine-tuning procedures. Researchers quickly recognized that analogous strategies could be applied to multimodal systems, yielding models capable of following open-ended visual instructions without task-specific architectural modifications.

Architecturally, MLLMs typically consist of three principal components: a visual encoder responsible for extracting feature representations from images or other non-textual inputs, a language model backbone that processes and generates text, and a cross-modal alignment module that bridges the representational gap between the two modalities [arxiv_2402.12451]. The design choices made at each of these stages have significant consequences for model performance, computational efficiency, and generalizability. Visual encoders are commonly derived from contrastive pre-training frameworks such as CLIP, which align image and text representations in a shared embedding space, while language model backbones are typically large autoregressive transformers. The alignment module, which may take the form of a projection layer, a cross-attention mechanism, or a more elaborate adapter architecture, is perhaps the most actively researched component, as it determines how effectively visual information is communicated to the language model [arxiv_2309.10020].

A particularly important line of work concerns the efficiency of cross-modal adaptation. Training large multimodal models from scratch is prohibitively expensive, and full fine-tuning of pre-trained LLMs for multimodal tasks can be similarly costly. This has motivated the development of parameter-efficient approaches that freeze the language model backbone and train only lightweight adapter modules. CROME exemplifies this direction, proposing a gated cross-modal adapter that combines visual and textual representations before they are fed into a frozen LLM [arxiv_2408.06610]. By training only this minimal set of parameters, CROME achieves competitive zero-shot performance on standard VQA and instruction-following benchmarks while also enabling efficient task-specific fine-tuning. This stands in contrast to approaches that require full or partial retraining of the language model, which impose substantially higher computational costs and reduce the practical accessibility of multimodal capabilities. The broader survey literature corroborates the importance of such efficiency considerations, noting that the widespread deployment of MLLMs is contingent on developing training and adaptation strategies that are cost-effective without sacrificing capability [arxiv_2402.12451].

The scope of modalities addressed by contemporary multimodal research has expanded considerably beyond the image-text dyad that dominated early VLP work. Recent tutorials and surveys have emphasized the integration of audio, video, sensor data, and other input streams alongside vision and language [arxiv_2410.05608]. This expansion reflects both the practical demands of real-world applications, where information arrives in heterogeneous formats, and the theoretical ambition of building truly general-purpose AI systems. The technical challenges associated with incorporating additional modalities are non-trivial: each modality introduces its own representational requirements, temporal dynamics, and alignment difficulties. Video, for instance, requires models to reason about temporal sequences of frames, while audio introduces spectral and prosodic features that have no direct visual analogue. Addressing these challenges has spurred the development of unified architectures capable of processing diverse input types through shared or modality-specific encoding pathways [arxiv_2309.10020].

Instruction tuning has emerged as a particularly influential training paradigm for MLLMs, enabling models to follow complex, open-ended instructions expressed in natural language. The technique involves fine-tuning a pre-trained model on a curated dataset of instruction-response pairs, teaching the model to interpret and execute a wide variety of tasks described in natural language. In the multimodal context, instruction tuning datasets pair visual inputs with textual instructions and expected responses, covering tasks ranging from image description and VQA to more complex reasoning and grounding tasks [arxiv_2402.12451]. The construction of such datasets is itself a significant research challenge, as manually annotating large quantities of instruction-following data is expensive and time-consuming. One innovative solution involves leveraging existing large multimodal models, such as GPT-4, to generate synthetic instruction-following data through a teacher-student framework. This approach has been applied in domain-specific contexts, such as the analysis of electron microscopy images in semiconductor manufacturing, where a large pre-trained model generates training data for a smaller, specialized model, substantially reducing the need for human annotation while preserving domain-relevant knowledge [arxiv_2409.07463]. Such knowledge distillation strategies illustrate how the capabilities of frontier MLLMs can be transferred to more compact and deployable systems, broadening the practical utility of multimodal AI.

The evaluation of MLLMs presents methodological challenges that have attracted considerable scholarly attention. Standard benchmarks for individual tasks such as VQA or image captioning provide useful but incomplete pictures of model capability, as they do not capture the full range of competencies expected of a general-purpose multimodal assistant. Comprehensive evaluation frameworks have therefore been developed to assess models across multiple dimensions simultaneously. The LVLM-eHub benchmark, for example, evaluates thirteen representative large vision-language models across five categories of multimodal capability, including visual question answering and object hallucination, using forty-two in-domain text-related visual benchmarks [ss_478371]. Crucially, the framework also incorporates an online arena platform that enables user-level evaluation in open-world question-answering scenarios, complementing the quantitative benchmarks with more naturalistic assessments of model behavior. This dual approach reflects a growing recognition that laboratory benchmarks, while necessary, may not fully predict how models perform in deployment contexts where user queries are diverse, ambiguous, and unpredictable. The study further investigates how specific model configurations and modality alignment strategies influence performance, providing empirical grounding for architectural design decisions.

The interpretability of multimodal transformer models is another dimension of the field that has received increasing attention, motivated by the recognition that understanding internal model mechanisms is essential for building trustworthy and capable systems. While interpretability tools have become relatively well-developed for unimodal NLP models, the internal workings of vision-language transformers remain comparatively opaque. VL-InterpreT addresses this gap by providing interactive visualizations of attention patterns and hidden representations in multimodal transformers, tracking statistics across attention heads throughout all layers for both visual and linguistic components and visualizing both cross-modal and intra-modal attention [arxiv_2203.17247]. The tool is designed to be task-agnostic, making it applicable across a range of multimodal architectures and tasks. The development of such interpretability infrastructure is important not only for scientific understanding but also for practical model improvement, as identifying failure modes and unexpected attention patterns can guide architectural refinements and training modifications.

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals a field characterized by rapid progress along multiple fronts simultaneously. The transition from specialist VLP models to general-purpose multimodal assistants has been enabled by advances in instruction tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, and large-scale pre-training [arxiv_2309.10020; arxiv_2402.12451]. The expansion of modality coverage beyond vision and language toward audio, video, and sensor data reflects an ambition to build systems that engage with the full complexity of real-world information environments [arxiv_2410.05608]. Evaluation frameworks have grown more comprehensive and ecologically valid, moving beyond single-task benchmarks toward holistic assessments of multimodal capability [ss_478371]. Domain-specific applications, such as semiconductor microscopy analysis, demonstrate that the techniques developed for general-purpose MLLMs can be adapted to specialized contexts through knowledge distillation and targeted instruction tuning [arxiv_2409.07463]. And the development of interpretability tools signals a maturing awareness that capability alone is insufficient; understanding and transparency are equally essential properties for systems that are to be deployed responsibly [arxiv_2203.17247]. These converging threads define the current state of multimodal large language model research and set the agenda for the investigations that follow.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential trajectories in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing, computer vision, and cross-modal representation learning into unified architectures capable of reasoning across text, images, audio, and beyond. This subsection surveys the foundational approaches, architectural innovations, training paradigms, and evaluative frameworks that have shaped the current landscape of multimodal large language models, drawing attention to both the remarkable progress achieved and the persistent challenges that continue to motivate new research directions.

A central theme in the evolution of MLLMs is the recognition that language and vision must be jointly scaled and co-trained rather than treated as independent modules that are loosely coupled at inference time. The PaLI model exemplifies this philosophy, demonstrating that a jointly scaled language-and-image model, trained with large pre-trained encoder-decoder language models and Vision Transformers, can achieve strong performance across a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks in many languages [arxiv_2209.06794]. Crucially, PaLI's authors found that joint scaling of the vision and language components is essential, and to explore the upper limits of visual capacity, they trained a 4-billion parameter Vision Transformer (ViT-e), finding that larger vision models continue to yield meaningful gains [arxiv_2209.06794]. This finding challenges earlier assumptions that language model capacity was the primary bottleneck and underscores the importance of treating the visual encoder as a first-class citizen in multimodal system design. The PaLI architecture also highlights the value of a flexible, unified task interface: by framing diverse tasks as text generation conditioned on visual and textual inputs, the model achieves broad generalization without requiring task-specific architectural modifications [arxiv_2209.06794].

While vision-language integration has received the greatest share of research attention, a growing body of work argues that truly comprehensive multimodal understanding must extend beyond the visual and textual modalities to incorporate audio. The VALOR model addresses this gap by jointly modeling the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end framework [ss_478472]. VALOR employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, and introduces two pretraining objectives—Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA) and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC)—that simultaneously build vision-language, audio-language, and audiovisual-language alignment while also learning to generate text conditioned on any combination of modalities [ss_478472]. This trimodal approach represents a meaningful departure from the dominant vision-language paradigm and raises important questions about how the inclusion of additional modalities affects representational quality and downstream task performance. Compared to models like PaLI that focus exclusively on vision and language [arxiv_2209.06794], VALOR's omni-perceptual design offers richer grounding for tasks involving video and audio content, though it also introduces greater complexity in data collection and training [ss_478472].

The integration of visual encoders into large language models has emerged as a particularly productive architectural strategy, enabling the reuse of powerful pretrained language model weights while extending their capabilities to visual inputs. This approach, sometimes described as model grafting, involves connecting independently pretrained vision encoders to instruction-tuned LLMs and then fine-tuning the combined system on multimodal instruction data [arxiv_2310.03211]. A comparative analysis of different multimodal instruction tuning approaches reveals that while this paradigm enables effective zero-shot generalization across tasks including complex reasoning, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification, current approaches still exhibit significant limitations in tasks requiring deep visual understanding and compositional reasoning [arxiv_2310.03211]. Rigorous benchmarking and ablation experiments in this line of work provide key insights for guiding architectural choices, suggesting that the manner in which visual features are projected into the language model's embedding space, and the quality and diversity of instruction tuning data, are both critical determinants of downstream performance [arxiv_2310.03211].

The challenge of obtaining high-quality instruction-tuning data for vision-language tasks is a recurring concern in the literature. Dominant paradigms such as LLaVA rely on language-only GPT-4 to generate instruction data, a process that requires pre-annotated image captions and bounding boxes and is therefore limited in its ability to capture fine-grained visual details [arxiv_2308.12714]. To address this limitation, the VIGC framework proposes using available MLLMs themselves to generate and iteratively correct instruction data for vision-language tasks, thereby reducing dependence on external annotation pipelines [arxiv_2308.12714]. However, the authors acknowledge that current MLLMs tend to produce inadequate responses and generate factually incorrect information, which motivates the correction component of their pipeline [arxiv_2308.12714]. This self-improvement loop, in which models generate training data that is then used to refine subsequent model versions, reflects a broader trend toward more autonomous and scalable data curation strategies, though it also raises concerns about error propagation and the amplification of model biases.

Recent work has also sought to address specific representational limitations in existing vision-language models. The Veagle model introduces a novel approach to enhancing multimodal capabilities by targeting the persistent challenge of accurately interpreting images and answering questions in real-world scenarios, a limitation observed across a wide range of current Vision Language Models and MLLMs [arxiv_2403.08773]. Veagle's design responds to the observation that while multimodal models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and visual grounding, they frequently fail in cases that require nuanced visual interpretation or integration of contextual knowledge [arxiv_2403.08773]. This work is representative of a broader class of contributions that take existing MLLM architectures as a starting point and propose targeted modifications to improve specific capability dimensions, rather than proposing entirely new training paradigms.

Visual Question Answering (VQA) has served as one of the primary benchmark tasks for evaluating multimodal language models, and its evolution mirrors the broader trajectory of the field. A comprehensive survey of VQA research situates the task at the intersection of natural language processing and computer vision, tracing its development from early pipeline-based systems to contemporary MLLMs that perform end-to-end reasoning over image-question pairs [arxiv_2411.17558]. The survey highlights the central role of vision-language pretraining models in enabling strong VQA performance, detailing how advances in extracting and fusing modal information have progressively improved both language understanding of images and knowledge reasoning capabilities [arxiv_2411.17558]. Notably, the survey distinguishes between the extraction of internal knowledge encoded in model parameters and the introduction of external knowledge from structured knowledge bases, identifying knowledge reasoning as a frontier challenge that current MLLMs have not fully resolved [arxiv_2411.17558]. This distinction is important because it points to a fundamental tension between the parametric knowledge stored in large pretrained models and the dynamic, structured knowledge that may be required for robust real-world question answering.

A critical perspective on the representational quality of vision-language models comes from work examining whether visual pretraining actually improves lexical grounding—that is, whether models trained on both text and images develop richer semantic representations than their text-only counterparts [arxiv_2109.10246]. Using a suite of analyses including clustering, probing, and performance on commonsense question answering, this study finds that multimodal models fail to significantly outperform text-only variants in language-only settings, suggesting that the benefits of visual grounding do not straightforwardly transfer to improved linguistic representations [arxiv_2109.10246]. This finding has important implications for the theoretical justification of multimodal pretraining: while VL models clearly excel on tasks that require joint reasoning over images and text, the claim that visual grounding enriches language understanding in a general sense remains empirically contested [arxiv_2109.10246]. This result stands in some tension with the broader enthusiasm for multimodal pretraining and calls for more careful analysis of what, precisely, is being learned during joint vision-language training.

The need for systematic evaluation frameworks has become increasingly urgent as the number of available MLLMs has grown rapidly. The LVLM-eHub benchmark addresses this need by providing a comprehensive evaluation of publicly available large multimodal models across six categories of multimodal capabilities, including visual question answering and embodied artificial intelligence, assessed on 47 standard text-related visual benchmarks, as well as an online arena platform for user-level evaluation in open-world question-answering scenarios [arxiv_2306.09265]. The study evaluates eight representative LVLMs including InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, revealing several innovative findings about the relative strengths and weaknesses of instruction-tuned models [arxiv_2306.09265]. The LVLM-eHub framework is notable for its dual approach—combining quantitative capability evaluation with human-centered arena-style assessment—reflecting the recognition that automated benchmarks alone may not capture the full range of qualities that matter for real-world deployment. This evaluation infrastructure complements the architectural and training contributions described above by providing the empirical scaffolding needed to identify where progress is genuine and where apparent improvements may be artifacts of benchmark overfitting.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals a field characterized by rapid architectural innovation, growing ambitions toward omni-modal understanding, and an increasing awareness of the gap between benchmark performance and robust real-world capability. The progression from early vision-language pretraining models to jointly scaled architectures like PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], trimodal systems like VALOR [ss_478472], and instruction-tuned multimodal LLMs evaluated through comprehensive frameworks like LVLM-eHub [arxiv_2306.09265] reflects both the maturation of core techniques and the emergence of new challenges around data quality [arxiv_2308.12714], representational grounding [arxiv_2109.10246], and the reliable evaluation of complex reasoning capabilities [arxiv_2310.03211, arxiv_2411.17558]. These challenges collectively define the frontier along which current and future research in multimodal large language models must advance.

### Evidence theme 3

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together the representational power of large language models (LLMs) with the perceptual richness of visual and other sensory modalities. At their core, MLLMs extend the capabilities of text-based LLMs to process and interpret multiple data modalities, including images, audio, and video, thereby enabling a far broader range of tasks than either modality-specific system could accomplish in isolation [arxiv_2412.03220]. This convergence has catalyzed rapid progress across a wide spectrum of applications, from image-text generation and visual question answering to cross-modal retrieval and video editing, while simultaneously surfacing a new set of technical and conceptual challenges that the research community is actively working to address [arxiv_2412.02104].

A foundational contribution to this trajectory is the work on visual instruction tuning, most prominently exemplified by LLaVA (Large Language and Vision Assistant) [arxiv_2304.08485]. Prior to this line of research, instruction tuning had demonstrated considerable success in improving the zero-shot generalization of LLMs on novel tasks, yet its application to the multimodal domain remained largely unexplored. LLaVA addressed this gap by leveraging a language-only GPT-4 model to generate multimodal language-image instruction-following data, which was then used to fine-tune an end-to-end architecture connecting a vision encoder with an LLM. The resulting model exhibited impressive multimodal conversational abilities, sometimes approximating the behavior of multimodal GPT-4 on unseen images and instructions, and achieved a relative score of 85.1% compared with GPT-4 on a synthetic multimodal instruction-following benchmark. When fine-tuned on the Science QA dataset, LLaVA further demonstrated strong performance, underscoring the versatility of the visual instruction tuning paradigm [arxiv_2304.08485]. This work established a template that subsequent research has both built upon and sought to refine.

One important limitation of early visual instruction tuning approaches, including LLaVA, is their reliance on image-level language-image instruction data, which provides only coarse-grained alignment between the visual and linguistic modalities. Recognizing this shortcoming, researchers have proposed methods that operate at a finer spatial granularity. Position-Enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT) extends the MLLM framework by integrating an additional region-level vision encoder alongside the standard image-level encoder [arxiv_2308.13437]. This architectural augmentation enables the model to attend to and reason about specific spatial regions within an image, promoting more detailed comprehension. To support this capability, PVIT introduces multiple data generation strategies for constructing an image-region-language instruction dataset, thereby achieving a more fine-grained cross-modal alignment between the vision modules and the LLM. The contrast between LLaVA and PVIT is instructive: while both rely on instruction tuning as their central training paradigm, PVIT's incorporation of region-level supervision addresses a structural gap in the alignment process that image-level approaches leave unresolved [arxiv_2308.13437]. This distinction highlights a broader trend in the field toward increasingly granular forms of visual grounding.

Another dimension along which MLLMs have been extended concerns their ability to handle domain-specific tasks that require explicit and precise understanding of visual information, rather than the general-purpose visual reasoning that models like LLaVA were primarily designed for. The Visual Question Answering Instruction (VQA-IN) framework addresses this challenge by transforming domain-specific visual and vision-language datasets into a unified question-answering format [arxiv_2402.08360]. By casting diverse domain-specific tasks—such as object detection, segmentation, and other structured visual prediction problems—as instances of visual question answering, VQA-IN enables MLLM architectures to be trained on these tasks without requiring fundamental architectural changes. Notably, the method was applied to smaller versions of LLMs (sLLMs), demonstrating that competitive performance on domain-specific visual tasks can be achieved even under computational constraints. This finding is significant because it suggests that the visual instruction tuning paradigm is not exclusively the province of large-scale models, and that careful data formatting can compensate, at least partially, for reduced model capacity [arxiv_2402.08360].

The question of model scale and computational efficiency is addressed more directly in the LLaVA-KD framework, which proposes a knowledge distillation approach for transferring capabilities from large-scale MLLMs (l-MLLMs) to small-scale MLLMs (s-MLLMs) [arxiv_2410.16236]. The motivation is straightforward: while large-scale MLLMs have achieved impressive performance across a range of benchmarks, their computational demands render them impractical for deployment in resource-constrained environments. Small-scale MLLMs, designed to reduce these costs, typically suffer from significant performance degradation relative to their larger counterparts. LLaVA-KD addresses this trade-off through two complementary distillation mechanisms: Multimodal Distillation (MDist), which transfers the teacher model's robust representations across both visual and linguistic modalities, and Relation Distillation (RDist), which transfers the teacher model's ability to capture relationships among visual tokens. These are combined within a three-stage training scheme intended to maximize the exploitation of the teacher model's knowledge. Compared with the VQA-IN approach, which achieves efficiency through data reformatting and smaller LLM backbones, LLaVA-KD takes a more explicitly architectural route, using the large model itself as a supervisory signal for training the small model [arxiv_2410.16236]. Together, these two lines of work illustrate the range of strategies being pursued to make MLLMs more practically deployable.

A further challenge that has received growing attention is the ability of MLLMs to comprehend textual content embedded within images—a capability that is distinct from general visual understanding and that has proven surprisingly difficult for many visually-tuned models. The TRINS dataset and the associated Language-vision Reading Assistant (LaRA) architecture were introduced specifically to address this limitation [arxiv_2406.06730]. TRINS is a text-rich image instruction dataset built upon LAION using hybrid annotation strategies that combine machine-assisted and human-assisted processes. It contains over 39,000 text-rich images and more than 102,000 questions, with annotations that are notably longer than those in comparable datasets, thereby introducing new challenges for models that must read and reason about embedded text. The observation that a majority of visually-tuned models struggle with this task, primarily due to limitations in training data, points to a broader issue in the field: the distribution of existing instruction-following datasets may not adequately cover the full range of visual understanding tasks that real-world applications demand [arxiv_2406.06730]. This concern is echoed in the literature on multimodal in-context learning, where it has been shown that standard benchmarks may fail to expose important limitations of current models.

The evaluation of MLLMs is itself a significant and evolving area of inquiry. In-context learning (ICL), the ability of a model to rapidly adapt to new tasks using few-shot examples provided as a prompt without weight updates, is a well-established strength of LLMs. However, investigations into multimodal ICL have predominantly focused on few-shot visual question answering and image captioning—tasks that, as argued in the VL-ICL Bench work, neither fully exploit the strengths of ICL nor adequately test its limitations [arxiv_2403.13164]. VL-ICL Bench introduces a comprehensive benchmark encompassing a broad spectrum of tasks involving both images and text as inputs and outputs, designed to probe the genuine capabilities and failure modes of multimodal ICL. The benchmark reveals that many current vision-language models perform surprisingly poorly on tasks that require genuine in-context adaptation, suggesting that the apparent ICL abilities observed on standard benchmarks may be partially illusory. This finding has important implications for how the field assesses progress: if benchmark tasks are not sufficiently challenging or diverse, performance improvements may reflect overfitting to narrow task distributions rather than genuine advances in multimodal reasoning [arxiv_2403.13164].

Cutting across all of these developments is the challenge of interpretability and explainability. As MLLMs grow in complexity and scale, understanding why they produce particular outputs becomes increasingly difficult, yet this understanding is essential for establishing the transparency and trustworthiness required in high-stakes applications [arxiv_2412.02104]. The convergence of LLMs and computer vision systems into unified multimodal frameworks compounds the interpretability challenges already present in each domain individually, since the interactions between visual and linguistic representations introduce additional layers of opacity. Research in this area seeks to develop methods for attributing model outputs to specific input features, understanding the internal representations that mediate cross-modal reasoning, and providing end users with meaningful explanations of model behavior [arxiv_2412.02104]. This concern for interpretability is not merely academic; it is increasingly recognized as a prerequisite for the responsible deployment of MLLMs in domains such as healthcare, legal reasoning, and autonomous systems.

Taken together, the body of work reviewed here traces a coherent arc from the foundational establishment of visual instruction tuning as a viable paradigm for multimodal learning [arxiv_2304.08485], through successive refinements targeting finer-grained alignment [arxiv_2308.13437], domain specificity [arxiv_2402.08360], computational efficiency [arxiv_2410.16236], and reading comprehension [arxiv_2406.06730], to broader questions about evaluation methodology [arxiv_2403.13164] and interpretability [arxiv_2412.02104]. Each of these threads reflects a recognition that the initial successes of MLLMs, while impressive, leave substantial room for improvement along multiple dimensions simultaneously. The field is thus characterized not by a single dominant research agenda but by a rich plurality of complementary efforts, each addressing a different facet of the challenge of building multimodal systems that are capable, efficient, interpretable, and broadly applicable.

### Evidence theme 4

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing, computer vision, and cross-modal representation learning into unified architectures capable of reasoning across heterogeneous input types. Understanding the trajectory of this field requires situating current models within a broader history of multimodal representation learning, examining the architectural innovations that have enabled increasingly capable systems, and critically assessing the data, evaluation, and integration challenges that continue to shape the frontier of the discipline.

The intellectual foundations of modern MLLMs lie in the longer history of multimodal representation learning, which has sought to embed information from different modalities—text, vision, and audio—into shared or aligned representational spaces. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2302.00389], this tradition encompasses a wide range of transformer-based architectures that have demonstrated extraordinary performance across tasks including Visual Question Answering (VQA), Natural Language for Visual Reasoning (NLVR), and Vision Language Retrieval (VLR). A central insight driving this body of work is that cross-modal interaction and the exploitation of complementary information across modalities are essential for models to perform optimally on tasks requiring understanding, recognition, retrieval, or generation [arxiv_2302.00389]. The evolution from early fusion-based approaches to the sophisticated pretraining paradigms that now dominate the field reflects a gradual deepening of this insight, as researchers have developed increasingly principled methods for aligning and integrating representations from fundamentally different data types.

The pretraining paradigm itself, which has proven so transformative in natural language processing through models such as BERT and the GPT series, has been extended with considerable success into the joint vision-language space. [arxiv_2306.07198] surveys this landscape through the lens of multimodal machine translation, noting that models such as CLIP have demonstrated substantial improvements on downstream tasks including image captioning and visual question answering. The survey highlights a notable asymmetry in the literature: while vision-language pretraining has attracted enormous research attention, the application of these pretrained representations to tasks such as multimodal machine translation—where visual information is used to augment text-to-text translation—remains comparatively underexplored [arxiv_2306.07198]. This observation points to a broader pattern in the field, whereby certain high-profile tasks attract disproportionate research effort while other potentially valuable applications remain underdeveloped.

A significant extension of the vision-language pretraining paradigm has been the incorporation of audio as a third modality, moving beyond the dyadic vision-language framework that has dominated much of the literature. [arxiv_2304.08345] proposes VALOR, a Vision-Audio-Language Omni-peRception pretraining model that jointly models relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner. The architecture employs three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, with two pretext tasks—Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA) and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC)—designed to build simultaneous vision-language, audio-language, and audiovisual-language alignment [arxiv_2304.08345]. This trimodal approach represents an important generalization beyond the dominant vision-language paradigm and anticipates the broader ambition of MLLMs to serve as general-purpose perceptual and reasoning systems capable of processing arbitrary combinations of input modalities.

The transition from pretrained multimodal models to instruction-following MLLMs has been substantially enabled by the technique of instruction tuning, which adapts large pretrained models to follow natural language instructions across diverse tasks. [arxiv_2306.14895] provides an accessible account of this transition, situating instruction-tuned large multimodal models within the broader context of GPT-like large models for vision-and-language modeling. The tutorial notes describe how instruction tuning, originally developed for large language models, has been extended into the multimodal space to produce models capable of engaging in open-ended visual dialogue and reasoning [arxiv_2306.14895]. This extension is non-trivial, requiring not only the alignment of visual and linguistic representations but also the construction of appropriate training data that captures the diversity of tasks and interaction styles that instruction-following models are expected to handle.

The question of training data quality and scale has emerged as a central concern in the development of capable MLLMs. [arxiv_2307.04087] directly addresses this bottleneck, arguing that the limits of multimodal capability remain largely underexplored due to the scarcity of high-quality instruction tuning data. In response, the authors introduce SVIT (Scaling up Visual Instruction Tuning), a dataset of 4.2 million visual instruction tuning examples comprising 1.6 million conversation QA pairs, 1.6 million complex reasoning QA pairs, 1.0 million referring QA pairs, and 106,000 detailed image descriptions [arxiv_2307.04087]. Crucially, the dataset is distinguished not only by its volume but also by its quality and diversity, having been generated by prompting GPT-4 with rich manual image annotations. This approach to synthetic data generation—leveraging the capabilities of powerful language models to produce training data at scale—has become an influential methodology in the field, reflecting a broader trend toward using AI systems to bootstrap the training of other AI systems.

Architectural innovation has proceeded in parallel with advances in data curation. A persistent challenge in MLLM design concerns how to integrate visual capabilities into large language models without degrading the natural language processing abilities that make these models valuable in the first place. [arxiv_2408.12902] addresses this tension directly, observing that common methods involving the unfreezing of the language model during multimodal training often lead to a diminution of NLP capabilities. As an alternative, the authors propose the Inner-Adaptor Architecture (IAA), which incorporates multiple multimodal adaptors at varying depths within a frozen large language model, enabling direct interaction with the inherently text-oriented transformer layers without modifying the underlying language model weights [arxiv_2408.12902]. This approach contrasts with strategies that treat the language model as a trainable component, and the empirical results reported suggest that freezing the language model while developing multimodal competencies through carefully positioned adaptors can yield satisfactory outcomes that preserve NLP performance. The IAA framework thus represents a principled response to the stability-plasticity dilemma that arises when adapting powerful pretrained models to new modalities.

A complementary architectural challenge concerns the integration of fine-grained visual perception with high-level multimodal comprehension. MLLMs have generally excelled at high-level image-text interpretation tasks but have struggled with fine-grained visual understanding, while dedicated visual perception models—though strong on detection and segmentation tasks—suffer from open-world distribution shifts due to limited model capacity [arxiv_2406.15768]. [arxiv_2406.15768] proposes MR-MLLM (Mutually Reinforced Multimodal Large Language Model) as a framework that synergistically enhances both visual perception and multimodal comprehension. The key mechanism is a shared query fusion approach that harmonizes detailed visual inputs from specialized vision models with the linguistic depth of language models, enabling the two types of capability to reinforce each other rather than being developed in isolation. This mutual reinforcement paradigm represents an important conceptual advance over architectures that treat visual encoding and language modeling as largely independent components connected only at the input or output stage.

Taken together, the architectural proposals surveyed here—from VALOR's trimodal pretraining [arxiv_2304.08345] to IAA's frozen-LLM adaptor strategy [arxiv_2408.12902] and MR-MLLM's mutual reinforcement framework [arxiv_2406.15768]—reflect a field grappling with a common set of tensions: between scale and quality, between flexibility and stability, and between high-level reasoning and fine-grained perception. These tensions are not merely technical but reflect deeper questions about the appropriate division of labor between specialized and general-purpose components in multimodal systems.

The evaluation of MLLMs has itself become a significant subfield, as the breadth and complexity of these systems have outpaced the capacity of any single benchmark to characterize their capabilities comprehensively. [arxiv_2408.08632] presents a systematic review of 200 benchmarks and evaluations for MLLMs, organized around five dimensions: perception and understanding, cognition and reasoning, specific domains, key capabilities, and other modalities. The survey argues that evaluation should be regarded as a crucial discipline in its own right, one that actively supports the development of MLLMs rather than merely measuring their outputs [arxiv_2408.08632]. This framing is significant because it positions benchmark design as a form of scientific inquiry rather than a purely instrumental activity, with implications for how the community prioritizes research directions and interprets empirical results. The limitations identified in current evaluation methods—including the tendency of benchmarks to saturate quickly as models improve, the difficulty of assessing open-ended generation quality, and the underrepresentation of certain modalities and domains—point to important directions for future work.

The literature reviewed here collectively illustrates the rapid maturation of the MLLM field, from its roots in multimodal representation learning and vision-language pretraining through the development of instruction-tuned models, large-scale training datasets, and sophisticated architectural innovations. The progression from early cross-modal alignment methods [arxiv_2302.00389] to contemporary systems capable of nuanced visual dialogue and reasoning [arxiv_2306.14895] reflects both the cumulative nature of scientific progress and the catalytic role played by a small number of key innovations—particularly the transformer architecture, the pretraining-finetuning paradigm, and instruction tuning—in enabling rapid capability gains. At the same time, the persistent challenges identified across this literature, including data scarcity [arxiv_2307.04087], architectural trade-offs between NLP and visual capabilities [arxiv_2408.12902], the gap between high-level comprehension and fine-grained perception [arxiv_2406.15768], and the difficulty of comprehensive evaluation [arxiv_2408.08632], underscore that the field remains far from a settled state and that significant theoretical and empirical work remains to be done.

The challenge of enabling MLLMs to read and interpret text embedded within images represents a distinct and underappreciated dimension of multimodal capability. While much of the literature has focused on understanding natural scene content, a substantial portion of real-world images—including documents, infographics, street signs, and product labels—contain textual information that must be recognized and semantically integrated with visual context. [arxiv_2406.06730] addresses this gap directly through the introduction of TRINS, a text-rich image instruction dataset constructed atop LAION using hybrid data annotation strategies. The authors demonstrate that models trained on TRINS exhibit substantially improved reading comprehension within images, a capability that standard visual instruction tuning pipelines fail to cultivate adequately. This work highlights a systematic blind spot in the field: the conflation of visual understanding with scene-level understanding, to the neglect of the document-level and text-in-image understanding that many practical applications demand. The TRINS contribution thus complements broader dataset construction efforts such as SVIT [arxiv_2307.04087] by targeting a qualitatively distinct type of visual knowledge, suggesting that comprehensive multimodal capability requires not a single large dataset but a carefully curated portfolio of datasets targeting different visual competencies.

The question of how information flows between visual and linguistic representations within MLLMs has attracted growing mechanistic interest. While behavioral evaluations can characterize what models can and cannot do, they provide limited insight into why particular capabilities emerge or fail. [arxiv_2411.18620] takes a mechanistic approach to this question, examining the internal information flow between visual and linguistic tokens across the layers of autoregressive MLLMs. The study reveals that visual and linguistic information are not uniformly integrated throughout the network; rather, cross-modal interaction is concentrated at specific layers and token positions, with implications for how architectural choices—such as the placement of cross-attention mechanisms or adaptor modules—affect the quality of multimodal representations. This finding resonates with earlier interpretability work on vision-language transformers [arxiv_2203.17247], which similarly identified layer-specific patterns of cross-modal attention that are not apparent from behavioral evaluation alone. Together, these mechanistic investigations suggest that the internal geometry of multimodal representations is a productive object of study in its own right, and that architectural decisions currently made on largely empirical grounds may benefit from being informed by a more principled understanding of how information is routed and transformed within these models.

Interpretability concerns extend beyond mechanistic analysis to the broader question of whether MLLMs can explain their own reasoning in ways that are meaningful and verifiable. [arxiv_2412.02104] provides a comprehensive survey of explainability and interpretability methods as applied to MLLMs, cataloguing approaches that range from attention visualization and probing classifiers to post-hoc explanation generation and self-explanation through chain-of-thought prompting. The survey identifies a fundamental tension between the scale and opacity of contemporary MLLMs and the transparency requirements of high-stakes deployment contexts, arguing that explainability must be treated as a first-class design objective rather than an afterthought. This argument is particularly salient in light of the domain-specific applications surveyed elsewhere in the literature—such as semiconductor electron microscopy analysis [arxiv_2409.07463] and insect identification for precision agriculture [arxiv_2502.09906]—where the consequences of model errors are significant and where users require not merely correct outputs but interpretable justifications. The convergence of interpretability research with domain-specific MLLM deployment thus represents an important frontier, one that demands methodological innovation at the intersection of mechanistic analysis, human-computer interaction, and domain expertise.

The extension of MLLMs to specialized scientific and industrial domains raises additional questions about the adequacy of general-purpose pretraining for tasks that require domain-specific visual and conceptual knowledge. [arxiv_2409.07463] demonstrates that a teacher-student instruction tuning framework, in which a large general-purpose MLLM such as GPT-4 is used to generate instruction-following data for training smaller specialized models, can yield effective domain adaptation for semiconductor electron microscopy analysis. The resulting small-scale multimodal models achieve competitive performance on zero-shot visual question answering and classification tasks within this narrow domain, despite having far fewer parameters than their teacher models. This finding has important practical implications: it suggests that the knowledge encoded in large general-purpose MLLMs can be distilled into more computationally tractable models through carefully designed instruction data, a strategy that parallels the knowledge distillation approach formalized in [arxiv_2410.16236] for the LLaVA family of models. The LLaVA-KD framework introduced in [arxiv_2410.16236] proposes a two-stage distillation procedure—comprising multimodal distillation pretraining followed by task-specific fine-tuning—that enables small-scale MLLMs to approach the performance of their large-scale counterparts while remaining deployable in resource-constrained environments. Taken together, these contributions suggest that the dichotomy between large general-purpose models and small specialized models is not fixed but can be bridged through principled knowledge transfer, provided that the instruction data and distillation objectives are carefully aligned with the target domain and task.

The evaluation of multimodal in-context learning represents a further dimension of MLLM assessment that has received comparatively limited attention. While few-shot prompting has been extensively studied in the context of unimodal language models, its multimodal analogue—in which both visual and textual exemplars are provided in context—poses distinct challenges that standard benchmarks do not adequately capture. [arxiv_2403.13164] introduces VL-ICL Bench, a benchmark specifically designed to probe the in-context learning capabilities of vision-language models across a diverse range of tasks that go beyond the standard few-shot visual question answering and image captioning paradigms. The benchmark reveals that current MLLMs exhibit substantial variability in their ability to leverage multimodal in-context examples, with performance depending sensitively on the nature of the task, the format of the exemplars, and the degree of distribution shift between the in-context examples and the test instance. These findings complement the broader evaluation landscape surveyed in [arxiv_2408.08632] and [arxiv_2306.09265], adding granularity to the picture of MLLM capabilities by identifying in-context learning as a capability that is both practically important and currently underdeveloped. The implication for future research is that instruction tuning and pretraining objectives should be designed with in-context generalization explicitly in mind, rather than treating it as an emergent byproduct of scale.

The architectural diversity of contemporary MLLMs extends to the choice of generative paradigm itself. While the dominant approach to MLLM generation has been autoregressive, with models producing output tokens sequentially conditioned on all preceding tokens, alternative generative frameworks have begun to attract attention. [arxiv_2505.16933] introduces LLaDA-V, a multimodal large language model built upon a masked diffusion language model rather than an autoregressive backbone. By integrating a vision encoder and MLP connector with the LLaDA diffusion framework, the authors demonstrate that diffusion-based generation can be extended to multimodal instruction following with competitive performance relative to autoregressive baselines. This contribution is significant not merely as a technical novelty but as a conceptual challenge to the assumption that autoregressive generation is the uniquely appropriate paradigm for language and multimodal modeling. The exploration of diffusion-based and other non-autoregressive architectures for MLLMs opens new research directions concerning the trade-offs between generation quality, inference efficiency, and the capacity for iterative refinement that diffusion models naturally support. As the field continues to mature, the coexistence of multiple generative paradigms may prove productive, with different architectures exhibiting complementary strengths across different task types and deployment contexts.

Efficient adaptation of MLLMs to new tasks and modalities without full retraining remains a central practical concern, particularly as model sizes continue to grow and the cost of full fine-tuning becomes prohibitive. [arxiv_2408.06610] addresses this challenge through CROME, a cross-modal adaptor framework that enables efficient vision-language instruction tuning without requiring retraining of the underlying language model. The CROME framework introduces a gated cross-modal adaptor that selectively integrates visual features into the language model's processing stream, providing a parameter-efficient mechanism for task-specific adaptation that complements the zero-shot capabilities developed during pretraining. This approach is situated within a broader ecosystem of parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods that have been developed for unimodal language models—including adapter layers, prefix tuning, and low-rank adaptation—and extends their logic to the multimodal setting. The comparative analysis presented in [arxiv_2310.03211] provides useful context for evaluating such approaches, demonstrating that the choice of instruction tuning strategy has substantial effects on zero-shot generalization performance across multimodal tasks, and that no single approach dominates across all evaluation dimensions. The implication is that efficient adaptation methods must be evaluated not only on their computational savings but on their effects on the breadth and robustness of the resulting model's capabilities, a consideration that connects directly to the comprehensive evaluation frameworks advocated in [arxiv_2408.08632] and [arxiv_2306.09265].
