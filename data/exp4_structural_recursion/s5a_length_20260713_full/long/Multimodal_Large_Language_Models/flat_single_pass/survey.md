# Multimodal Large Language Models (long)

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together decades of progress in natural language processing, computer vision, and representation learning into unified architectures capable of reasoning across text, images, audio, and video. To appreciate the current state of the field, it is necessary to trace the intellectual lineage of these systems, understand the architectural innovations that have enabled them, examine the training paradigms and datasets that shape their capabilities, and critically assess the benchmarks and evaluation frameworks used to measure their performance. This review synthesizes the growing body of scholarship on MLLMs, drawing on a wide range of recent surveys, architectural proposals, and empirical studies to present a coherent account of the field's development, its principal achievements, and the challenges that remain unresolved.

The conceptual foundations of MLLMs lie in the broader project of vision-language pre-training (VLP), which sought to learn joint representations of images and text from large-scale paired data. Early VLP methods were designed with specific downstream tasks in mind, including image captioning, image-text retrieval, visual question answering (VQA), and visual grounding [arxiv_2210.09263]. These task-specific models demonstrated that pre-training on large corpora of image-text pairs could yield transferable representations, but they were limited in their generality: a model trained for retrieval was not necessarily well-suited for generation, and vice versa. The transition from specialist models to general-purpose assistants has been a defining theme of the field's evolution, as documented in comprehensive surveys of multimodal foundation models [arxiv_2309.10020]. This transition was catalyzed by the success of large language models (LLMs) such as GPT and BERT, which demonstrated that scale, combined with appropriate pre-training objectives, could produce models with remarkable zero-shot and few-shot generalization capabilities. The natural question that followed was whether similar principles could be extended to the multimodal domain.

Vision-language pre-training methods can be broadly categorized according to the tasks they target and the architectural choices they make [arxiv_2210.09263]. For image-text tasks, early approaches such as ViLBERT, UNITER, and OSCAR employed dual-stream or single-stream transformer architectures that fused visual features extracted from object detectors with token embeddings from text encoders. These models were pre-trained on objectives such as masked language modeling, image-text matching, and masked region prediction, and they achieved strong results on benchmarks like VQA, NLVR2, and Flickr30K retrieval. For core computer vision tasks, models like CLIP and ALIGN demonstrated that contrastive pre-training on hundreds of millions of noisy image-text pairs from the internet could produce visual representations that generalized remarkably well to open-set classification, object detection, and segmentation. For video-text tasks, analogous approaches extended the image-text paradigm to the temporal domain, enabling video captioning, video-text retrieval, and video question answering [arxiv_2210.09263]. The survey by Fang and colleagues provides a particularly useful taxonomy of these developments, situating them within a broader narrative of progress toward multimodal intelligence.

A complementary perspective is offered by the survey of multimodal representation learning by Jabeen and colleagues, which traces the evolution of deep learning architectures for handling textual, visual, and audio features [arxiv_2302.00389]. This work emphasizes the centrality of cross-modal interaction and complementary information fusion for achieving strong performance on tasks such as VQA, natural language for visual reasoning (NLVR), and vision-language retrieval. The authors document the progression from early fusion approaches, which concatenated features from different modalities before processing, to late fusion approaches, which processed modalities independently before combining their outputs, to the transformer-based architectures that have come to dominate the field. Transformer models, with their attention mechanisms capable of attending to arbitrary pairs of tokens regardless of modality, proved particularly well-suited to multimodal tasks, and their success has been a major driver of progress in the field.

The relationship between vision-language pre-training and lexical grounding has been a subject of theoretical interest. Linguistic representations derived from text alone have been criticized for their lack of grounding—that is, their failure to connect words to their meanings in the physical world. VLP models, trained jointly on text and image data, were proposed as a response to this criticism, on the grounds that exposure to visual information during training might enrich the semantic representations learned by the language component. However, empirical investigation has complicated this intuition. A study comparing the semantic representations learned by VLP models versus text-only pre-training found that multimodal models failed to significantly outperform their text-only counterparts on a suite of language-only analyses, including clustering, probing, and commonsense question answering [arxiv_2109.10246]. This finding suggests that the benefits of visual grounding for linguistic representations are not automatic, and that future work is needed to develop training objectives and architectures that more effectively integrate visual and linguistic information at the level of semantic representation.

The architecture of modern MLLMs typically involves three main components: a vision encoder, a language model, and a mechanism for connecting the two. The vision encoder is responsible for extracting visual features from images or video frames; the language model provides the generative and reasoning capabilities; and the connector bridges the representational gap between the two. Different approaches to each of these components have been explored, and the choices made have significant implications for model performance, efficiency, and flexibility [arxiv_2402.12451]. Vision encoders based on Vision Transformers (ViTs) have largely supplanted convolutional neural networks in this role, owing to their strong performance on large-scale pre-training tasks and their compatibility with the transformer-based language models they are paired with. The PaLI model, for instance, trained a large 4-billion parameter ViT (ViT-e) to serve as the vision encoder for a jointly-scaled multilingual language-image model, finding that joint scaling of the vision and language components was important for achieving strong performance across a wide range of tasks [arxiv_2209.06794]. PaLI generates text based on visual and textual inputs, enabling it to perform many vision, language, and multimodal tasks in many languages, and its development highlighted the importance of training large vision encoders rather than relying solely on scaling the language component.

The connector between vision encoder and language model has been implemented in various ways. Some approaches use a simple linear projection to map visual features into the token embedding space of the language model, while others employ more sophisticated cross-attention mechanisms or adapter modules. The LLaVA model, which represents a landmark in the field of visual instruction tuning, used a linear projection to connect a CLIP visual encoder to a Vicuna language model, and demonstrated that this simple architecture, when combined with high-quality instruction-following data, could achieve impressive multimodal chat abilities [arxiv_2304.08485]. The CROME framework proposed a gated cross-modal adapter that combines visual and textual representations prior to input into a frozen language model, enabling efficient cross-modal understanding with minimal trainable parameters [arxiv_2408.06610]. The Inner-Adaptor Architecture (IAA) took a different approach, incorporating multiple multimodal adaptors at varying depths within the language model to facilitate direct interaction with the inherently text-oriented transformer layers, thereby enabling the frozen language model to develop multimodal capabilities without sacrificing its natural language processing performance [arxiv_2408.08632]. These architectural variations reflect a broader tension in the field between the desire to leverage the capabilities of pre-trained language models and the need to adapt them for multimodal tasks.

The question of whether to freeze or fine-tune the language model during multimodal training is a recurring theme in the literature. Fine-tuning the language model on vision-language data can lead to improved visual understanding, but it often comes at the cost of degraded natural language processing capabilities, a phenomenon sometimes referred to as catastrophic forgetting [arxiv_2408.12902]. Freezing the language model avoids this problem but can limit the depth of visual understanding that the model achieves. The IAA approach addresses this dilemma by freezing the language model while using inner adaptors to enable multimodal interaction, achieving satisfactory outcomes without performance degradation on NLP tasks [arxiv_2408.12902]. Similarly, CROME's use of a frozen LLM with a lightweight cross-modal adapter enables efficient adaptation without the expense of full language model retraining [arxiv_2408.06610]. These approaches reflect a growing recognition that parameter efficiency is an important consideration in the development of MLLMs, particularly as model sizes continue to grow.

Knowledge distillation has emerged as another strategy for managing the computational demands of large MLLMs. The LLaVA-KD framework proposes transferring knowledge from large-scale MLLMs to smaller ones, using Multimodal Distillation (MDist) to transfer robust representations across both visual and linguistic modalities, and Relation Distillation (RDist) to transfer the teacher model's ability to capture visual token relationships [arxiv_2410.16236]. This three-stage training scheme is designed to fully exploit the potential of small-scale MLLMs, enabling them to achieve performance levels closer to their larger counterparts while remaining deployable in resource-constrained scenarios. The development of such distillation frameworks reflects a broader trend toward making MLLMs more accessible and practical for real-world applications.

Visual instruction tuning has been one of the most influential paradigms in the development of MLLMs. The key insight behind this approach is that large language models can be adapted for multimodal tasks by training them on instruction-following data that pairs images with natural language instructions and responses. The LLaVA model pioneered this approach, using language-only GPT-4 to generate multimodal instruction-following data from image captions and bounding box annotations, and demonstrating that instruction tuning on such data could produce a model with impressive multimodal chat abilities [arxiv_2304.08485]. This work established a template that has been widely followed and extended. The SVIT project scaled up visual instruction tuning by constructing a dataset of 4.2 million visual instruction tuning examples, including conversation QA pairs, complex reasoning QA pairs, referring QA pairs, and detailed image descriptions, generated by prompting GPT-4 with manual image annotations [arxiv_2307.04087]. The scale and diversity of this dataset enabled the exploration of the limits of multimodal capability in a way that smaller datasets could not.

The quality of instruction-following data is as important as its quantity, and several works have addressed the challenge of generating high-quality data for visual instruction tuning. The VIGC framework proposed using available MLLMs to generate instruction data for vision-language tasks, but noted that current MLLMs tend to produce inadequate responses and generate false information [arxiv_2308.12714]. To address this, VIGC introduced a Visual Instruction Generation and Correction mechanism that iteratively refines generated data, improving its quality and reducing hallucinations. This work highlights a fundamental challenge in the field: the models used to generate training data are themselves imperfect, and their imperfections can propagate into the training data and ultimately into the models trained on it. The teacher-student approach adopted in the semiconductor electron microscopy domain provides another illustration of this challenge, using GPT-4 to generate instruction-following data for zero-shot VQA and classification tasks, and then using this data to customize smaller multimodal models for specialized applications [arxiv_2409.07463]. This approach demonstrates the potential of knowledge transfer from large general-purpose models to smaller domain-specific ones, while also highlighting the importance of domain expertise in constructing effective training data.

Fine-grained visual understanding has been identified as a persistent weakness of many MLLMs. While these models excel at high-level image-text interpretation, they often struggle with tasks that require precise localization, region-level understanding, or fine-grained attribute recognition. The Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT) framework addressed this limitation by integrating an additional region-level vision encoder into the MLLM architecture, enabling more detailed comprehension of images [arxiv_2308.13437]. Multiple data generation strategies were designed to construct an image-region-language instruction dataset, supporting fine-grained alignment between vision modules and the language model. The Veagle model similarly sought to enhance multimodal capabilities by addressing limitations in accurately interpreting images and answering questions in real-world scenarios, proposing a novel approach to multimodal representation learning [arxiv_2403.08773]. The MR-MLLM framework took a complementary approach, proposing a shared query fusion mechanism to harmonize detailed visual inputs from vision models with the linguistic depth of language models, enabling mutual reinforcement between visual perception and multimodal comprehension [arxiv_2406.06730]. These works collectively reflect a recognition that the integration of coarse-grained and fine-grained visual understanding is essential for building truly capable MLLMs.

The ability to read and understand text embedded in images is another capability that has received increasing attention. Many real-world images contain text—signs, documents, labels, captions—and the ability to comprehend this text is essential for a wide range of practical applications. However, most visually-tuned MLLMs struggle with this task, primarily due to limitations in their training data. The TRINS dataset was introduced to address this gap, providing a large collection of text-rich images with captions and questions designed to challenge models' reading abilities [arxiv_2406.06730]. The dataset contains 39,153 text-rich images and 102,437 questions, with annotations that are significantly longer than those in related datasets, providing new challenges for multimodal models. The Language-vision Reading Assistant (LaRA) architecture was also introduced in this work, demonstrating that targeted training on text-rich image data can substantially improve models' ability to comprehend embedded text.

Beyond vision and language, a growing body of work has sought to extend MLLMs to additional modalities, including audio. The VALOR model represents a significant contribution in this direction, proposing a Vision-Audio-Language Omni-perception pretraining model that jointly models the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner [arxiv_2304.08345] [ss_478472]. VALOR consists of three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, and is pre-trained on two pretext tasks: Multimodal Grouping Alignment (MGA), which projects vision, language, and audio into a common space, and Multimodal Grouping Captioning (MGC), which learns to generate text conditioned on vision, audio, or both. The construction of a large-scale, high-quality vision-audio-language dataset to support this research represents an important contribution to the field, as the scarcity of such data has been a significant bottleneck. The tutorial by Liang and colleagues provides a broader overview of recent advancements in multimodal pretrained and large models capable of integrating and processing diverse data forms including text, images, audio, and video, covering the foundational concepts of multimodality, the evolution of multimodal research, and the key technical challenges addressed by these models [arxiv_2410.05608]. This work situates audio and video modalities within the broader landscape of multimodal AI, highlighting the challenges of integrating heterogeneous data types and the opportunities that arise from doing so.

The survey of multimodal foundation models by Li and colleagues provides a comprehensive taxonomy of the field, distinguishing between models pre-trained for specific purposes and those designed to serve as general-purpose assistants [arxiv_2309.10020]. The former category includes methods for learning vision backbones for visual understanding and text-to-image generation, while the latter encompasses unified vision models inspired by LLMs, end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs, and chaining multimodal tools with LLMs. This taxonomy is useful for understanding the different research trajectories that have contributed to the current state of the field, and for identifying the connections and tensions between them. The survey by Caffagni and colleagues offers a similarly comprehensive review of recent visual-based MLLMs, analyzing their architectural choices, multimodal alignment strategies, and training techniques, and conducting detailed analysis across a wide range of tasks including visual grounding, image generation and editing, visual understanding, and domain-specific applications [arxiv_2402.12451]. Together, these surveys provide a rich picture of the field's diversity and dynamism.

The survey of different LLM architectures by Shoaib and colleagues situates MLLMs within the broader landscape of large language model development, noting that MLLMs extend LLM capabilities to process and interpret multiple data modalities including images, audio, and video [arxiv_2412.03220]. This work documents the progression from text-only LLMs to multimodal systems, highlighting the architectural innovations that have enabled this transition and the benchmarks used to evaluate the resulting models. The emphasis on transformer-based architectures as the foundation for both LLMs and MLLMs underscores the centrality of the attention mechanism to modern AI systems, and the survey provides useful context for understanding how multimodal capabilities have been grafted onto existing language model architectures.

In-context learning (ICL) is one of the most celebrated capabilities of large language models, enabling rapid adaptation to new tasks using few-shot examples provided as a prompt without updating model weights. The extension of ICL to the multimodal domain has been an active area of research, but investigations have predominantly focused on few-shot VQA and image captioning, which neither fully exploit the strengths of ICL nor adequately test its limitations. The VL-ICL Bench was introduced to address this gap, providing a comprehensive benchmark for multimodal in-context learning that encompasses a broad spectrum of tasks involving both images and text as inputs and outputs [arxiv_2403.13164]. This work reveals that the broader capabilities and limitations of multimodal ICL remain under-explored, and that existing benchmarks may give an overly optimistic picture of models' ICL abilities. The benchmark's design, which includes tasks that challenge models in ways that go beyond standard VQA and captioning, provides a more rigorous test of multimodal ICL and highlights areas where further research is needed.

Visual question answering has served as a central benchmark task for MLLMs, and the survey by Guo and colleagues provides a comprehensive overview of the development of VQA and the latest models addressing it [arxiv_2411.17558]. This survey covers natural language understanding of images and text, knowledge reasoning based on image-question information, and the extraction and fusion of modal information with vision-language pre-training models and MLLMs. The survey also reviews progress in knowledge reasoning in VQA, detailing the extraction of internal knowledge and the introduction of external knowledge, and discusses evaluation datasets and metrics. The VQA task has evolved considerably since its introduction, from simple factual questions about image content to complex reasoning tasks that require multi-step inference, commonsense knowledge, and the integration of information from multiple sources. The survey by Guo and colleagues documents this evolution and situates current MLLMs within it, providing a useful reference for understanding the state of the art in VQA.

The survey of vision-language pre-training from the lens of multimodal machine translation by Sulubacak and colleagues highlights a somewhat neglected application of VLP models: the use of visual information to improve text-to-text translation [arxiv_2306.07198]. While models like CLIP have demonstrated improvements in downstream tasks like image captioning and VQA, comparatively little work has explored their use for multimodal machine translation, where the goal is to leverage image or video modality in text-to-text translation. This survey fills a gap in the literature by examining the landscape of language-and-vision pre-training from this perspective, and by identifying the challenges and opportunities that arise when visual information is used to disambiguate or enrich translation. The relative neglect of this application area, despite its practical importance, reflects the broader tendency of the field to focus on tasks where visual information is the primary input rather than an auxiliary signal.

The interpretability and explainability of MLLMs have emerged as important concerns as these models are deployed in high-stakes applications. The complexity and scale of MLLMs introduce significant challenges in understanding their internal mechanisms, and the opacity of these systems can undermine trust and reliability. The survey by Zhao and colleagues provides a comprehensive review of explainability and interpretability methods for MLLMs, covering techniques for understanding how these models process and integrate information from different modalities [arxiv_2412.02104]. This work situates the interpretability challenge within the broader context of AI transparency and accountability, and identifies the specific challenges that arise in the multimodal setting, where the interactions between visual and linguistic representations are particularly difficult to disentangle. The VL-InterpreT tool represents a practical contribution to this challenge, providing interactive visualizations for interpreting the attentions and hidden representations in multimodal transformers [arxiv_2203.17247]. VL-InterpreT tracks a variety of statistics in attention heads throughout all layers for both vision and language components, and visualizes cross-modal and intra-modal attention patterns, enabling researchers to gain insight into how these models process multimodal inputs. The development of such tools is essential for building more capable and trustworthy MLLMs, and for identifying failure modes and biases that might not be apparent from benchmark performance alone.

The evaluation of MLLMs has been a major focus of research, driven by the recognition that existing benchmarks may not adequately capture the full range of capabilities and limitations of these models. The LVLM-eHub benchmark provides a comprehensive evaluation of publicly available large multimodal models, assessing five categories of multimodal capabilities including visual question answering and object hallucination on 42 in-domain text-related visual benchmarks, as well as user-level evaluation through an online arena platform [arxiv_2306.09265] [ss_478371]. The benchmark includes 13 representative LVLMs such as InstructBLIP and LLaVA, and its findings reveal several important insights about the factors that influence LVLM performance, including model configurations and modality alignment strategies. The study found that instruction-tuned LVLMs with larger language models generally performed better, but that the relationship between model size and performance was not always straightforward, and that the quality of instruction-following data was a critical factor. The comparison between the earlier version of LVLM-eHub, which evaluated 8 models on 47 benchmarks [arxiv_2306.09265], and the later version, which expanded to 13 models and 42 benchmarks [ss_478371], illustrates the rapid pace of development in the field and the ongoing need for updated evaluation frameworks.

The survey of benchmarks for MLLMs by Li and colleagues provides a comprehensive review of 200 benchmarks and evaluations, organized around five dimensions: perception and understanding, cognition and reasoning, specific domains, key capabilities, and other modalities [arxiv_2408.08632]. This work argues that evaluation should be regarded as a crucial discipline to support the development of MLLMs, and identifies limitations of current evaluation methods including the tendency to focus on narrow task types, the use of metrics that may not align with human judgment, and the lack of benchmarks for emerging capabilities such as multimodal reasoning and cross-modal generation. The survey highlights the importance of developing evaluation frameworks that are comprehensive, rigorous, and aligned with the practical requirements of real-world applications.

The performance of multimodal language models across different tasks and architectures has been systematically analyzed in several comparative studies. The study by Caffagni and colleagues conducts a comparative analysis of different multimodal instruction tuning approaches, evaluating their performance across complex reasoning, conversation, image captioning, multiple-choice questions, and binary classification [arxiv_2310.03211]. Through rigorous benchmarking and ablation experiments, this work reveals key insights for guiding architectural choices when incorporating multimodal capabilities into LLMs, and identifies limitations of current approaches that point toward directions for future research. The finding that current approaches have significant limitations in certain task types underscores the importance of comprehensive evaluation and the need for continued architectural innovation.

Domain-specific applications of MLLMs have attracted considerable interest, as the general-purpose capabilities of these models can be leveraged for specialized tasks with appropriate adaptation. The VQA-IN framework developed a method to transform domain-specific visual and vision-language datasets into a unified question answering format, extending MLLMs to domain-specific tasks such as medical image analysis and remote sensing [arxiv_2402.08360]. The framework was applied to train multiple MLLM architectures using smaller versions of LLMs, achieving high performance on domain-specific visual tasks while maintaining general capabilities. The semiconductor electron microscopy application similarly demonstrated the potential of instruction-tuned MLLMs for specialized industrial applications, using a teacher-student approach to transfer knowledge from large general-purpose models to smaller domain-specific ones [arxiv_2409.07463]. These works collectively illustrate the versatility of the MLLM paradigm and its potential for impact across a wide range of domains.

The tutorial notes from the CVPR 2023 tutorial on large multimodal models provide a useful synthesis of the state of the field at a pivotal moment in its development, summarizing the background on GPT-like large models for vision-and-language modeling, the basics of instruction tuning in LLMs and its extension to the multimodal space, and the practical steps involved in building multimodal GPT-4-like models with open-source resources [arxiv_2306.14895]. This work captures the excitement and momentum of the field at a time when the first generation of instruction-tuned MLLMs was emerging, and provides a useful reference for understanding the conceptual foundations of the paradigm.

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this body of literature. First, the importance of scale—in terms of model size, training data, and computational resources—is a recurring finding. Models trained on larger datasets with more parameters consistently achieve better performance on standard benchmarks, although the relationship between scale and capability is not always linear, and there are important qualitative differences between models that cannot be captured by scale alone. Second, the quality of training data, particularly instruction-following data, is a critical determinant of model performance. The generation of high-quality instruction-following data remains a challenge, and the use of large language models or MLLMs to generate such data introduces the risk of propagating errors and biases. Third, the alignment between visual and linguistic representations is a fundamental challenge that has been addressed through a variety of architectural and training innovations, but remains an open problem. Fourth, the evaluation of MLLMs is a complex and multifaceted challenge, and existing benchmarks may not adequately capture the full range of capabilities and limitations of these models. Fifth, the extension of MLLMs to additional modalities beyond vision and language, including audio and video, opens up new possibilities but also introduces new challenges in terms of data collection, model architecture, and evaluation.

The field of multimodal large language models is characterized by rapid progress and significant diversity of approaches. From the early VLP models designed for specific tasks to the general-purpose instruction-tuned assistants of today, the trajectory of the field reflects a consistent drive toward greater generality, capability, and efficiency. The architectural innovations documented in this review—from cross-modal adapters and inner adaptors to knowledge distillation frameworks and mutual reinforcement mechanisms—reflect the ingenuity of the research community in addressing the fundamental challenges of multimodal AI. At the same time, the limitations identified in this literature—including the difficulty of fine-grained visual understanding, the challenge of reading text in images, the opacity of internal mechanisms, and the inadequacy of existing evaluation frameworks—point toward the substantial work that remains to be done. The convergence of vision, language, audio, and other modalities in a single unified model represents a long-standing goal of AI research, and the progress documented in this review suggests that this goal is becoming increasingly attainable, even as new challenges emerge at each step of the way.

The preceding synthesis establishes the broad contours of multimodal large language model research, yet several important threads merit deeper elaboration. The following paragraphs extend the review by examining architectural diversity, training methodology, in-context learning, interpretability, domain adaptation, omni-modal extensions, and the emerging generation of diffusion-based multimodal models, drawing on recent scholarship to provide a more complete picture of the field's current state and open problems.

**Architectural Diversity and the Connector Design Problem**

One of the most consequential design decisions in building MLLMs concerns the mechanism by which visual representations are connected to the language backbone. Early approaches relied on relatively shallow projection layers or cross-attention bridges, but subsequent work has demonstrated that the design of this connector has a profound effect on both alignment quality and downstream task performance. The CROME framework addresses this challenge by proposing cross-modal adapters that enable efficient vision-language instruction tuning without requiring expensive retraining of the language model itself [arxiv_2408.06610]. By inserting lightweight adapter modules at strategic points in the network, CROME achieves competitive zero-shot and task-specific performance while substantially reducing the computational cost of adaptation. This work highlights a broader tension in the field between the desire for deep cross-modal fusion, which typically requires modifying the language backbone, and the practical need for parameter efficiency, which favors frozen or lightly adapted language models.

The Inner-Adaptor Architecture (IAA) takes a complementary approach to this problem, inserting multiple adaptor modules directly within the frozen language model rather than relying on a single external bridge [arxiv_2408.12902]. This design allows visual information to interact with linguistic representations at multiple levels of abstraction, enabling richer cross-modal understanding without degrading the natural language processing capabilities that the language model acquired during pretraining. The authors demonstrate that freezing the language model while developing multimodal competencies through inner adaptors avoids the performance degradation on NLP tasks that is commonly observed when the language model is fine-tuned on vision-language data. This finding has important implications for the practical deployment of MLLMs in applications that require both strong language understanding and robust visual reasoning, as it suggests that the two objectives need not be in tension if the architecture is designed appropriately.

The Veagle model further advances multimodal representation learning by integrating dynamic high-resolution image processing with a flexible connector architecture, enabling the model to handle images at varying resolutions without sacrificing alignment quality [arxiv_2403.08773]. The authors argue that existing approaches are limited by their reliance on fixed-resolution visual encoders, which can lose important fine-grained details when processing high-resolution images. By dynamically adjusting the resolution of the visual input and using a connector that can accommodate variable-length visual token sequences, Veagle achieves improved performance on tasks that require detailed visual understanding, such as visual grounding and fine-grained visual question answering. This work underscores the importance of resolution-aware design in multimodal architectures and points toward the need for more flexible and adaptive visual encoding strategies.

The InfMLLM framework takes a unified approach to visual-language tasks, implementing a three-stage training strategy that progressively aligns visual and linguistic representations before fine-tuning on task-specific instruction data [arxiv_2311.06791]. This staged training approach reflects a broader trend in the field toward curriculum-based training strategies that first establish coarse-grained alignment before refining it through more targeted supervision. The authors demonstrate that this approach enables the model to handle a diverse range of tasks, including image captioning, visual question answering, and visual grounding, within a single unified framework. The success of this approach suggests that the apparent diversity of vision-language tasks may mask a common underlying structure that can be captured through appropriate training strategies.

**Instruction Tuning: Data Quality, Scale, and Generation Strategies**

The quality and scale of instruction-following data have emerged as critical determinants of MLLM performance, and a substantial body of work has been devoted to developing better methods for generating and curating such data. The LLaVA framework, which introduced the use of language-only GPT-4 to generate multimodal instruction-following data, established a paradigm that has been widely adopted and extended [arxiv_2304.08485]. By prompting GPT-4 with image captions and bounding box annotations, LLaVA generates diverse and contextually rich instruction-following examples that enable the model to engage in open-ended visual conversations. The success of this approach demonstrated that high-quality instruction-following data could be generated at scale without requiring expensive human annotation, opening the door to rapid progress in the field.

Building on this foundation, the SVIT framework scales up visual instruction tuning by constructing a dataset of 3.2 million instruction-following examples, substantially larger than those used in prior work [arxiv_2307.04087]. The authors demonstrate that scaling up the instruction-following dataset leads to consistent improvements in model performance across a range of benchmarks, suggesting that data scale is an important lever for improving MLLM capabilities. However, the authors also note that the quality of the generated data is as important as its quantity, and that naive scaling without attention to data quality can lead to diminishing returns. This observation motivates the development of more sophisticated data generation and curation strategies that balance scale with quality.

The VIGC framework addresses the quality problem directly by proposing a visual instruction generation and correction mechanism that uses a multimodal model to iteratively generate and refine instruction-following data [arxiv_2308.12714]. Unlike approaches that rely on language-only models to generate instruction data from pre-annotated captions, VIGC uses a multimodal model that can directly perceive the image content, enabling it to generate more accurate and contextually appropriate instructions. The correction mechanism further improves data quality by identifying and correcting errors in the generated instructions, resulting in a dataset that is both more accurate and more diverse than those produced by prior methods. This work represents an important step toward closing the loop between data generation and model training, and suggests that iterative self-improvement may be a viable strategy for enhancing MLLM capabilities.

The Position-enhanced Visual Instruction Tuning (PVIT) framework extends the standard instruction tuning paradigm by incorporating fine-grained positional information into the training data, enabling the model to develop more precise spatial understanding [arxiv_2308.13437]. By augmenting instruction-following examples with region-level annotations that specify the spatial locations of objects and attributes mentioned in the instructions, PVIT enables the model to develop a more detailed understanding of the spatial relationships between visual elements. The authors demonstrate that this approach leads to significant improvements on tasks that require fine-grained spatial reasoning, such as visual grounding and referring expression comprehension, while maintaining competitive performance on standard VQA benchmarks. This work highlights the importance of fine-grained supervision in developing spatially aware MLLMs and points toward the need for richer annotation schemes in instruction-following datasets.

The TRINS dataset and associated training framework address a specific but important limitation of existing MLLMs: their difficulty in comprehending textual content embedded within images [arxiv_2406.06730]. Despite the prevalence of text-rich images in real-world applications—including documents, signs, charts, and infographics—most MLLMs struggle to accurately read and reason about such content, primarily due to the lack of appropriate training data. TRINS addresses this gap by constructing a large-scale text-rich image instruction dataset using hybrid annotation strategies that combine automatic text extraction with human verification. The authors demonstrate that training on TRINS leads to substantial improvements in the ability of MLLMs to comprehend and reason about text-rich images, while maintaining strong performance on standard visual understanding tasks. This work underscores the importance of targeted data collection strategies for addressing specific capability gaps in MLLMs.

**In-Context Learning and Few-Shot Generalization**

The ability to perform in-context learning—adapting to new tasks from a small number of examples provided in the prompt without updating model weights—is one of the most remarkable emergent capabilities of large language models, and its extension to the multimodal domain has attracted considerable research attention. The VL-ICL Bench provides a systematic evaluation of multimodal in-context learning across a diverse range of tasks that go beyond the standard few-shot VQA and image captioning settings that have dominated prior work [arxiv_2403.13164]. The authors identify several important dimensions of multimodal ICL that have been underexplored, including the ability to learn from examples that involve complex visual reasoning, cross-modal analogical reasoning, and tasks that require integrating information from multiple images. Their evaluation reveals significant gaps between the in-context learning capabilities of current MLLMs and those of large language models on analogous text-only tasks, suggesting that multimodal ICL remains a challenging and largely unsolved problem.

The analysis in VL-ICL Bench also highlights the importance of the details of the in-context examples—including the choice of examples, their ordering, and the format in which they are presented—in determining the effectiveness of multimodal ICL. These findings echo similar observations in the text-only ICL literature and suggest that the development of principled strategies for selecting and formatting in-context examples is an important direction for future research. The authors also note that the performance of MLLMs on multimodal ICL tasks is highly sensitive to the specific formulation of the task, with small changes in the prompt leading to large changes in performance. This sensitivity to prompt formulation suggests that current MLLMs may not have developed robust representations of the underlying task structure, and that further work is needed to develop models that are more robust to variations in task presentation.

**Omni-Modal Extensions: Audio, Video, and Beyond**

While the majority of MLLM research has focused on the integration of vision and language, a growing body of work has explored the extension of multimodal models to additional modalities, including audio, video, and sensor data. The VALOR framework represents an early and influential effort in this direction, proposing a vision-audio-language omni-perception pretraining model that jointly models the relationships among vision, audio, and language in an end-to-end manner [arxiv_2304.08345]. VALOR uses three separate encoders for single-modality representations and a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, and is pretrained on a large-scale dataset of video-audio-text triplets. The authors demonstrate that joint modeling of all three modalities leads to better performance on tasks that require integrating information from multiple modalities, such as audio-visual question answering and video captioning, compared to models that are pretrained on only two modalities. This finding suggests that the relationships among different modalities are mutually reinforcing, and that joint modeling of multiple modalities can lead to richer and more robust representations.

The tutorial on multimodal large language models and tunings provides a comprehensive overview of the state of the art in multimodal pretraining and large models, covering not only vision and language but also audio, video, and sensor modalities [arxiv_2410.05608]. The tutorial highlights the key technical challenges involved in extending MLLMs to additional modalities, including the need for modality-specific encoders, the challenge of aligning representations across modalities with very different statistical properties, and the difficulty of collecting large-scale multimodal datasets that cover all relevant modalities. The tutorial also discusses the latest multimodal datasets and pretrained models, providing a useful reference for researchers entering the field. The breadth of modalities covered in this tutorial reflects the growing ambition of the MLLM research community to develop truly general-purpose multimodal assistants that can perceive and reason about the full richness of human experience.

The survey on multimodal large language models and tunings further contextualizes these developments within the broader landscape of multimodal AI, examining how the integration of diverse data forms—text, images, audio, and video—enables more capable and versatile AI systems [arxiv_2411.06284]. The authors provide a detailed analysis of the architectural components and training methods used in state-of-the-art MLLMs, and discuss the key challenges and open problems in the field. Their survey highlights the importance of developing unified architectures that can handle multiple modalities without requiring separate models for each modality, and identifies the development of large-scale multimodal datasets as a critical bottleneck for further progress. The authors also discuss the potential societal impacts of MLLMs, including both the benefits of more capable AI assistants and the risks associated with the misuse of these technologies.

**Interpretability and the Inner Workings of MLLMs**

Despite the impressive capabilities of MLLMs, their internal mechanisms remain largely opaque, and the development of interpretability tools and methods for understanding how these models process and integrate multimodal information is an important and underexplored research direction. The VL-InterpreT tool represents an early effort to address this gap, providing an interactive visualization framework for interpreting the internal representations and attention patterns of vision-language transformers [arxiv_2203.17247]. By visualizing the attention weights and token representations at different layers of the model, VL-InterpreT enables researchers to gain insights into how the model integrates visual and linguistic information, and to identify potential failure modes and biases. The authors demonstrate the utility of their tool through a series of case studies that reveal interesting patterns in how vision-language transformers process multimodal inputs, including the tendency for certain attention heads to specialize in cross-modal alignment.

More recent work has extended the interpretability agenda to the study of information flow in autoregressive MLLMs, examining how visual and linguistic information interact within these models at a mechanistic level [arxiv_2411.18620]. This study investigates the cross-modal information flow between different components of the model, revealing that visual information is progressively transformed and integrated with linguistic representations as it propagates through the network. The authors identify specific layers and attention heads that play a critical role in cross-modal integration, and demonstrate that the patterns of information flow are consistent across different model architectures and tasks. These findings provide important insights into the internal mechanisms of MLLMs and suggest that targeted interventions at specific points in the network may be an effective strategy for improving cross-modal alignment.

The comprehensive survey on explainable and interpretable MLLMs provides a broader perspective on the interpretability challenge, reviewing the full range of methods that have been proposed for understanding and explaining the behavior of MLLMs [arxiv_2412.02104]. The authors organize existing methods into several categories, including attention-based methods, gradient-based methods, probing methods, and example-based methods, and discuss the strengths and limitations of each approach. They also identify several important open problems in MLLM interpretability, including the need for methods that can explain the model's behavior at the level of individual predictions rather than aggregate statistics, and the challenge of developing interpretability methods that are robust to the specific architectural choices made in different models. The survey highlights the importance of interpretability not only as a scientific goal but also as a practical requirement for the safe and responsible deployment of MLLMs in high-stakes applications.

**Domain Adaptation and Specialized Applications**

The application of MLLMs to specialized domains has emerged as an important research direction, driven by the recognition that general-purpose models may not have sufficient domain-specific knowledge to perform well on specialized tasks without targeted adaptation. The Insect-Foundation model exemplifies this trend, proposing a foundation model and large multimodal dataset specifically designed for vision-language understanding of insects [arxiv_2502.09906]. The authors argue that existing conversational models lack sufficient knowledge about visual insects because they are trained primarily on general-domain data, and that this gap limits their utility for applications in precision agriculture and biodiversity monitoring. By constructing a large-scale dataset of insect images paired with detailed textual descriptions and developing a foundation model pretrained on this dataset, the authors demonstrate that domain-specific pretraining can substantially improve performance on insect-related vision-language tasks while maintaining competitive performance on general benchmarks.

The MR-MLLM framework addresses the challenge of integrating high-level multimodal comprehension with fine-grained visual perception, proposing a mutual reinforcement mechanism that enables the two capabilities to improve each other iteratively [arxiv_2406.15768]. The authors observe that existing MLLMs tend to excel at high-level image-text interpretation but struggle with fine-grained visual understanding tasks such as object detection and segmentation, while visual perception models excel at these tasks but lack the open-world generalization capabilities of MLLMs. By developing a framework that enables the two types of models to reinforce each other through iterative training, MR-MLLM achieves improved performance on both high-level and fine-grained tasks. This work represents an important step toward developing MLLMs that can seamlessly integrate high-level reasoning with detailed visual perception, a capability that is essential for many real-world applications.

The reformulation of vision-language foundation models toward universal multimodal assistants, as explored in the Uni-MoE and related frameworks, reflects a broader trend toward developing models that can handle a wide range of tasks and modalities within a single unified architecture [arxiv_2310.00653]. The authors argue that the capabilities of MLLMs depend critically on both the model architecture and the quality of the multimodal instruction-tuning datasets, and propose improvements to both components. Their work demonstrates that carefully designed instruction-tuning datasets that cover a diverse range of tasks and modalities can substantially improve the generality and robustness of MLLMs, and that architectural innovations that facilitate better feature alignment between visual and linguistic modules can further enhance performance. This work underscores the importance of co-designing model architectures and training datasets to achieve the best possible performance on diverse multimodal tasks.

**Knowledge Distillation and Efficient Deployment**

The computational demands of large-scale MLLMs present significant challenges for their deployment in resource-constrained environments, and the development of efficient methods for compressing and distilling these models has become an important research priority. The LLaVA-KD framework addresses this challenge by proposing a novel knowledge distillation approach specifically designed for MLLMs, enabling the transfer of knowledge from large-scale MLLMs to smaller, more efficient models [arxiv_2410.16236]. The authors demonstrate that their distillation framework substantially improves the performance of small-scale MLLMs compared to standard training approaches, closing a significant portion of the performance gap between small and large models. The framework incorporates both feature-level and output-level distillation objectives, enabling the student model to learn not only the final predictions of the teacher model but also the intermediate representations that underlie those predictions.

The broader challenge of balancing model capability with computational efficiency is also addressed in the CROME framework, which demonstrates that cross-modal adapters can achieve competitive performance with a fraction of the computational cost of full model fine-tuning [arxiv_2408.06610]. The authors provide a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between different efficiency strategies, including adapter-based methods, prompt tuning, and low-rank approximation, and demonstrate that their approach achieves the best balance between performance and efficiency across a range of tasks and model sizes. These findings have important implications for the practical deployment of MLLMs in applications where computational resources are limited, such as mobile devices and edge computing environments.

**Benchmarking, Evaluation, and the Measurement of Progress**

The development of comprehensive and rigorous evaluation frameworks is essential for measuring progress in the field and identifying the most important directions for future research. The LVLM-eHub benchmark provides a holistic evaluation of publicly available large multimodal models across a wide range of capability dimensions, including visual perception, knowledge-based question answering, reasoning, and commonsense understanding [arxiv_2306.09265]. By evaluating a diverse set of models on a common set of benchmarks, LVLM-eHub enables systematic comparisons across different architectures and training strategies, and identifies the strengths and weaknesses of each approach. The benchmark also includes an online arena component that enables human evaluation of model outputs, providing a more nuanced assessment of model quality than automated metrics alone.

The SEED-Bench-2 benchmark extends the evaluation agenda to cover the full range of capabilities of next-generation MLLMs, including not only comprehension of single image-text inputs but also generation of images and videos in response to multimodal prompts [arxiv_2311.17092]. The authors argue that existing benchmarks are inadequate for evaluating the capabilities of the latest generation of MLLMs, which can generate not only text but also images and videos, and propose a comprehensive benchmark that covers both comprehension and generation across multiple modalities. Their evaluation reveals significant differences in the capabilities of different models across different task types, highlighting the importance of comprehensive evaluation for understanding the true capabilities and limitations of MLLMs. The development of such benchmarks is essential for guiding the field toward the development of models that are genuinely capable across the full range of multimodal tasks.

**Diffusion-Based Multimodal Models and Emerging Paradigms**

A particularly noteworthy recent development is the emergence of diffusion-based approaches to multimodal language modeling, which represent a significant departure from the autoregressive paradigms that have dominated the field. The LLaDA-V framework introduces a purely diffusion-based MLLM that integrates visual instruction tuning with masked diffusion models, demonstrating that the autoregressive assumption is not a necessary condition for effective multimodal language modeling [arxiv_2505.16933]. Built upon the LLaDA large language diffusion model, LLaDA-V incorporates a vision encoder and MLP connector that projects visual features into the language embedding space, enabling effective multimodal alignment within a non-autoregressive framework. The authors demonstrate that LLaDA-V achieves competitive performance with autoregressive MLLMs on standard benchmarks, suggesting that diffusion-based approaches may offer a viable alternative to autoregressive models for multimodal tasks.

The significance of LLaDA-V extends beyond its empirical results, as it challenges the prevailing assumption that autoregressive generation is the natural paradigm for multimodal language modeling. Diffusion-based models offer several potential advantages over autoregressive models, including the ability to generate outputs in a non-sequential manner, which may be better suited to tasks that require holistic reasoning about the entire output sequence. However, diffusion-based models also face significant challenges, including the need for new training objectives and inference procedures that are adapted to the multimodal setting. The development of LLaDA-V represents an important proof of concept for diffusion-based multimodal modeling, and opens up a new research direction that may yield important insights into the fundamental principles of multimodal AI.

**Synthesis and Forward-Looking Perspectives**

Taken together, the works reviewed in these additional paragraphs paint a picture of a field that is simultaneously maturing and expanding. The maturation is evident in the increasing sophistication of architectural designs, training strategies, and evaluation frameworks, which reflect a deepening understanding of the fundamental challenges of multimodal AI. The expansion is evident in the growing diversity of modalities, domains, and applications being addressed, which reflects the increasing ambition of the research community to develop truly general-purpose multimodal assistants. The convergence of these two trends—maturation and expansion—creates both opportunities and challenges for the field. On the one hand, the accumulated knowledge and infrastructure of the field provide a strong foundation for tackling new challenges. On the other hand, the rapid pace of progress makes it difficult to maintain a coherent and comprehensive understanding of the state of the art, and the diversity of approaches makes it challenging to identify the most promising directions for future research.

Several important open problems emerge from this synthesis. First, the challenge of fine-grained visual understanding—including the ability to read text in images, reason about spatial relationships, and perceive fine-grained visual details—remains a significant limitation of current MLLMs, despite the progress documented in works such as TRINS [arxiv_2406.06730], PVIT [arxiv_2308.13437], and Veagle [arxiv_2403.08773]. Addressing this challenge will likely require both architectural innovations and the development of richer and more diverse training datasets. Second, the interpretability of MLLMs remains a critical open problem, with important implications for both scientific understanding and practical deployment. The works reviewed here, including VL-InterpreT [arxiv_2203.17247] and the cross-modal information flow study [arxiv_2411.18620], represent important steps toward addressing this challenge, but much work remains to be done. Third, the extension of MLLMs to additional modalities beyond vision and language, as exemplified by VALOR [arxiv_2304.08345] and the multimodal tuning tutorial [arxiv_2410.05608], opens up new possibilities but also introduces new challenges that will require sustained research effort to address. Finally, the emergence of diffusion-based multimodal models [arxiv_2505.16933] suggests that the field may be on the cusp of a new paradigm shift, and that the architectural assumptions that have guided the field thus far may need to be revisited. These open problems collectively define the frontier of multimodal large language model research and point toward the most important directions for future work.
