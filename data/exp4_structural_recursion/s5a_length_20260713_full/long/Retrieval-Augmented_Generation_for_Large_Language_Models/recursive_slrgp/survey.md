# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of RAG — evidence cluster A

The emergence of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) as a paradigm for enhancing large language models (LLMs) has prompted a substantial body of survey literature aimed at systematizing its principles, components, and applications. Across these works, a consistent motivation recurs: despite their remarkable capabilities in language understanding and generation, LLMs are fundamentally constrained by the static nature of their parametric knowledge, their susceptibility to hallucination, and their limited capacity to incorporate domain-specific or temporally current information. RAG addresses these limitations by coupling the generative capacity of LLMs with dynamic retrieval from external knowledge bases, and the growing complexity of this paradigm has necessitated increasingly sophisticated taxonomic and analytical frameworks.

The foundational challenge motivating RAG research is well-articulated across the surveyed literature. Large language models store knowledge implicitly within their parameters, and while this enables impressive zero-shot and few-shot performance, it introduces several critical failure modes. As noted in multiple comprehensive reviews, LLMs suffer from hallucination problems, knowledge update issues, and a lack of domain-specific expertise that cannot be remedied through parameter scaling alone [arxiv_2407.13193]. Similarly, the inherent limitations of hallucination and out-of-date internal knowledge are identified as core drivers for the development of retrieval-augmented approaches that harness external and authoritative knowledge bases rather than relying solely on the model's internal representations [arxiv_2405.06211]. This shared diagnosis across survey works underscores a consensus in the field that parametric knowledge alone is insufficient for reliable, knowledge-intensive generation tasks.

The structural organization of RAG systems has been approached from several complementary angles in the literature. One influential framework decomposes the RAG pipeline into four sequential categories: pre-retrieval, retrieval, post-retrieval, and generation, offering a detailed perspective centered on the retrieval viewpoint and tracing the evolution of significant methodological contributions [arxiv_2404.10981]. This retrieval-centric decomposition is particularly valuable for understanding how query formulation, document indexing, and result reranking interact to shape the quality of information passed to the generative component. A related but distinct organizational scheme is offered by the tripartite framework that scrutinizes retrieval, generation, and augmentation as the foundational pillars of RAG systems, situating these within a developmental trajectory that encompasses Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG paradigms [arxiv_2312.10997]. The progression from naive to modular configurations reflects the field's movement toward more flexible, composable architectures capable of addressing the shortcomings of earlier, more rigid pipeline designs.

A particularly notable taxonomic contribution in the recent survey literature concerns the classification of retrieval fusion strategies. Rather than treating the integration of retrieved content as a monolithic operation, one survey introduces a novel taxonomy distinguishing among query-based, logits-based, latent, and parametric fusion approaches, providing structured comparisons across dimensions of accessibility, efficiency, and use cases [arxiv_2407.13193]. This granular treatment of fusion mechanisms represents a meaningful advance over earlier surveys that tended to treat the augmentation step as relatively undifferentiated. By contrast, the survey framing RAG within the broader context of AI-Generated Content emphasizes the role of retrieval in enabling generative models to produce high-quality outputs by supplying the latest and most helpful auxiliary information, situating RAG within the wider ecosystem of generative AI rather than focusing narrowly on pipeline mechanics [arxiv_2405.06211].

The question of what RAG enables in practical terms is addressed across all four surveys, though with varying emphases. The enhancement of accuracy and credibility in knowledge-intensive tasks is consistently highlighted, as is the capacity for continuous knowledge updates that circumvent the need for costly model retraining [arxiv_2312.10997]. The cost-effectiveness of RAG as an alternative to generating plausible but potentially incorrect responses is also foregrounded, with the methodology described as providing a practical solution to the reliability limitations of purely parametric generation through the dynamic integration of real-world data [arxiv_2404.10981]. These practical motivations are complemented by attention to the theoretical underpinnings of how retrieved knowledge is merged with the model's intrinsic knowledge, with one survey characterizing RAG as synergistically combining LLMs' internal representations with vast, dynamic external repositories [arxiv_2312.10997].

Evaluation methodology and application breadth represent additional dimensions along which the surveys can be compared. The survey focused on NLP tasks explicitly addresses evaluation methods alongside its taxonomic contributions, reflecting a recognition that the field requires standardized benchmarks and assessment protocols commensurate with the diversity of RAG applications [arxiv_2407.13193]. The survey on retrieval-augmented LLMs similarly attends to the breadth of tasks for which RAG provides convenience, situating the paradigm within the expansive landscape of AIGC applications [arxiv_2405.06211]. Meanwhile, the retrieval-viewpoint survey traces the field's progression through analysis of significant studies, offering a historically grounded perspective on how methodological innovations have accumulated over time [arxiv_2404.10981].

Taken together, these survey works establish a rich and increasingly differentiated intellectual landscape for RAG research. They converge on a shared understanding of the core limitations that RAG addresses while diverging productively in their organizational frameworks, the granularity of their component analyses, and their emphases on evaluation, application, and architectural evolution. The movement from early naive pipeline designs toward modular, fusion-aware architectures reflects a maturing field grappling with the complexity of integrating retrieval and generation in ways that are simultaneously accurate, efficient, and adaptable to diverse knowledge-intensive tasks [arxiv_2312.10997][arxiv_2404.10981][arxiv_2407.13193][arxiv_2405.06211].

### Surveys and Overviews of RAG — evidence cluster B

The integration of retrieval mechanisms into large language model (LLM) pipelines has emerged as one of the most consequential developments in natural language processing research, offering a principled means of grounding generative outputs in verifiable external knowledge. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has attracted substantial scholarly and industrial attention precisely because it addresses one of the most persistent failure modes of LLMs: the tendency to produce fluent but factually unsupported content, commonly referred to as hallucination [arxiv_2410.20598]. By coupling a generative model with an information retrieval component, RAG frameworks enable the model to condition its outputs on dynamically retrieved documents rather than relying exclusively on parametric knowledge encoded during pretraining. This architectural choice has proven consequential across a wide range of downstream applications, from open-domain question answering to domain-specific knowledge-intensive tasks, and has spurred a growing body of research aimed at understanding, evaluating, and refining the interplay between retrieval and generation [arxiv_2309.01431].

Despite the evident promise of RAG as a paradigm, the field has increasingly recognized that the mere combination of a retriever and a generator does not guarantee reliable or high-quality outputs. As the application of RAG has broadened, a corresponding set of limitations and failure modes has come into sharper focus, motivating deeper investigation into the foundational principles that govern effective retrieval-augmented systems [arxiv_2410.20598]. A central concern is whether LLMs can consistently exploit retrieved information in a manner that is robust, accurate, and contextually appropriate. Addressing this concern requires not only architectural innovation but also rigorous empirical evaluation frameworks capable of isolating the specific capabilities and bottlenecks that determine RAG performance across different model families and task configurations.

Toward this evaluative goal, systematic benchmarking efforts have sought to characterize how different LLMs behave when augmented with retrieved context. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation Benchmark (RGB) represents a notable contribution in this direction, providing a structured corpus for evaluating RAG performance in both English and Chinese across four fundamental capabilities: noise robustness, negative rejection, information integration, and counterfactual robustness [arxiv_2309.01431]. This decomposition is analytically valuable because it moves beyond aggregate performance metrics to interrogate the specific cognitive demands that RAG places on a language model. Noise robustness, for instance, tests whether a model can identify and utilize relevant information in the presence of distracting or irrelevant retrieved passages, while negative rejection assesses whether the model appropriately declines to answer when retrieved documents do not contain the necessary information. Information integration examines the capacity to synthesize evidence across multiple retrieved passages, and counterfactual robustness probes whether the model can resist being misled by retrieved content that contradicts its parametric knowledge [arxiv_2309.01431]. Together, these dimensions reveal that the challenge of RAG is not simply one of retrieval quality but also one of how effectively the generative model can reason over and adjudicate among retrieved inputs.

A complementary line of research has focused on improving the retrieval component itself, recognizing that the quality of generation is fundamentally constrained by the quality of the documents that are retrieved. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), which represents queries and documents as dense vectors in a shared embedding space, has become a widely adopted approach for fetching semantically relevant documents in response to user queries [arxiv_2405.13008]. However, standard DPR models have been found to exhibit limitations in retrieval accuracy, particularly in domain-specific settings where the distribution of queries and documents may diverge from the general-domain data on which the retriever was trained. One proposed solution involves the incorporation of control tokens into the DPR framework, a modification that has been shown to yield a 13% improvement in Top-1 accuracy and a 4% improvement in Top-20 accuracy relative to the baseline DPR model [arxiv_2405.13008]. This result underscores the sensitivity of downstream generation quality to upstream retrieval precision and highlights the potential for targeted architectural modifications to the retrieval component to produce meaningful gains in end-to-end RAG performance.

While improvements to the retrieval component are necessary, they are not sufficient to fully resolve the challenges inherent in RAG systems. A deeper structural issue concerns the semantic gap that exists between the retriever and the generator, which arise from differences in their respective training objectives and architectural designs [arxiv_2406.13249]. Because retrievers are typically trained to optimize relevance ranking rather than to support the specific inferential demands of a downstream language model, the documents they surface may be relevant in a general sense while nonetheless being difficult for the LLM to interpret or integrate effectively. This misalignment forces the language model into a passive role, obliging it to accept whatever documents the retriever provides and to expend its own representational capacity on distinguishing among them, a burden that can degrade generation quality [arxiv_2406.13249]. The R²AG framework has been proposed as a direct response to this problem, introducing a mechanism for incorporating retrieval-level information directly into the generation process. Specifically, R²AG employs a dedicated R²-Former module to capture nuanced features from the retriever and integrates these features into the LLM through a retrieval-aware prompting strategy, thereby enabling the generator to be informed not only by the content of retrieved documents but also by the retriever's own assessment of their relevance [arxiv_2406.13249]. This approach represents a meaningful departure from the conventional pipeline architecture, in which retrieval and generation are treated as largely independent stages, and points toward a more tightly coupled paradigm in which information flows bidirectionally between the two components.

Taken together, these research directions reflect a maturing understanding of RAG as a complex, multi-component system in which performance is determined by the quality of retrieval, the capacity of the generator to reason over retrieved content, and the degree of alignment between the two. The establishment of dedicated research venues, such as the R³AG workshop at SIGIR-AP 2024, signals the community's recognition that addressing these challenges requires sustained, focused inquiry into the basic principles and practical implementation of refined and reliable retrieval-augmented generation [arxiv_2410.20598]. The convergence of benchmarking efforts, retrieval model improvements, and architectural innovations aimed at bridging the retriever-generator gap collectively define the current frontier of RAG research and set the stage for the more detailed technical investigations that follow in subsequent sections of this review.

### RAG System Components and Architecture Improvements — evidence cluster A

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential developments in the deployment of large language models (LLMs) for knowledge-intensive tasks, offering a principled mechanism for grounding model outputs in externally retrieved evidence rather than relying solely on parametric knowledge encoded during pretraining. The core motivation for this paradigm stems from well-documented limitations of standalone generative models, including their tendency to hallucinate factual content, their inability to incorporate information beyond their training cutoff, and the prohibitive cost of continually retraining large models to keep their knowledge current. By coupling a retrieval component with a generative component, RAG systems allow LLMs to condition their responses on dynamically retrieved passages, substantially improving factual accuracy in domains such as open-domain question answering (ODQA), document-grounded dialogue, and knowledge-intensive reasoning [arxiv_2502.20245].

The foundational tension within RAG research lies in balancing retrieval precision and recall against the quality and coherence of downstream generation. Traditional retrieval methods such as BM25 operate on lexical overlap and are computationally efficient but lack the semantic depth necessary to capture paraphrastic or conceptually related content. Dense retrieval approaches, exemplified by Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), address this limitation by encoding queries and passages into shared embedding spaces, enabling similarity search over semantically related content. Empirical comparisons confirm that dense retrievers achieve meaningful gains over sparse baselines; for instance, DPR attains a top-1 accuracy of 50.17% on the Natural Questions benchmark, while hybrid models that combine sparse and dense signals improve nDCG@10 scores on the BEIR benchmark from 43.42 for BM25 alone to 52.59, demonstrating the complementary strengths of these retrieval paradigms [arxiv_2502.20245]. Nevertheless, even high-performing dense retrievers face challenges when queries are ambiguous, underspecified, or require multi-hop reasoning across documents that are not individually relevant to the original query.

A recurring problem in RAG pipelines is that a single query formulation is often insufficient to retrieve all documents necessary for answering complex questions. This observation motivates research into multi-stage and iterative retrieval strategies. The Dynamic-Relevant Retrieval-Augmented Generation (DR-RAG) framework addresses this limitation directly by proposing a two-stage retrieval process in which documents retrieved in the first stage are combined with the original query to formulate enriched secondary queries, thereby recovering documents that exhibit low direct relevance to the query but high relevance to the answer when considered in conjunction with initially retrieved material [arxiv_2406.07348]. This insight—that critical documents may be latently relevant rather than directly relevant—represents an important conceptual advance over single-pass retrieval. DR-RAG further incorporates a compact classifier to assess document relevance dynamically, improving recall without incurring the prohibitive cost of repeated full LLM inference [arxiv_2406.07348]. The framework thus navigates a practical efficiency-accuracy tradeoff that is central to deploying RAG systems at scale.

Complementary to retrieval strategy, the quality of the query itself has received considerable attention as a lever for improving RAG performance. BlendFilter introduces a query generation blending approach that integrates both external knowledge augmentation and internal knowledge augmentation derived from the LLM's own parametric representations alongside the original query [arxiv_2402.11129]. By constructing a richer, multi-faceted query representation, BlendFilter aims to ensure more comprehensive information gathering from external corpora. Crucially, the framework also incorporates a knowledge filtering module that leverages the intrinsic capabilities of the LLM to identify and discard noisy or irrelevant retrieved passages before they are presented as context for generation [arxiv_2402.11129]. This dual focus on query enrichment and noise suppression addresses two distinct failure modes that commonly degrade RAG performance: retrieval gaps caused by underspecified queries and generation errors caused by misleading context. The filtering step is particularly significant because it acknowledges that retrieved documents are not uniformly beneficial; irrelevant or contradictory passages can actively harm generation quality, a finding that motivates post-retrieval processing as a necessary component of robust RAG pipelines.

While the approaches discussed above operate primarily at the level of text chunks or passages, a structurally distinct line of research argues that chunk-level retrieval inherently fails to capture the relational structure of knowledge. The KG²RAG framework addresses this by integrating knowledge graphs (KGs) into the retrieval process to encode fact-level relationships between chunks [arxiv_2502.06864]. Rather than treating retrieved passages as isolated units of information, KG²RAG uses an initial semantic retrieval step to identify seed chunks and then employs KG-guided chunk expansion and KG-based chunk organization to assemble retrieved content into coherent, well-structured paragraphs that reflect the underlying relational topology of the knowledge base [arxiv_2502.06864]. This approach directly targets the diversity and coherence of retrieved results, two dimensions that purely semantic retrieval methods tend to optimize poorly. By surfacing implicit connections between chunks that share entities or predicates in the knowledge graph, KG²RAG is particularly well-suited to multi-hop question answering tasks such as those in the HotpotQA benchmark, where correct answers require synthesizing information from multiple documents linked by intermediate reasoning steps.

Taken together, these contributions reveal several converging themes in RAG research. First, single-pass retrieval is increasingly recognized as insufficient for complex queries, driving interest in iterative, multi-stage, and graph-guided retrieval architectures [arxiv_2406.07348; arxiv_2502.06864]. Second, the quality of the query representation is as important as the retrieval mechanism itself, motivating hybrid query formulation strategies that blend parametric and non-parametric knowledge sources [arxiv_2402.11129]. Third, post-retrieval filtering and organization are emerging as essential stages that mediate between raw retrieval output and final generation, ensuring that the context presented to the LLM is both relevant and coherent [arxiv_2402.11129; arxiv_2502.06864]. Finally, systematic benchmarking across retrieval paradigms continues to clarify the relative strengths of sparse, dense, and hybrid approaches, providing empirical grounding for architectural choices in RAG system design [arxiv_2502.20245]. These threads collectively define the current frontier of RAG research and set the stage for more sophisticated integration of retrieval and generation in future LLM systems.

### RAG System Components and Architecture Improvements — evidence cluster B

The foundational challenge motivating retrieval-augmented generation lies in a fundamental tension within large pre-trained language models: while these models demonstrably encode substantial factual knowledge within their parameters and achieve strong performance on many downstream tasks, their capacity to access, precisely manipulate, and update that knowledge remains inherently constrained [arxiv_2005.11401]. This limitation becomes especially pronounced in knowledge-intensive tasks, where task-specific architectures continue to outperform general-purpose language models, and where critical open problems such as providing provenance for model decisions and refreshing world knowledge without full retraining persist [arxiv_2005.11401]. The seminal work introducing the RAG framework directly addressed these shortcomings by proposing a general-purpose fine-tuning recipe that combines pre-trained parametric memory—the knowledge encoded in model weights—with non-parametric memory in the form of a differentiable access mechanism to an external document index [arxiv_2005.11401]. This hybrid architecture allowed the model to retrieve relevant passages at generation time and condition its outputs on both its internalized knowledge and the retrieved evidence, yielding state-of-the-art results on a range of knowledge-intensive benchmarks while simultaneously enabling more transparent and updatable knowledge representations [arxiv_2005.11401].

The broader landscape of retrieval-augmented language models has since expanded considerably, encompassing not only generation tasks but also understanding tasks, giving rise to the unified category of Retrieval-Augmented Language Models (RALMs) [arxiv_2404.19543]. Comprehensive surveys of this space identify three essential interacting components: the retriever, the language model, and the augmentation mechanism through which retrieved content is integrated into the model's processing pipeline [arxiv_2404.19543]. The diversity of model structures and downstream applications that emerge from varying these components is substantial, spanning question answering, dialogue, summarization, and domain-specific knowledge tasks, among others [arxiv_2404.19543]. This taxonomy underscores that RAG is not a monolithic technique but rather a family of approaches whose design choices at each component level have significant consequences for overall system behavior, performance, and reliability [arxiv_2404.19543]. Importantly, the survey literature also highlights that the challenges motivating RAG—particularly hallucination and the need for domain-specific or up-to-date knowledge—are not incidental but structural features of parametric language models that retrieval-based augmentation is uniquely positioned to address [arxiv_2404.19543].

Despite the promise of the RAG paradigm, practical deployments face a set of well-documented limitations that have spurred ongoing research into both refinements and alternative approaches. Chief among these limitations are retrieval latency, the risk of selecting irrelevant or misleading documents, and the added architectural complexity that retrieval pipelines introduce into otherwise streamlined inference workflows [arxiv_2412.15605]. These concerns have motivated the proposal of cache-augmented generation (CAG) as an alternative paradigm, particularly suited to settings where the corpus of relevant knowledge is of limited and manageable size [arxiv_2412.15605]. Rather than performing real-time retrieval at inference, CAG preloads all relevant documents into the extended context window of a large language model and caches the resulting runtime parameters, allowing subsequent queries to be answered without any retrieval step [arxiv_2412.15605]. Comparative analyses demonstrate that this approach eliminates retrieval latency and avoids errors attributable to imperfect document selection, suggesting that for constrained knowledge domains the added complexity of retrieval may be unnecessary [arxiv_2412.15605]. The emergence of CAG as a viable alternative reflects the broader impact of dramatically extended context windows in recent LLMs, which have shifted the boundary conditions under which retrieval is genuinely necessary versus merely conventional [arxiv_2412.15605]. Nevertheless, CAG's applicability remains bounded by the size of the knowledge base relative to the context window, meaning that for large-scale, open-domain, or continuously updated knowledge sources, retrieval-based approaches retain their practical necessity.

Within the RAG paradigm itself, a distinct line of research has focused on improving the quality of retrieved content as it is presented to the language model, recognizing that naive retrieval can introduce redundancy that wastes the limited capacity of the context window [arxiv_2407.12101]. Because context windows, even when extended, remain finite, the selection of which retrieved passages to include carries significant consequences: passages that are individually relevant but mutually redundant collectively provide less informational value than a more diverse set covering complementary aspects of the query [arxiv_2407.12101]. Prior approaches to this diversity-relevance trade-off, such as Maximal Marginal Relevance, address the problem by explicitly parameterizing a balance between the two objectives, but this framing treats diversity and relevance as competing rather than jointly optimizable properties [arxiv_2407.12101]. In contrast, the relevant information gain metric proposed in more recent work offers a probabilistic measure of the additional task-relevant information contributed by each candidate passage given what has already been selected, thereby unifying the diversity and relevance objectives within a single coherent framework [arxiv_2407.12101]. This approach represents a meaningful conceptual advance over earlier methods, as it grounds passage selection in an information-theoretic account of what the model actually needs rather than in heuristic trade-off parameters [arxiv_2407.12101].

Taken together, these contributions illuminate the multi-dimensional nature of the RAG research agenda. The original RAG formulation established the core value proposition of combining parametric and non-parametric memory [arxiv_2005.11401]; subsequent survey work has mapped the resulting landscape of model variants and applications [arxiv_2404.19543]; emerging alternatives like CAG have clarified the boundary conditions under which retrieval is and is not necessary [arxiv_2412.15605]; and work on retrieval quality has pushed toward more principled selection of what retrieved content should enter the model's context [arxiv_2407.12101]. Each of these threads reflects a different dimension—architectural, taxonomic, comparative, and optimization-theoretic—along which the field continues to develop.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms in contemporary natural language processing, offering a principled mechanism for grounding the outputs of large language models (LLMs) in verifiable external knowledge. Rather than relying solely on the parametric knowledge encoded during pre-training, RAG systems dynamically retrieve relevant documents or data at inference time and condition the generative process on that retrieved context. This architectural choice directly addresses a fundamental limitation of standalone LLMs: their tendency to produce plausible-sounding but factually incorrect outputs, a phenomenon widely referred to as hallucination. The growing body of literature surveying and extending RAG reflects both the urgency of this problem and the richness of the solution space that researchers have begun to explore.

At the broadest level, RAG can be understood as the integration of two historically distinct research traditions—information retrieval and conditional language generation—into a unified pipeline. Surveys of the field have traced this integration from its foundational formulations to increasingly sophisticated architectures. One comprehensive treatment characterizes the basic RAG architecture as a system in which retrieval and generation are tightly coupled to handle knowledge-intensive tasks, and documents a trajectory of significant technological advancement including innovations in retrieval-augmented language models and applications spanning question answering, summarization, and knowledge-based reasoning [arxiv_2410.12837]. This evolutionary framing is important because it situates current work not as a collection of isolated techniques but as the product of cumulative refinement, with each generation of methods responding to the shortcomings identified in its predecessors. The same survey acknowledges that ongoing challenges—including scalability, bias, and ethical considerations—continue to constrain the practical deployment of RAG systems, suggesting that the field remains far from a settled consensus on best practices [arxiv_2410.12837].

A complementary survey adopts a knowledge-oriented perspective, examining RAG through the lens of how external knowledge sources are selected, structured, and integrated with generative objectives. This work provides a taxonomy that categorizes RAG methods along a spectrum from basic retrieval-then-generate pipelines to more elaborate configurations in which retrieval and generation interact iteratively [arxiv_2503.10677]. Crucially, this survey foregrounds the alignment problem: retrieved information does not automatically serve the generative model's objectives, and ensuring that retrieved content is both relevant and appropriately incorporated into the generation process requires deliberate architectural and training choices [arxiv_2503.10677]. The emphasis on knowledge orientation distinguishes this framing from purely engineering-focused accounts, directing attention toward the semantic and epistemic dimensions of retrieval rather than merely its computational efficiency.

One of the most persistent sources of failure in RAG pipelines is the quality of the queries used to drive retrieval. Even when a retrieval corpus is comprehensive and well-indexed, a vague or poorly formed query will fail to surface the most relevant documents, and the generative model will consequently be conditioned on suboptimal context. This problem is not merely theoretical: empirical work has demonstrated that optimizing query generation through a query-document alignment score, and iteratively refining queries using LLMs themselves, yields measurable improvements in retrieval precision and downstream accuracy [arxiv_2407.12325]. The reported average accuracy gain of 1.6% across experimental conditions may appear modest in absolute terms, but it is significant given that the intervention operates entirely at the query formulation stage, without modifying the retrieval index or the generative model [arxiv_2407.12325]. This finding has broader implications for the design of RAG systems: it suggests that the interface between the user's information need and the retrieval mechanism is a high-leverage point for optimization, and that LLMs can be productively employed in a self-improving loop to sharpen their own retrieval queries.

The challenge of domain specificity represents another major axis of current RAG research. General-purpose LLMs, even when augmented with retrieval, frequently exhibit insufficient adaptability when deployed in specialized domains such as medicine, law, or engineering, where the relevant knowledge is not only technical but also structured in ways that flat document retrieval may fail to capture. The DSRAG framework addresses this limitation by constructing domain-specific multimodal knowledge graphs derived from domain documents and using these graphs as the primary retrieval substrate [arxiv_2509.10467]. This approach contrasts with the flat vector-store retrieval that characterizes many baseline RAG systems, replacing it with a structured representation that can encode relationships between entities, concepts, and modalities in a manner more faithful to the underlying domain knowledge [arxiv_2509.10467]. The knowledge graph construction process is itself driven by the domain documents, meaning that the retrieval structure is grounded in the same corpus that would be used in a conventional RAG pipeline, but reorganized to support richer contextual modeling.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate several recurring tensions in RAG research. There is a tension between generality and specialization: systems designed to work across diverse domains may sacrifice the structured knowledge representations that specialized domains require, while domain-specific frameworks may not transfer gracefully to new settings. There is also a tension between retrieval quality and generation quality: improvements at the retrieval stage, such as better query formulation [arxiv_2407.12325] or graph-structured indexing [arxiv_2509.10467], do not automatically translate into better generation if the generative model cannot effectively exploit the retrieved context. The alignment challenge identified in knowledge-oriented surveys [arxiv_2503.10677] is precisely the site where these tensions converge. Finally, there is a tension between the ambition of RAG as a solution to hallucination and the empirical reality that hallucinations persist even in well-designed RAG systems, as acknowledged across multiple surveys [arxiv_2410.12837, arxiv_2407.12325]. Addressing these tensions will require not only continued innovation in individual components but also more integrated theoretical frameworks that can guide the co-design of retrieval and generation across diverse deployment contexts.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has rapidly matured from a straightforward retrieve-then-read pipeline into a complex, multi-component architecture that intersects retrieval quality, generation fidelity, system efficiency, and user personalization. As large language models (LLMs) have grown in capability, the demands placed on the retrieval mechanisms that support them have grown correspondingly, giving rise to a rich body of work that seeks to optimize individual components, evaluate system behavior in a granular fashion, and ultimately align retrieval and generation in an end-to-end manner. Understanding the current state of this literature requires examining how researchers have diagnosed the shortcomings of earlier RAG designs, what architectural innovations have been proposed in response, and how the field is beginning to grapple with the opacity of retrieval-generation interactions.

Early RAG systems operated on a deceptively simple premise: retrieve a set of relevant documents and condition the language model's output on those documents. While this approach demonstrably improved factual accuracy over purely parametric generation, it exposed a cluster of persistent weaknesses. Complex questions that require the synthesis of multifaceted semantic information often defeat single-pass retrieval, because the query as originally posed may not adequately capture all the informational dimensions the answer requires. Repeated retrieval during long-horizon or multi-turn serving introduces latency and computational redundancy, and the resulting responses, even when factually grounded, tend to be generic rather than tailored to individual users. These limitations are catalogued and directly addressed by ERAGent [arxiv_2405.06683], which frames them as the central motivation for a new framework. ERAGent introduces synergistically operated modules—an Enhanced Question Rewriter and a Knowledge Filter—that work in concert to improve retrieval quality for semantically complex queries, while a Retrieval Trigger mechanism reduces unnecessary re-retrieval during extended serving sessions. The framework also incorporates personalization mechanisms, positioning ERAGent as one of the more comprehensive attempts to address multiple failure modes simultaneously rather than treating each in isolation.

The question of how to optimize the two principal components of a RAG system—the retriever and the generator—has proven to be one of the field's most technically demanding problems. A common pattern in prior work has been to fine-tune these components independently: the retriever is adapted to a frozen language model, or the language model is trained to consume documents produced by an off-the-shelf retriever. This decoupled strategy lacks end-to-end training supervision and therefore cannot capture the complex dependencies between what is retrieved and how the generator uses it. More recent efforts have attempted joint training, but these approaches have typically relied on simplifying assumptions of document independence that are widely acknowledged to be unrealistic. The Direct Retrieval-Augmented Optimization framework, DRO [arxiv_2505.03075], addresses this gap by enabling end-to-end training of both a generative knowledge selection component and the language model itself, explicitly modeling document interdependencies rather than treating each retrieved passage as an isolated input. By doing so, DRO moves the field closer to a regime in which the retriever and generator are co-optimized with respect to the same objective, a goal that earlier piecemeal approaches could only approximate.

While architectural innovation has driven much of the recent progress, the field has simultaneously recognized that evaluation methodology has not kept pace. The dominant evaluation paradigm for RAG systems has been end-to-end assessment of generated outputs, which conflates the contributions of retrieval quality, query routing accuracy, and generation fidelity into a single undifferentiated score. This opacity makes it difficult to diagnose where a system is failing or to compare systems in a principled way. The mmRAG benchmark [arxiv_2505.11180] is a direct response to this limitation. By integrating queries from six diverse question-answering datasets spanning text, tables, and knowledge graphs, and by converting all sources into uniformly retrievable documents, mmRAG enables modular evaluation in which individual components—retrieval accuracy, query routing, and generation quality—can be assessed independently. The benchmark's annotation of document relevance following standard information retrieval procedures provides the kind of granular, interpretable signal that end-to-end metrics obscure. Compared to ERAGent's focus on improving system performance, mmRAG's contribution is primarily diagnostic: it provides the infrastructure needed to understand why a system performs as it does across heterogeneous data modalities.

The interpretability problem extends beyond benchmarking to the internal dynamics of RAG workflows themselves. Even when a system produces high-quality outputs, the mechanisms by which retrieved knowledge is integrated into the generation process remain largely opaque. This opacity is not merely an academic concern; it has practical consequences for debugging, auditing, and refining deployed systems. RAGTrace [arxiv_2508.06056] addresses this challenge through an interactive evaluation system designed to analyze retrieval and generation dynamics at multiple levels of granularity, from high-level performance metrics down to fine-grained examination of retrieval relevance, generation fidelity, and cross-component interactions. Informed by a literature review and expert interviews, RAGTrace supports a multi-level analysis approach that distinguishes it from conventional evaluation practices, which typically report aggregate scores without exposing the internal knowledge integration process. In this respect, RAGTrace complements mmRAG: where mmRAG provides modular benchmarking infrastructure for comparing systems across datasets, RAGTrace provides an interactive lens for examining the internal behavior of a single system in depth.

Taken together, these four lines of work illuminate the current trajectory of RAG research. The field is moving simultaneously in two directions: toward more sophisticated architectures that address the retrieval quality, efficiency, and personalization gaps identified in earlier systems [arxiv_2405.06683], and toward end-to-end optimization frameworks that eliminate the artificial independence assumptions of decoupled training [arxiv_2505.03075]. At the same time, the community is investing in the evaluative and interpretive infrastructure needed to make progress measurable and meaningful, whether through modular multi-modal benchmarks [arxiv_2505.11180] or interactive tools for analyzing retrieval-generation dynamics [arxiv_2508.06056]. The convergence of these efforts suggests that the next generation of RAG systems will be not only more capable but also more transparent and more rigorously evaluated.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The evolution of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems has prompted sustained scholarly attention not only to the mechanics of retrieval and generation themselves, but also to the architectural decisions that govern how these two processes interact, how models are trained to perform them, and how the resulting systems are evaluated. A central tension in the literature concerns whether retrieval and generation should remain modular and loosely coupled or whether they ought to be unified within a single model. Alongside this architectural debate, researchers have increasingly recognized that RAG systems must be sensitive to user context, capable of hierarchical reasoning, and subject to rigorous, standardized evaluation. The following discussion synthesizes recent contributions across these interrelated dimensions.

A foundational assumption of conventional RAG architectures is that retrieval and generation constitute distinct pipeline stages, with an explicit textual query serving as the interface between them. This assumption has been productively challenged by ImpRAG, which proposes a query-free framework that integrates retrieval and generation into a single unified model [arxiv_2506.02279]. Rather than requiring human-specified queries to bridge the two components, ImpRAG allows the model to implicitly express its own information needs by dividing a pretrained decoder-only language model into specialized layer groups that optimize retrieval and generation simultaneously. Crucially, the system employs a two-stage inference process that reuses the same model parameters and forward pass for both tasks, thereby minimizing the representational disparity that typically arises when a separately trained retriever must interface with a generative language model. Evaluated across eight knowledge-intensive tasks, this approach demonstrates that collapsing the retriever–generator boundary can yield meaningful generalization gains [arxiv_2506.02279]. The significance of this contribution lies in its implicit critique of pipeline architectures: by treating the query as an emergent, latent representation rather than an explicit textual artifact, ImpRAG sidesteps the error propagation that occurs when a poorly formed query leads to irrelevant retrieved passages, which in turn degrades generation quality.

While ImpRAG addresses the structural coupling between retrieval and generation, a complementary line of research focuses on the generative model's internal reasoning capabilities once relevant documents have been retrieved. HIRAG argues that existing RAG systems over-rely on the in-context learning abilities of large language models without systematically cultivating the specific competencies that effective RAG generation demands [arxiv_2507.05714]. The framework proposes that RAG models should develop three progressively hierarchical abilities: filtering, which involves selecting relevant information from retrieved passages; combination, which involves integrating semantic information across multiple sources; and deeper chain-of-thought utilization that goes beyond surface-level synthesis. This hierarchical decomposition is operationalized through instruction tuning, providing the generative model with granular supervision tailored to RAG-specific subtasks rather than relying on general-purpose pretraining to handle these challenges implicitly [arxiv_2507.05714]. Compared to ImpRAG's strategy of architectural unification, HIRAG takes a training-centric approach: rather than redesigning the retrieval–generation interface, it enriches the generative model's capacity to reason over retrieved content. Both approaches, however, share the diagnosis that standard RAG pipelines leave substantial performance on the table by treating the language model as a passive consumer of retrieved text rather than an active, reasoning participant.

A third dimension of RAG system design concerns personalization. PersonaRAG introduces user-centric agents that adapt both retrieval and generation in real time based on user data and interaction history [arxiv_2407.09394]. The motivation is straightforward: even when a RAG system retrieves factually accurate and topically relevant passages, the generated response may fail to meet the specific informational needs of the individual user if the system lacks awareness of their background, preferences, or prior queries. By incorporating agents that model user context dynamically, PersonaRAG moves beyond the one-size-fits-all retrieval paradigm that characterizes most baseline systems. Evaluated across multiple question-answering datasets, the framework demonstrates consistent superiority over non-personalized baselines, suggesting that user-adapted information retrieval represents a promising and underexplored direction [arxiv_2407.09394]. In relation to the other frameworks discussed here, PersonaRAG occupies a distinct niche: whereas ImpRAG and HIRAG are primarily concerned with the internal mechanics of retrieval–generation integration and reasoning depth respectively, PersonaRAG foregrounds the external, user-facing dimension of RAG system behavior. These perspectives are not mutually exclusive; a fully realized RAG system would ideally combine implicit query formation, hierarchical reasoning over retrieved content, and sensitivity to user context.

Cutting across all of these architectural and training concerns is the question of how RAG systems should be evaluated. The survey by [arxiv_2405.07437] provides a comprehensive overview of this challenge, introducing a Unified Evaluation Process of RAG (Auepora) that systematically examines metrics applicable to both the retrieval and generation components. The authors identify and compare quantifiable metrics such as relevance, accuracy, and faithfulness, analyzing how these metrics relate to possible output and ground-truth pairs across existing benchmarks [arxiv_2405.07437]. A key insight of this work is that the hybrid structure of RAG systems and their reliance on dynamic, external knowledge sources create evaluation challenges that are not adequately addressed by metrics designed for either pure retrieval systems or pure generative models in isolation. The survey further highlights limitations in current benchmarks, pointing to gaps that future evaluation frameworks must address [arxiv_2405.07437]. This evaluative perspective is essential for contextualizing the empirical claims made by systems like ImpRAG, HIRAG, and PersonaRAG: without standardized, component-aware evaluation protocols, it is difficult to determine whether performance improvements on any given benchmark reflect genuine advances in retrieval quality, generation faithfulness, or the interaction between the two.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate a research landscape in which RAG systems are being refined along multiple, partially independent axes. Architectural unification [arxiv_2506.02279], training-based reasoning enhancement [arxiv_2507.05714], user-centric personalization [arxiv_2407.09394], and rigorous evaluation methodology [arxiv_2405.07437] each address a distinct limitation of conventional pipeline-based RAG, and progress along any one axis does not automatically resolve the challenges addressed by the others. The literature thus points toward a multidimensional research agenda in which advances in system design must be accompanied by equally sophisticated advances in evaluation.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms for addressing the well-documented limitations of large language models (LLMs), particularly their susceptibility to hallucination and their reliance on static, potentially outdated parametric knowledge. By coupling generative models with external retrieval mechanisms, RAG systems aim to ground text generation in verifiable, dynamically accessible information. Yet as the field has matured, researchers have increasingly recognized that the original formulation of RAG—wherein a retriever fetches documents once before generation begins—is insufficient for the full range of knowledge-intensive tasks that modern applications demand. The literature surveyed here reflects a trajectory from foundational RAG architectures toward more sophisticated, adaptive, and unified frameworks that attempt to resolve the persistent tensions between retrieval quality, generation fidelity, and system coherence.

A central challenge motivating much of this work is the disconnect between retrieval modules and the generative tasks they are designed to support. Traditional retrieval pipelines rely on large document indices and operate largely independently of the downstream generation process, creating a structural mismatch that can degrade overall system performance [arxiv_2402.01176]. This observation has spurred interest in generative retrieval (GR), a paradigm in which language models retrieve relevant documents by directly generating document identifiers rather than querying an external index. While GR has demonstrated superior performance in retrieval tasks in isolation, the relationship between generative retrieval and downstream knowledge-intensive tasks had remained largely unexplored prior to recent integrative efforts [arxiv_2402.01176]. The CorpusLM framework addresses this gap by proposing a unified language model that integrates generative retrieval, closed-book generation, and retrieval-augmented generation through a single, coherent decoding procedure [arxiv_2402.01176]. This unification is significant because it collapses what had previously been treated as distinct subtasks—document retrieval, knowledge recall, and grounded generation—into a single model operating over a shared corpus, thereby reducing the architectural fragmentation that characterizes pipeline-based RAG systems.

The question of how to improve the relevance of retrieved content has attracted parallel attention, particularly for queries with complex or multi-hop information needs. Standard single-pass retrieval often fails to capture the full informational requirements of such queries, because the initial query formulation may not adequately represent the knowledge gaps that only become apparent during generation [arxiv_2305.15294]. The Iter-RetGen approach responds to this limitation by synergizing retrieval and generation in an iterative, mutually reinforcing loop: each generation step produces output that serves as an informative context for the subsequent retrieval step, and each retrieval step in turn enriches the context available for the next generation step [arxiv_2305.15294]. This iterative synergy exploits the insight that a model's partial output reveals what additional knowledge may be needed to complete a task, effectively transforming generation into a form of query reformulation. Compared to static RAG architectures, Iter-RetGen demonstrates that strong performance on complex reasoning tasks can be achieved without requiring specialized training objectives, relying instead on the emergent capacity of large language models to guide their own retrieval [arxiv_2305.15294].

Building on this theme of dynamic adaptation, more recent work has proposed context-guided retrieval mechanisms that go beyond iteration to enable continuous, state-aware knowledge scheduling throughout the generation process. The approach described in [arxiv_2504.19436] introduces a multi-level perceptive retrieval vector construction strategy alongside a differentiable document matching path, enabling end-to-end joint training of retrieval and generation modules. This design directly addresses the limitations of static RAG structures in context adaptation, which struggle to modulate knowledge access in response to the evolving semantic state of an ongoing generation [arxiv_2504.19436]. By making the retrieval process differentiable and jointly optimizable with generation, this framework represents a methodological advance over both the pipeline architectures critiqued in [arxiv_2402.01176] and the iterative but still modular approach of [arxiv_2305.15294]. Experiments conducted across multiple large models—including GPT-4, GPT-4o, and DeepSeek—on the Natural Questions dataset provide evidence that dynamic, context-sensitive retrieval yields measurable improvements in generation quality for open-domain question answering and complex generation tasks [arxiv_2504.19436].

Alongside these architectural innovations, the field has increasingly grappled with the challenge of evaluating RAG systems in a principled and comprehensive manner. The hybrid architecture of RAG—combining retrieval and generation components that each introduce distinct failure modes—complicates evaluation considerably, as does the dependence of RAG systems on dynamic, externally maintained knowledge sources [arxiv_2504.14891]. A comprehensive survey of RAG evaluation methods identifies the need to assess not only system-level performance but also factual accuracy, safety, and computational efficiency, recognizing that optimizing for one dimension may introduce trade-offs along others [arxiv_2504.14891]. This multi-dimensional evaluation perspective is particularly relevant when comparing approaches such as CorpusLM, Iter-RetGen, and context-guided dynamic retrieval, each of which makes different architectural commitments that affect the ease of evaluation: unified models like CorpusLM may be harder to decompose for component-level analysis, while iterative and jointly trained systems raise questions about the attribution of performance gains to retrieval versus generation improvements [arxiv_2402.01176; arxiv_2305.15294; arxiv_2504.19436; arxiv_2504.14891].

Taken together, the literature reviewed here charts a clear progression in the conceptualization of RAG. Early formulations treated retrieval as a preprocessing step external to generation; subsequent work recognized that retrieval quality is inseparable from generation quality and proposed iterative coupling as a remedy; the most recent contributions push toward full architectural integration through joint training and unified model designs. Each advance responds to limitations identified in prior work, yet each also introduces new evaluation challenges that the field is only beginning to systematically address.
