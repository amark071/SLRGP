# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models (long)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms in contemporary natural language processing, representing a fundamental rethinking of how large language models (LLMs) access, integrate, and reason over knowledge. The core intuition behind RAG is deceptively straightforward: rather than relying exclusively on the parametric knowledge encoded in a model's weights during pretraining, a RAG system supplements the generative process with dynamically retrieved information drawn from an external, non-parametric knowledge store. This combination of parametric and non-parametric memory, first formally articulated in the seminal work by Lewis and colleagues [arxiv_2005.11401], has since evolved into a rich and multifaceted research area that touches on retrieval systems, language model architectures, training methodologies, evaluation frameworks, and a wide spectrum of downstream applications. The present review synthesizes the current state of this literature, tracing the conceptual foundations of RAG, examining the architectural components and their interactions, surveying the major paradigmatic advances, and situating ongoing challenges within the broader trajectory of the field.

The foundational motivation for RAG arises directly from well-documented limitations of large pre-trained language models. Despite their remarkable capacity to store and recall factual knowledge through their parameters, LLMs are prone to hallucination—the generation of plausible-sounding but factually incorrect content—and their internal knowledge is necessarily frozen at the time of training, rendering them unable to reflect developments that postdate their pretraining corpus [arxiv_2312.10997]. Furthermore, LLMs often lack the depth of domain-specific expertise required for specialized applications, and their reasoning processes can be opaque and difficult to trace or verify [arxiv_2404.19543]. These limitations are not merely theoretical concerns; they represent practical barriers to deployment in high-stakes domains such as medicine, law, and finance, where factual accuracy and verifiability are paramount. RAG addresses these concerns by grounding generation in retrieved evidence, thereby enabling continuous knowledge updates, improving factual reliability, and providing a degree of provenance for generated outputs [arxiv_2405.06211]. As multiple survey papers have noted, the appearance of RAG has substantially mitigated these core limitations, though it has simultaneously introduced a new set of challenges related to retrieval quality, system complexity, and the integration of retrieved content with generative processes [arxiv_2407.13193, arxiv_2404.10981].

The original RAG formulation by Lewis et al. [arxiv_2005.11401] introduced a general-purpose fine-tuning recipe in which a pre-trained sequence-to-sequence model was combined with a dense retrieval component backed by a non-parametric memory of Wikipedia passages. The retrieval component, based on Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), encoded both queries and documents into a shared dense vector space, enabling efficient maximum inner product search over large corpora. The generative model then conditioned its output on both the input query and the retrieved passages, with two variants proposed: RAG-Sequence, which used the same retrieved document for the entire output sequence, and RAG-Token, which allowed different documents to inform different tokens in the output. This architecture demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on a range of knowledge-intensive NLP tasks, including open-domain question answering, fact verification, and knowledge-grounded dialogue generation, establishing the template upon which much subsequent work has been built. The significance of this contribution lies not only in its empirical results but in its conceptual framing of retrieval as a differentiable, end-to-end trainable component that could be jointly optimized with the generative model—a departure from earlier pipeline approaches in which retrieval and generation were treated as independent modules.

Subsequent work has substantially elaborated on this foundational architecture, and several comprehensive surveys have attempted to organize the rapidly expanding literature into coherent taxonomies. The survey by Gao et al. [arxiv_2312.10997] offers one of the most widely cited organizational frameworks, distinguishing among three successive paradigms: Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG. Naive RAG refers to the basic retrieve-then-read pipeline, in which a query is used to retrieve relevant passages from an index, and those passages are concatenated with the query as context for the generative model. While effective in many settings, Naive RAG suffers from several well-understood failure modes: retrieval may return noisy or irrelevant documents, the retrieved passages may contain conflicting information, and the generative model may not effectively utilize the provided context. Advanced RAG addresses these issues through a range of pre-retrieval and post-retrieval enhancements, including query reformulation, re-ranking of retrieved passages, and context compression. Modular RAG represents a further evolution in which the system is decomposed into discrete, interchangeable components—retrievers, rerankers, filters, generators, and memory modules—that can be independently optimized and recombined in flexible configurations. This modular perspective has proven particularly influential, as it provides a principled basis for understanding the diverse array of RAG variants that have proliferated in the literature [arxiv_2503.10677, arxiv_2410.12837].

The survey by Fan et al. [arxiv_2405.06211] adopts a complementary perspective, situating RAG within the broader context of AI-generated content and emphasizing the role of retrieval in enabling generative models to produce high-quality, reliable outputs. This work highlights the particular importance of RAG in the era of large language models, where the scale of parametric knowledge is enormous but still fundamentally limited by the static nature of pretraining. Similarly, the survey by Zhao et al. [arxiv_2404.10981] organizes the RAG paradigm into four stages—pre-retrieval, retrieval, post-retrieval, and generation—providing a detailed perspective from the retrieval viewpoint and tracing the field's progression through an analysis of significant studies. The survey by Chan et al. [arxiv_2404.19543] extends this perspective to encompass both Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Retrieval-Augmented Understanding, recognizing that retrieval augmentation is relevant not only to generative tasks but also to discriminative and comprehension-oriented tasks. Collectively, these surveys reflect a field that has matured considerably from its origins in simple retrieve-then-read pipelines, developing into a sophisticated ecosystem of techniques, architectures, and evaluation methodologies.

A central concern in the RAG literature is the quality of the retrieval component, which serves as the foundation upon which all subsequent processing depends. The choice of retrieval method has significant implications for both the relevance of retrieved content and the computational efficiency of the overall system. Traditional sparse retrieval methods such as BM25 rely on lexical overlap between queries and documents, offering high efficiency and interpretability but limited ability to capture semantic similarity. Dense retrieval methods, exemplified by DPR [arxiv_2005.11401], encode queries and documents into continuous vector representations and retrieve by nearest-neighbor search in the embedding space, enabling more nuanced semantic matching at the cost of greater computational overhead. A systematic comparison of these approaches [arxiv_2502.20245] demonstrates that dense retrievers, particularly DPR, achieve strong performance on open-domain question answering benchmarks such as Natural Questions, with a top-1 accuracy of 50.17%, while hybrid models that combine sparse and dense retrieval improve normalized discounted cumulative gain scores on broader retrieval benchmarks, suggesting that the two paradigms are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

The limitations of standard DPR have motivated a range of enhancements. One line of work focuses on improving the accuracy of document retrieval through the incorporation of control tokens into the DPR model [arxiv_2405.13008]. By augmenting the DPR encoder with domain-specific control tokens, this approach achieves a 13% improvement in Top-1 accuracy and a 4% improvement in Top-20 accuracy over the standard DPR baseline, demonstrating that relatively modest architectural modifications can yield meaningful gains in retrieval precision. Another important direction involves the optimization of query generation, recognizing that the quality of the query submitted to the retrieval system is a critical determinant of retrieval success. Vague or ambiguous queries are a significant source of retrieval failure in RAG systems, and several approaches have been proposed to address this. The work on query generation optimization [arxiv_2407.12325] introduces a query-document alignment score to guide the refinement of queries using LLMs, achieving an average accuracy gain of 1.6% in document retrieval. While this improvement may appear modest in absolute terms, it reflects the broader principle that systematic query optimization can have compounding benefits throughout the RAG pipeline.

The BlendFilter framework [arxiv_2402.11129] addresses both query quality and retrieval noise through a two-pronged approach. On the query side, BlendFilter proposes a blending process that integrates both external and internal knowledge augmentation with the original query, ensuring comprehensive information gathering. On the retrieval side, a knowledge filtering module leverages the intrinsic capabilities of the LLM to eliminate extraneous data from the retrieved context. This combination of query blending and knowledge filtering is shown to be effective on complex open-domain question answering tasks, where single-query retrieval often fails to capture the full range of relevant information. The ERAGent framework [arxiv_2405.06683] similarly addresses the challenge of complex questions that require the retrieval of multifaceted semantic information, introducing an Enhanced Question Rewriter and Knowledge Filter as synergistically operated modules. ERAGent also incorporates a Retrieval Trigger mechanism to address inefficiencies in knowledge re-retrieval during long-term serving, and a Personalized Response Generator to address the lack of personalized responses—a dimension of RAG that has received relatively limited attention in the broader literature.

The challenge of retrieving relevant documents for complex, multi-hop questions has motivated the development of multi-stage and iterative retrieval frameworks. The DR-RAG framework [arxiv_2406.07348] addresses the observation that even though there may be low relevance between some critical documents and the original query, it is often possible to retrieve those documents by combining parts of already-retrieved documents with the query. This insight motivates a two-stage retrieval framework in which an initial retrieval step is followed by a dynamic relevance-based second stage that mines additional relevant documents by combining query fragments with initially retrieved content. A compact classifier is employed to maintain efficiency while improving document retrieval recall and answer accuracy. This approach reflects a broader trend toward multi-step retrieval strategies that iteratively refine the set of retrieved documents based on intermediate results.

The Iter-RetGen framework [arxiv_2305.15294] takes this iterative philosophy further, proposing a method that synergizes retrieval and generation in an explicitly iterative manner. The key insight is that a model's output at each generation step provides informative context for retrieving more relevant knowledge in the next step, creating a virtuous cycle in which generation improves retrieval and retrieval improves generation. This iterative synergy is shown to achieve strong performance on tasks requiring complex reasoning and multi-hop inference, where a single round of retrieval is insufficient to gather all necessary information. The Iter-RetGen approach can be seen as an early instantiation of the broader principle of adaptive retrieval, in which the retrieval process is dynamically adjusted based on the evolving state of the generation.

A related but distinct challenge concerns the diversity and redundancy of retrieved passages. When multiple passages are retrieved for a given query, they may contain overlapping information, wasting the limited context window of the LLM and potentially diluting the signal from more informative passages. The work on Relevant Information Gain [arxiv_2407.12101] addresses this problem by proposing a novel optimization metric based on a probabilistic measure of the additional relevant information contributed by each candidate passage, given the passages already selected. This approach contrasts with earlier diversity-promoting methods such as Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR), which explicitly trade off diversity and relevance through a weighted combination of the two objectives. The Relevant Information Gain metric provides a more principled basis for this trade-off, grounded in information-theoretic reasoning, and is shown to improve the quality of RAG outputs by ensuring that the retrieved context is both relevant and non-redundant.

The integration of retrieved information into the generative process is another critical dimension of RAG system design. A fundamental challenge in this regard is the semantic gap between retrievers and LLMs, which arises from differences in their training objectives and architectures. Standard RAG systems force LLMs to passively accept the documents provided by the retriever, placing the burden of relevance discrimination on the LLM's inherent knowledge. The R²AG framework [arxiv_2406.13249] addresses this misalignment by incorporating retrieval information directly into the generation process. Specifically, R²AG utilizes nuanced features from the retriever and employs a dedicated R²-Former module to capture retrieval information, which is then integrated into the generation process through a retrieval-aware prompting strategy. This approach effectively bridges the semantic gap between retrievers and LLMs, enabling more coherent and accurate generation by making the retrieval signal explicit to the generative model.

The taxonomy of retrieval fusion strategies introduced in the survey by [arxiv_2407.13193] provides a useful framework for understanding the diverse ways in which retrieved information can be integrated with the generative process. Query-based fusion involves modifying the input query based on retrieved information before passing it to the generator. Logits-based fusion combines the probability distributions produced by the generator conditioned on different retrieved passages. Latent fusion integrates retrieved information at the level of hidden representations within the generator. Parametric fusion involves updating the parameters of the generator based on retrieved information, effectively encoding retrieved knowledge into the model's weights. Each of these fusion strategies involves different trade-offs in terms of accessibility, computational efficiency, and suitability for different use cases, and the choice among them should be guided by the specific requirements of the application at hand.

The Context-Guided Dynamic Retrieval approach [arxiv_2504.19436] represents a further development in the direction of tight integration between retrieval and generation. This work proposes a state-aware dynamic knowledge retrieval mechanism that introduces a multi-level perceptive retrieval vector construction strategy and a differentiable document matching path, enabling end-to-end joint training and collaborative optimization of the retrieval and generation modules. By making the retrieval process sensitive to the evolving context of the generation, this approach addresses the limitations of static RAG structures in context adaptation and knowledge access, and is shown to improve performance on open-domain question answering tasks across multiple large models including GPT-4, GPT-4o, and DeepSeek.

The Direct Retrieval-Augmented Optimization (DRO) framework [arxiv_2505.03075] tackles the challenge of end-to-end optimization of RAG systems from a different angle. Most prior work has independently fine-tuned the retriever to adapt to frozen LLMs, or trained LLMs to use documents retrieved by off-the-shelf retrievers, lacking end-to-end training supervision. Recent work has addressed this by jointly training the two components, but has relied on overly simplifying assumptions of document independence. DRO proposes a generative knowledge selection component that enables end-to-end training of both the retriever and the generator, relaxing the independence assumption and providing more realistic training supervision. This work highlights the importance of joint optimization for achieving the full potential of RAG systems, and points toward a future in which retrieval and generation are truly co-designed rather than assembled from independently developed components.

The ImpRAG framework [arxiv_2506.02279] pushes this integration even further by eliminating the need for explicit textual queries altogether. Traditional RAG systems treat retrieval and generation as separate processes connected by explicit queries, which can limit the model's ability to generalize across diverse tasks. ImpRAG proposes a query-free RAG system that integrates retrieval and generation into a unified model, allowing the model to implicitly express its information needs without human-specified queries. By dividing pretrained decoder-only language models into specialized layer groups, ImpRAG optimizes retrieval and generation tasks simultaneously using the same model parameters and forward pass, minimizing the disparity between retrievers and language models. Experiments on eight knowledge-intensive tasks demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach, suggesting that the traditional separation between retrieval and generation may be an architectural artifact rather than a fundamental necessity.

Knowledge graph-based approaches represent another important direction in the evolution of RAG systems, motivated by the observation that standard semantic retrieval methods retrieve isolated chunks of text without capturing the relational structure among them. The KG²RAG framework [arxiv_2502.06864] proposes to use knowledge graphs to provide fact-level relationships between chunks, improving the diversity and coherence of retrieved results. After performing an initial semantic retrieval to identify seed chunks, KG²RAG employs a knowledge graph-guided chunk expansion process and a knowledge graph-based chunk organization process to deliver relevant and important knowledge in well-organized paragraphs. Experiments on the HotpotQA dataset demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach for multi-hop reasoning tasks, where the relational structure of knowledge is particularly important. The DSRAG framework [arxiv_2509.10467] extends this idea to domain-specific applications, proposing a multimodal knowledge graph-driven retrieval-augmented generation framework that leverages domain-specific documents as the primary source for constructing the knowledge graph. This approach addresses the limitations of general-purpose LLMs in domain-specific tasks, where knowledge hallucination and insufficient domain adaptability are particularly acute concerns.

The CorpusLM framework [arxiv_2402.01176] explores a different approach to integrating retrieval with generation, proposing a unified language model that leverages external corpus to tackle various knowledge-intensive tasks by integrating generative retrieval, closed-book generation, and RAG through a unified greedy decoding process. Generative retrieval, in which the model retrieves by directly generating document identifiers rather than encoding queries into a vector space, offers a fundamentally different paradigm for retrieval that may offer advantages in terms of end-to-end trainability and integration with the generative process. CorpusLM demonstrates that these three approaches—generative retrieval, closed-book generation, and RAG—can be unified within a single model, with the model learning to select the appropriate strategy based on the characteristics of the input.

Personalization represents an emerging dimension of RAG research that has received growing attention. Standard RAG systems retrieve and present information without regard to the specific needs, preferences, or background knowledge of individual users, potentially resulting in responses that are accurate but not optimally tailored to the user's context. The PersonaRAG framework [arxiv_2407.09394] addresses this limitation by incorporating user-centric agents that adapt retrieval and generation based on real-time user data and interactions. Evaluated across various question answering datasets, PersonaRAG demonstrates superiority over baseline models in providing tailored answers to user needs, suggesting that personalization is a valuable dimension of RAG system design that deserves further investigation. The ERAGent framework [arxiv_2405.06683] similarly incorporates a Personalized Response Generator as one of its core components, reflecting a broader recognition that the utility of RAG systems depends not only on their factual accuracy but also on their ability to communicate information in a manner appropriate to the user's context and needs.

The evaluation of RAG systems presents unique challenges that have motivated a substantial body of dedicated research. Unlike standard NLP tasks, where evaluation can be conducted against fixed ground-truth labels, RAG systems involve a hybrid architecture that combines retrieval and generation components, and their performance depends on dynamic knowledge sources that may change over time. The survey by [arxiv_2405.07437] provides a comprehensive overview of RAG evaluation methods and benchmarks, examining and comparing quantifiable metrics for both the retrieval and generation components, including relevance, accuracy, and faithfulness. The survey identifies several key challenges in RAG evaluation, including the difficulty of disentangling the contributions of retrieval and generation to overall system performance, the lack of standardized benchmarks, and the limitations of automated metrics in capturing the full range of qualities that matter for real-world applications.

The Retrieval-Augmented Generation Benchmark (RGB) [arxiv_2309.01431] represents one of the most systematic efforts to evaluate the impact of RAG on different LLMs. RGB divides evaluation instances into four categories corresponding to fundamental abilities required for RAG: noise robustness, negative rejection, information integration, and counterfactual robustness. Noise robustness measures the model's ability to generate correct answers in the presence of irrelevant or misleading retrieved passages. Negative rejection measures the model's ability to abstain from answering when the retrieved passages do not contain relevant information. Information integration measures the model's ability to synthesize information from multiple retrieved passages. Counterfactual robustness measures the model's ability to recognize and reject factually incorrect information in retrieved passages. This multi-dimensional evaluation framework reveals significant variation in RAG performance across different LLMs and across different task types, highlighting the importance of rigorous, multi-faceted evaluation for understanding the true capabilities and limitations of RAG systems.

The comprehensive survey on RAG evaluation [arxiv_2504.14891] extends this perspective, systematically reviewing traditional and emerging evaluation approaches for system performance, factual accuracy, safety, and computational efficiency in the LLM era. This work compiles and categorizes RAG-specific datasets and evaluation frameworks, conducting a meta-analysis of evaluation practices across the literature. A key finding is that evaluation practices in the RAG literature are highly heterogeneous, with different studies using different datasets, metrics, and evaluation protocols, making it difficult to draw reliable comparisons across systems. The survey calls for greater standardization of evaluation practices as a prerequisite for meaningful progress in the field.

The mmRAG benchmark [arxiv_2505.11180] addresses the limitation that existing RAG evaluation predominantly focuses on text retrieval and relies on opaque, end-to-end assessments of generated outputs. mmRAG introduces a modular benchmark designed for evaluating multi-modal RAG systems, integrating queries from six diverse question-answering datasets spanning text, tables, and knowledge graphs. By converting all data sources into retrievable documents and annotating document relevance following standard information retrieval procedures, mmRAG enables direct, granular evaluation of individual RAG components—such as retrieval accuracy and query routing—beyond end-to-end generation quality. This modular approach to evaluation is particularly valuable for identifying specific bottlenecks in RAG systems and for guiding targeted improvements to individual components.

The RAGTrace system [arxiv_2508.06056] addresses the opacity of RAG workflows from a human-computer interaction perspective, introducing an interactive evaluation system designed to analyze retrieval and generation dynamics in RAG-based workflows. RAGTrace supports a multi-level analysis approach, ranging from high-level performance evaluation to fine-grained examination of retrieval relevance, generation fidelity, and cross-component interactions. This work highlights the importance of interpretability and transparency in RAG systems, not only for evaluation purposes but also for building user trust and enabling effective human oversight of AI-generated content.

The HIRAG framework [arxiv_2507.05714] approaches the challenge of RAG generation quality from the perspective of instruction tuning, proposing that RAG models should possess three progressively hierarchical abilities: filtering (selecting relevant information), combination (combining semantic information from multiple sources), and reasoning (performing multi-step inference over retrieved content). Traditional RAG systems primarily rely on the in-context learning capabilities of the LLM, without explicitly training the model for the specific demands of RAG tasks. HIRAG addresses this gap through hierarchical-thought instruction tuning, which provides granular supervision for each of the three hierarchical abilities and incorporates chain-of-thought processes to enable deeper utilization of retrieved information. This work reflects a broader recognition that the generative component of RAG systems requires targeted training and optimization, not merely the provision of retrieved context.

An important and somewhat contrarian perspective on RAG is offered by the work on Cache-Augmented Generation (CAG) [arxiv_2412.15605], which questions whether real-time retrieval is always necessary or desirable. CAG proposes an alternative paradigm in which all relevant resources are preloaded into the LLM's extended context window and cached as runtime parameters, eliminating the need for retrieval during inference. This approach is motivated by the advent of LLMs with significantly extended context windows, which can accommodate large amounts of information without the need for dynamic retrieval. CAG eliminates retrieval latency and avoids the potential errors associated with document selection, at the cost of requiring that the relevant knowledge base be of a limited and manageable size. Comparative analyses reveal that CAG can match or exceed RAG performance in settings where the knowledge base is small enough to fit within the context window, suggesting that the appropriate choice between RAG and CAG depends on the specific characteristics of the application, including the size and dynamism of the knowledge base. This work serves as a useful reminder that RAG is not a universal solution, and that the design of knowledge-augmented generation systems should be guided by careful consideration of the specific requirements and constraints of the deployment context.

The R³AG workshop [arxiv_2410.20598] reflects the growing recognition within the research community that, despite the impressive progress made in RAG, fundamental challenges remain that require deeper investigation. The workshop identifies several key areas requiring further exploration, including the reliability of retrieval, the robustness of generation, and the refinement of the overall RAG framework for downstream AI tasks. This initiative underscores the importance of community-level coordination in addressing the complex, multi-faceted challenges of RAG research, and points toward a future in which RAG systems are not only more capable but also more reliable, interpretable, and trustworthy.

The survey on knowledge-oriented RAG [arxiv_2503.10677] provides a comprehensive overview of RAG methods organized around the concept of knowledge, examining how different types of knowledge sources—including documents, databases, and structured data—can be integrated with generative models to improve performance and generate more accurate and contextually relevant outputs. This survey discusses the key characteristics of RAG, such as its ability to augment generative models with dynamic external knowledge, and the challenges associated with aligning retrieved information with generative objectives. The taxonomy presented in this survey categorizes RAG methods along multiple dimensions, including the type of knowledge source, the retrieval mechanism, and the integration strategy, providing a useful framework for understanding the diversity of approaches in the literature.

Across the literature, several recurring themes and tensions emerge that define the current state of RAG research and point toward future directions. One central tension is between retrieval quality and computational efficiency. More sophisticated retrieval methods, such as multi-stage retrieval [arxiv_2406.07348], iterative retrieval [arxiv_2305.15294], and knowledge graph-guided retrieval [arxiv_2502.06864, arxiv_2509.10467], can substantially improve the relevance and completeness of retrieved information, but at the cost of increased computational overhead. Simpler methods such as single-stage dense retrieval [arxiv_2005.11401] are more efficient but may miss important information for complex queries. The appropriate trade-off depends on the specific application, and there is a need for more systematic analysis of the efficiency-quality frontier in RAG systems.

A second tension concerns the degree of coupling between the retrieval and generation components. Tightly coupled systems, in which retrieval and generation are jointly trained end-to-end [arxiv_2505.03075, arxiv_2504.19436], can achieve better alignment between the two components but are more complex to train and may be less flexible in terms of component substitution. Loosely coupled systems, in which retrieval and generation are independently developed and combined at inference time, are more modular and easier to update but may suffer from the semantic gap between retrievers and LLMs [arxiv_2406.13249]. The R²AG framework [arxiv_2406.13249] and the ImpRAG framework [arxiv_2506.02279] represent different points on this spectrum, with R²AG bridging the gap through explicit retrieval information integration and ImpRAG eliminating the gap by unifying retrieval and generation within a single model.

A third tension concerns the scope of the knowledge base. RAG systems that retrieve from large, general-purpose corpora such as Wikipedia can address a wide range of queries but may lack the depth of domain-specific knowledge required for specialized applications. Domain-specific RAG systems [arxiv_2509.10467] can provide more accurate and relevant information for specialized tasks but require the construction and maintenance of domain-specific knowledge bases. The choice between general and domain-specific retrieval is a fundamental design decision that should be guided by the specific requirements of the application.

The question of how to handle retrieved information that conflicts with the LLM's parametric knowledge is another important challenge that has received growing attention. The RGB benchmark [arxiv_2309.01431] specifically evaluates counterfactual robustness—the model's ability to recognize and reject factually incorrect information in retrieved passages—highlighting the importance of this capability for reliable RAG systems. More generally, the interaction between parametric and non-parametric knowledge in RAG systems is a complex and underexplored area, with important implications for the reliability and trustworthiness of generated outputs.

The scalability of RAG systems to very large knowledge bases is another ongoing challenge. As the size of the knowledge base grows, the computational cost of retrieval increases, and the risk of retrieving irrelevant or misleading information also grows. Approaches such as hierarchical indexing, approximate nearest neighbor search, and query routing can help to manage these challenges, but there is a need for more systematic investigation of scalability in RAG systems [arxiv_2410.12837]. The mmRAG benchmark [arxiv_2505.11180] takes a step in this direction by evaluating RAG systems across multiple modalities and knowledge sources, but much work remains to be done in understanding how RAG systems scale to the full diversity of real-world knowledge.

The safety and ethical dimensions of RAG systems have also begun to receive attention, though they remain relatively underexplored compared to performance-oriented research. The comprehensive evaluation survey [arxiv_2504.14891] explicitly includes safety as one of the dimensions of RAG evaluation, recognizing that retrieved information can introduce biases, misinformation, or harmful content into generated outputs. The R³AG workshop [arxiv_2410.20598] similarly identifies reliability and trustworthiness as key concerns for the field. As RAG systems are deployed in increasingly high-stakes applications, the importance of safety evaluation and mitigation will only grow.

Looking across the literature as a whole, it is clear that RAG has evolved from a relatively simple retrieve-then-read paradigm into a sophisticated and multifaceted research area encompassing a wide range of architectural innovations, training methodologies, evaluation frameworks, and application domains. The foundational work of Lewis et al. [arxiv_2005.11401] established the basic template, and subsequent work has progressively addressed the limitations of this template through innovations in retrieval quality [arxiv_2405.13008, arxiv_2407.12325, arxiv_2402.11129], retrieval-generation integration [arxiv_2406.13249, arxiv_2504.19436, arxiv_2505.03075], iterative and adaptive retrieval [arxiv_2305.15294, arxiv_2406.07348], knowledge graph-based retrieval [arxiv_2502.06864, arxiv_2509.10467], diversity-aware retrieval [arxiv_2407.12101], personalization [arxiv_2407.09394, arxiv_2405.06683], and evaluation [arxiv_2309.01431, arxiv_2405.07437, arxiv_2504.14891, arxiv_2505.11180]. The modular RAG paradigm [arxiv_2312.10997] provides a unifying framework for understanding this diversity of approaches, while the emergence of alternative paradigms such as CAG [arxiv_2412.15605] and query-free RAG [arxiv_2506.02279] points toward continued innovation in the fundamental architecture of knowledge-augmented generation systems.

The breadth and depth of the RAG literature reflects the fundamental importance of the problem it addresses: enabling LLMs to access, integrate, and reason over external knowledge in a reliable, efficient, and trustworthy manner. As LLMs continue to grow in capability and deployment scope, the importance of RAG as a mechanism for grounding generation in verified, up-to-date information will only increase. The challenges that remain—including end-to-end optimization, scalability, safety, interpretability, and personalization—are substantial, but the pace of progress in the field suggests that they are tractable. The literature reviewed here provides a rich foundation for future work, and the continued development of rigorous evaluation frameworks [arxiv_2309.01431, arxiv_2505.11180, arxiv_2508.06056] will be essential for ensuring that progress is measured in a meaningful and reproducible manner.

The preceding synthesis has traced the major architectural and methodological currents in RAG research, but several important dimensions of the literature warrant deeper treatment. In particular, the interplay between retrieval granularity and generation quality, the role of noise and adversarial conditions in shaping system robustness, the acceleration of RAG pipelines from a systems perspective, and the emerging landscape of unified or parametric alternatives to conventional RAG each represent active research fronts that merit careful examination.

**Retrieval Granularity, Query Formulation, and Context Management**

One of the most practically consequential design decisions in any RAG system concerns the granularity at which documents are indexed and retrieved. Early systems typically retrieved fixed-length passages or paragraphs, but subsequent work has demonstrated that the optimal retrieval unit varies considerably across tasks and query types. The DR-RAG framework [arxiv_2406.07348] addresses this challenge by applying dynamic document relevance scoring, recognizing that even passages with low surface-level similarity to a query may contain information that becomes relevant in the context of multi-hop reasoning chains. This insight motivates a more flexible approach to retrieval in which the relevance of a document is assessed not only with respect to the original query but also with respect to the intermediate reasoning states generated by the LLM. Such dynamic relevance assessment is particularly important for complex question-answering tasks in which the information need evolves as the model generates its response.

Query formulation is a closely related challenge. Vague or ambiguous queries are a primary source of retrieval failure in RAG systems, and several approaches have been proposed to address this problem. The work on optimizing query generation [arxiv_2407.12325] demonstrates that refining queries using a query-document alignment score can substantially improve retrieval precision and reduce the incidence of hallucination in downstream generation. Similarly, the BlendFilter framework [arxiv_2402.11129] proposes a combination of query generation blending—in which multiple query variants are generated and their retrieved results are merged—with knowledge filtering to remove noisy or irrelevant passages before they are presented to the LLM. This two-stage approach addresses both the query formulation problem and the noisy retrieval problem simultaneously, and the empirical results reported in that work suggest that both components contribute independently to overall performance. The DRAGIN framework [arxiv_2403.10081] takes a complementary approach by dynamically deciding both when to trigger retrieval and what query to issue based on the information needs of the LLM at each step of the generation process, using token-level uncertainty estimates to identify the precise moments at which external knowledge is most needed.

Context window management is an increasingly important concern as RAG systems are deployed with LLMs that have larger but still finite context windows. The work on relevant information gain [arxiv_2407.12101] formalizes the problem of selecting a diverse and non-redundant set of retrieved passages to maximize the information contributed to the LLM's context window. By measuring the marginal information gain of each candidate passage relative to the passages already selected, this approach avoids the common failure mode in which multiple retrieved passages convey essentially the same information, wasting valuable context window space. This concern is particularly acute in multi-hop and multi-document reasoning tasks, where the relevant information may be distributed across many passages and the selection of a diverse, complementary set of passages is essential for correct answer generation. The tension between coverage and redundancy in context construction is a fundamental challenge that is likely to become more rather than less important as RAG systems are applied to increasingly complex tasks.

**Robustness, Noise, and Adversarial Conditions**

A recurring theme in the RAG literature is the sensitivity of generation quality to the quality of retrieved passages. While the ideal scenario is one in which retrieval consistently returns highly relevant, accurate, and non-redundant passages, real-world retrieval systems frequently return passages that are partially relevant, factually incorrect, or even adversarially crafted. Understanding how LLMs respond to such imperfect retrieval is essential for building reliable RAG systems.

The work on the power of noise in RAG systems [arxiv_2401.14887] provides a counterintuitive but important finding: the presence of noisy or irrelevant passages in the retrieved context does not always degrade generation quality, and in some cases may even improve it by providing a richer context from which the LLM can extract relevant information. This finding challenges the assumption that retrieval precision is the dominant factor in RAG performance and suggests that LLMs may be more robust to retrieval noise than previously assumed. However, this robustness is not uniform across models or tasks, and the RGB benchmark [arxiv_2309.01431] demonstrates that different LLMs vary considerably in their ability to handle noisy, conflicting, or counterfactual retrieved passages. Specifically, the RGB benchmark evaluates four key capabilities—noise robustness, negative rejection, information integration, and counterfactual robustness—and finds that even state-of-the-art LLMs struggle with the latter two, particularly when retrieved passages contain plausible but incorrect information.

The comprehensive survey on RAG robustness frontiers [arxiv_2506.00054] provides a systematic treatment of the various ways in which RAG systems can fail under adversarial or noisy conditions, including retrieval of irrelevant passages, retrieval of factually incorrect passages, and adversarial manipulation of the knowledge base. This survey identifies grounding fidelity—the degree to which generated outputs are faithfully grounded in retrieved evidence—as a key dimension of RAG robustness that is distinct from both retrieval accuracy and generation fluency. Ensuring grounding fidelity requires not only high-quality retrieval but also LLMs that are capable of critically evaluating retrieved evidence and refusing to generate outputs that are not supported by the retrieved context. The development of evaluation frameworks that can measure grounding fidelity in a scalable and reliable manner is an important open problem, and the RAGTrace system [arxiv_2508.06056] represents a promising step in this direction by providing interactive tools for tracing the relationship between retrieved passages and generated outputs.

The question of when retrieval helps and when it hurts is addressed directly by the analysis in [arxiv_2510.09106], which examines the conditions under which retrieval augmentation improves or degrades LLM performance. This work finds that retrieval is most beneficial when the LLM's parametric knowledge is insufficient or outdated, but can be harmful when retrieved passages are irrelevant or misleading. These findings have important implications for the design of adaptive retrieval systems that can decide when to retrieve based on an assessment of the LLM's confidence in its parametric knowledge. The DRAGIN framework [arxiv_2403.10081] and the DR-RAG framework [arxiv_2406.07348] both address this challenge from different angles, but a fully principled solution to the when-to-retrieve problem remains an open research question.

**Systems-Level Acceleration and Efficiency**

While much of the RAG literature focuses on improving the quality of retrieved information and the faithfulness of generated outputs, the practical deployment of RAG systems at scale requires careful attention to computational efficiency. The profiling and acceleration work in [arxiv_2412.15246] provides a detailed analysis of the computational bottlenecks in RAG execution pipelines, demonstrating that the retrieval and generation phases interact in complex ways that are not always intuitive. In particular, this work shows that exact retrieval schemes, while computationally expensive, can paradoxically reduce overall inference time compared to approximate retrieval schemes in some settings, because the higher quality of exact retrieval reduces the number of generation steps required to produce a satisfactory answer. These findings underscore the importance of co-designing retrieval and generation components with attention to their joint computational profile, rather than optimizing each component in isolation.

The study of best practices for RAG systems [arxiv_2501.07391] provides a complementary perspective on efficiency, examining how choices such as chunk size, overlap, embedding model, and reranking strategy affect both quality and latency across a range of tasks. This work finds that there is no single configuration that is optimal across all tasks and that the best configuration depends on the specific characteristics of the task, the knowledge base, and the available computational budget. These findings reinforce the importance of task-specific tuning and highlight the need for efficient hyperparameter search methods for RAG systems. The comparison of retrieval and generation approaches in [arxiv_2502.20245] similarly finds that the relative advantages of traditional retrieval models such as BM25 and DPR versus generative models such as GPT-4 depend heavily on the specific task and evaluation metric, with no single approach dominating across all settings.

The emergence of cache-augmented generation (CAG) as an alternative to conventional RAG [arxiv_2412.15605] represents a particularly interesting development from a systems perspective. By preloading the entire knowledge base into the LLM's extended context window and caching the resulting key-value states, CAG eliminates the retrieval latency and document selection errors that are inherent in conventional RAG. However, this approach is only feasible when the knowledge base is small enough to fit within the context window, and it introduces its own computational costs in terms of the memory required to store the cached key-value states. The trade-offs between CAG and conventional RAG are therefore highly dependent on the size of the knowledge base and the frequency with which it is updated, and the two approaches may be complementary in practice, with CAG being preferred for small, stable knowledge bases and conventional RAG being preferred for large, dynamic knowledge bases.

**Parametric and Hybrid Alternatives to Conventional RAG**

The conventional RAG paradigm, in which retrieved documents are appended to the LLM's input context, has been challenged by several alternative approaches that seek to integrate external knowledge more deeply into the LLM's parameters or generation process. The parametric RAG framework [arxiv_2501.15915] proposes to inject retrieved knowledge directly into the LLM's parameters through a lightweight adaptation mechanism, rather than appending it to the input context. This approach has the advantage of avoiding the context length limitations of conventional RAG and enabling the LLM to integrate retrieved knowledge more seamlessly with its parametric knowledge. However, parametric injection is more computationally expensive than context injection and may be less flexible in terms of handling dynamic or frequently updated knowledge bases.

The CorpusLM framework [arxiv_2402.01176] takes a different approach by unifying retrieval and generation within a single language model that can both retrieve documents by generating their identifiers and generate answers conditioned on retrieved documents. This generative retrieval approach eliminates the need for a separate retrieval component and enables end-to-end training of the entire system, but requires the language model to memorize the identifiers of all documents in the knowledge base, which may be challenging for very large corpora. The direct retrieval-augmented optimization (DRAG) framework [arxiv_2505.03075] addresses the end-to-end training challenge from a different angle by proposing a joint training objective that simultaneously optimizes the retriever and the generator, enabling the two components to be trained in a mutually beneficial manner without requiring the generator to memorize document identifiers.

The HIRAG framework [arxiv_2507.05714] introduces hierarchical-thought instruction tuning as a mechanism for improving the RAG generation model's ability to handle documents of varying quality and relevance. By training the LLM to reason hierarchically about retrieved documents—first assessing their overall relevance, then extracting the most relevant information, and finally integrating this information into a coherent response—HIRAG addresses the challenge of inconsistent document quality that is a common failure mode in conventional RAG systems. This hierarchical reasoning approach is complementary to the retrieval-side innovations discussed earlier and highlights the importance of improving not only the quality of retrieved documents but also the LLM's ability to reason effectively over imperfect retrieved contexts.

**Collaborative, Personalized, and Domain-Specific RAG**

Beyond the core architectural and training challenges, the RAG literature has increasingly attended to the social and contextual dimensions of knowledge retrieval and generation. The DuetRAG framework [arxiv_2405.13002] addresses the challenge of domain-specific question answering by proposing a collaborative retrieval approach in which a domain-specific retriever and a general-purpose retriever work together to provide complementary knowledge. This collaborative approach is motivated by the observation that complex domain questions often require both specialized domain knowledge and general background knowledge, and that neither a domain-specific nor a general-purpose retriever alone is sufficient to address this need. The empirical results reported in that work demonstrate that the collaborative approach consistently outperforms either retriever alone on complex multi-hop domain questions.

The PersonaRAG framework [arxiv_2407.09394] extends the RAG paradigm in a different direction by incorporating user-centric agents that adapt retrieval and generation based on real-time user data and interaction history. This personalization approach addresses the limitation of conventional RAG systems, which treat all users as equivalent and do not account for individual differences in knowledge, preferences, or information needs. By maintaining user profiles and using them to guide retrieval and generation, PersonaRAG can provide more relevant and personalized responses, particularly in settings where users have diverse backgrounds and expertise levels. The ERAGent framework [arxiv_2405.06683] similarly addresses personalization and efficiency challenges by introducing a multi-component pipeline that includes enhanced question understanding, knowledge retrieval, and response generation modules, with particular attention to the challenge of retrieving multifaceted semantic information for complex questions.

The DSRAG framework [arxiv_2509.10467] represents a particularly ambitious approach to domain-specific RAG, constructing a multimodal knowledge graph from domain-specific documents and using this graph to guide retrieval and generation. By capturing not only the textual content of domain documents but also the relationships between entities and concepts, the DSRAG knowledge graph enables more accurate and contextually relevant retrieval than conventional text-based approaches. This work is part of a broader trend toward knowledge graph-augmented RAG [arxiv_2502.06864], in which structured knowledge representations are used to complement or replace unstructured text retrieval. The KG²RAG framework [arxiv_2502.06864] similarly uses knowledge graphs to provide fact-level guidance for retrieval, addressing the limitation of semantic-based approaches that retrieve isolated chunks without regard for their relational context.

**Evaluation Frameworks and Benchmarking**

The maturation of the RAG field has been accompanied by a growing recognition of the importance of rigorous and comprehensive evaluation. The survey on RAG evaluation [arxiv_2405.07437] identifies several unique challenges in evaluating RAG systems, including the hybrid nature of the retrieval-generation pipeline, the dependence on dynamic knowledge sources, and the difficulty of measuring faithfulness and groundedness in a scalable manner. The comprehensive evaluation survey [arxiv_2504.14891] extends this analysis to the era of large language models, examining how the evaluation of RAG systems must adapt to the capabilities and limitations of modern LLMs. This survey identifies a tension between automated evaluation metrics, which are scalable but may not capture all relevant dimensions of quality, and human evaluation, which is more comprehensive but expensive and difficult to reproduce.

The mmRAG benchmark [arxiv_2505.11180] addresses the limitation of existing RAG benchmarks, which predominantly focus on text retrieval and end-to-end evaluation of generated outputs. By integrating queries from six diverse question-answering datasets spanning text, tables, and knowledge graphs, mmRAG enables modular evaluation of RAG systems across multiple modalities and knowledge source types. This modular evaluation approach is important because it allows researchers to identify the specific components of a RAG system that are responsible for performance differences, rather than attributing all differences to the end-to-end system. The RAGTrace system [arxiv_2508.06056] complements this modular evaluation approach by providing interactive tools for tracing the relationship between retrieved passages and generated outputs, enabling researchers and practitioners to understand the internal dynamics of RAG systems in a more fine-grained manner.

The knowledge-oriented RAG survey [arxiv_2503.10677] provides a comprehensive overview of the various knowledge sources and retrieval strategies that have been explored in the RAG literature, organizing them according to the type of knowledge they provide and the tasks they are designed to support. This survey highlights the diversity of knowledge sources that have been integrated into RAG systems, including unstructured text, structured databases, knowledge graphs, and multimodal data, and identifies the specific challenges and opportunities associated with each type of knowledge source. The RAG and RAU survey [arxiv_2404.19543] similarly provides a broad overview of retrieval-augmented language models, covering both retrieval-augmented generation and retrieval-augmented understanding, and identifying the key differences and commonalities between these two paradigms.

Taken together, these evaluation and benchmarking efforts reflect a growing consensus in the RAG community that progress in the field must be measured against rigorous, comprehensive, and reproducible standards. The development of such standards is challenging given the diversity of RAG architectures, knowledge sources, and application domains, but it is essential for ensuring that the field advances in a coherent and cumulative manner. The R³AG workshop [arxiv_2410.20598] and the comprehensive surveys [arxiv_2312.10997, arxiv_2404.10981, arxiv_2407.13193, arxiv_2410.12837, arxiv_2506.00054] collectively represent the community's effort to synthesize and consolidate the rapidly growing body of RAG research, and they provide an important foundation for future work in this area. As the field continues to evolve, the integration of insights from retrieval, generation, evaluation, and application research will be essential for realizing the full potential of RAG as a mechanism for grounding LLM generation in reliable, up-to-date, and contextually relevant external knowledge.
