# 3D Gaussian Splatting (long)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most consequential developments in computer vision and graphics in recent years. Introduced by Kerbl and colleagues, 3DGS fundamentally reframes the problem of scene representation by abandoning the implicit, coordinate-based neural network formulations that had previously defined the field, instead modeling scenes as collections of explicit, optimizable three-dimensional Gaussian ellipsoids [arxiv_2308.04079]. This architectural choice carries profound implications for rendering speed, training efficiency, and downstream applicability, and has catalyzed an expansive body of follow-on research that continues to grow rapidly [arxiv_2403.11134].

To appreciate the significance of 3DGS, it is necessary to situate it within the broader trajectory of neural rendering research. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), introduced in 2020, established the foundational paradigm of representing scenes as continuous volumetric functions parameterized by neural networks, enabling photorealistic novel view synthesis from collections of posed images [arxiv_2210.00379]. NeRF-based methods model scene geometry and appearance through a ray-marching procedure in which sample points along each camera ray are queried against a multi-layer perceptron that predicts density and view-dependent color. While this approach yielded remarkable visual fidelity, it imposed substantial computational costs: training a single scene could require hours, and rendering a single frame at moderate resolution could take seconds, rendering real-time applications infeasible [arxiv_2308.04079]. The five years following NeRF's introduction saw an enormous proliferation of variants addressing these limitations through techniques such as hash-based spatial encodings, factored representations, and baked radiance caches, yet no NeRF-based method succeeded in achieving high-quality real-time rendering at full HD resolution for unbounded scenes [arxiv_2210.00379].

3DGS addressed this bottleneck through a fundamentally different representational strategy. Rather than querying a neural network along rays, 3DGS initializes a set of Gaussian primitives from the sparse point cloud produced during camera calibration via Structure-from-Motion, and then optimizes the position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients of each Gaussian through differentiable rasterization [arxiv_2308.04079]. The rendering pipeline projects these three-dimensional Gaussians onto the image plane using an efficient tile-based rasterizer, sorting and alpha-compositing them in a manner that approximates volumetric rendering without requiring per-ray numerical integration. The result is a system capable of achieving real-time rendering at 1080p resolution at frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second, while simultaneously matching or surpassing the visual quality of leading NeRF-based methods on standard benchmarks [arxiv_2308.04079]. The explicit nature of the representation also facilitates gradient flow during optimization, enabling competitive training times compared to many neural implicit approaches.

The relationship between 3DGS and volumetric rendering theory is, however, more nuanced than a simple replacement of one paradigm by another. As analyzed in depth by recent work, 3DGS employs a hybrid rendering solution that draws on the conceptual framework of volume rendering while introducing a series of approximations that prioritize computational efficiency [arxiv_2502.19318]. These approximations include the use of a 2D projection of the 3D Gaussian covariance rather than true volumetric integration, as well as the tile-based sorting and compositing scheme that avoids the per-ray depth ordering required by strict volumetric rendering. A central question motivating this line of inquiry is whether replacing these approximations with more principled volumetric rendering solutions would yield improvements in reconstruction quality [arxiv_2502.19318]. The analysis suggests that the approximations embedded in 3DGS are not merely engineering shortcuts but are deeply intertwined with the method's optimization dynamics, and that naive substitution of more accurate rendering formulations does not straightforwardly improve results. This finding underscores that 3DGS should be understood not simply as an approximation of NeRF-style volumetric rendering, but as a distinct representational and computational paradigm with its own theoretical properties.

The explicit, discrete nature of the 3DGS representation, while enabling its speed advantages, also introduces characteristic limitations that have motivated a substantial body of subsequent work. One prominent challenge concerns memory consumption: because each Gaussian primitive stores an independent set of parameters including position, full 3D covariance, opacity, and high-degree spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent appearance, scenes with complex geometry and appearance can require millions of Gaussians, resulting in substantial memory overhead [arxiv_2509.17083]. This stands in contrast to neural implicit representations, which amortize scene information across shared network weights. Hybrid approaches have been proposed to address this tension, combining explicit Gaussian primitives with grid-based neural fields that predict certain Gaussian properties rather than storing them independently per primitive [arxiv_2509.17083]. Such hybrid representations aim to retain the rendering speed and optimization tractability of 3DGS while reducing memory footprint and improving the modeling of high-frequency spatial variations in appearance and geometry. The decomposition of scene information into a compact set of explicit Gaussians handling critical high-frequency parameters alongside neural fields handling smoother properties represents a promising direction for reconciling the complementary strengths of the two paradigms [arxiv_2509.17083].

Surface reconstruction presents another domain in which the discrete, primitive-based nature of 3DGS creates specific challenges. Because scene geometry is implicitly encoded in the spatial arrangement and shape of Gaussian ellipsoids rather than in an explicit surface representation, extracting clean meshes or continuous surfaces from a trained 3DGS model is non-trivial. In sparse regions of the scene, the Gaussian primitives may be insufficient to define a coherent surface, leading to rough or incomplete reconstructions [arxiv_2411.01853]. Approaches such as Gaussian Voxel Kernel Functions address this by establishing a continuous scene representation derived from the discrete Gaussian primitives through kernel regression, effectively bridging the gap between the fast rasterization of 3DGS and the smooth implicit representations better suited to surface extraction [arxiv_2411.01853]. This integration allows high-fidelity surface reconstruction in open, unbounded scenes while preserving the computational advantages of the 3DGS rendering pipeline.

The quality of 3DGS reconstruction is also sensitive to the quality of the input images used for training. Blurry training images, which arise commonly in practice due to lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake, pose a significant challenge because the optimization process may fit the Gaussian primitives to the blurred observations rather than the underlying sharp scene content [arxiv_2401.00834]. Several approaches have been developed to address this problem within the 3DGS framework. One line of work models the blur as a physical process and incorporates blur-aware loss functions or explicit blur kernels into the optimization, allowing the system to recover sharp scene representations even from degraded inputs [arxiv_2401.00834]. A complementary approach, BAD-Gaussians, addresses the specific case of motion blur by modeling the camera trajectory during exposure as a continuous curve and synthesizing the blurred image as an average of sharp renderings along this trajectory, jointly optimizing the Gaussian scene representation and the camera motion parameters [arxiv_2403.11831]. This bundle-adjusted deblurring strategy leverages the explicit nature of the 3DGS representation to recover fine scene details that implicit NeRF-based methods struggle to reconstruct from severely motion-blurred inputs, while also achieving real-time rendering after training [arxiv_2403.11831]. The contrast with NeRF-based deblurring methods is instructive: while several NeRF variants have been proposed for training on blurry images, the implicit representation of NeRF limits both the fidelity of detail recovery and the rendering speed, disadvantages that the explicit Gaussian representation directly addresses [arxiv_2403.11831].

Surveying the broader landscape of 3DGS research, it is evident that the method has rapidly expanded beyond its original application to static scene reconstruction and novel view synthesis. The explicit, editable nature of the Gaussian representation has made it particularly amenable to dynamic scene reconstruction, where individual Gaussians or groups of Gaussians can be deformed or animated over time [arxiv_2403.11134]. Similarly, the geometric interpretability of the Gaussian primitives facilitates physical simulation, where the primitives can be coupled with physics engines to model deformable or rigid body dynamics. Geometry editing tasks, such as object insertion, removal, or stylization, are also more naturally supported by the explicit representation than by neural implicit fields, where edits must be mediated through the neural network's learned encoding [arxiv_2403.11134]. This breadth of downstream applicability has contributed to the rapid growth of the 3DGS research community and its displacement of NeRF as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis and related tasks [arxiv_2210.00379].

In summary, 3DGS represents a paradigm shift in scene representation and novel view synthesis, achieving real-time rendering quality through explicit Gaussian primitives and differentiable rasterization [arxiv_2308.04079]. Its relationship to volumetric rendering theory involves deliberate approximations whose implications are still being actively investigated [arxiv_2502.19318]. The method's limitations in memory efficiency, surface reconstruction, and robustness to degraded inputs have motivated a rich ecosystem of extensions and hybrid approaches [arxiv_2509.17083, arxiv_2411.01853, arxiv_2401.00834, arxiv_2403.11831], while its explicit representational structure has opened new avenues for dynamic reconstruction, editing, and simulation [arxiv_2403.11134]. Together, these developments establish 3DGS as a foundational technology whose influence on computer vision and graphics continues to expand.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has catalyzed a broad and rapidly expanding body of research addressing its foundational capabilities, inherent limitations, and diverse application domains. This subsection surveys the principal directions that have emerged from the core 3DGS framework, examining how researchers have sought to improve rendering fidelity, reduce memory overhead, extend the representation to dynamic content, enable scene manipulation, and address challenges arising from sparse input data.

At its core, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of explicit 3D Gaussian primitives that are differentiably rasterized to produce novel views at real-time speeds. This stands in marked contrast to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which rely on implicit volumetric representations queried through computationally expensive ray-marching procedures. As noted across multiple works, NeRF-based methods, while capable of high-quality synthesis, impose substantial training times and slow inference that hinder deployment in interactive or resource-constrained settings [arxiv_2312.04564] [arxiv_2405.17083] [arxiv_2405.17811]. The explicit, point-based nature of 3DGS circumvents these bottlenecks, enabling accelerated training and rendering while preserving competitive visual quality. Nevertheless, the transition from implicit to explicit representation introduces its own set of challenges, most prominently the large number of Gaussian primitives required to faithfully represent complex scenes and the correspondingly high memory demands that accompany them.

Memory efficiency has consequently become one of the most actively investigated problems in the 3DGS literature. Because each scene may require millions of Gaussian points, each carrying attributes such as position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients for view-dependent color, the aggregate storage cost can be prohibitive. Two complementary strategies have been proposed to address this. The first involves compressing the per-point attribute representation. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] introduces quantized embeddings to reduce the per-point memory footprint, coupling this with a coarse-to-fine training strategy that stabilizes optimization and a pruning stage that reduces the total number of Gaussians. The result is a representation that achieves faster training and rendering while occupying substantially less memory. The second strategy, exemplified by F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083], draws on classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a factorized coordinate and attribute space. Rather than storing each Gaussian independently, F-3DGS decomposes the representation into lower-rank factors, achieving significant reductions in storage overhead while preserving image quality. These two approaches are complementary in spirit: EAGLES operates at the level of individual point attributes through quantization and pruning, whereas F-3DGS restructures the global representation through factorization. Together they illustrate that the memory problem in 3DGS can be attacked from both local and global perspectives.

Beyond memory, the geometric expressiveness of Gaussian primitives has been identified as a fundamental limitation. Gaussians are smooth, diffuse shapes that spread continuously through space, making it difficult to represent sharp edges, flat surfaces, or geometrically structured regions without resorting to very large numbers of primitives. As argued by the authors of 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) [arxiv_2411.14974], Gaussians tend to disperse irregularly around actual surfaces in the absence of hand-crafted regularizers, and capturing hard edges requires a disproportionate increase in primitive count. To circumvent these issues, 3DCS replaces Gaussian primitives with 3D smooth convex shapes, which offer greater geometric flexibility and can more naturally represent flat surfaces and sharp boundaries. This substitution preserves the differentiable rasterization pipeline that makes 3DGS attractive while enabling more geometrically meaningful scene reconstructions. Similarly, TRIPS [arxiv_2401.06003] identifies blurring and cloudy artifacts as persistent failure modes of 3DGS in highly detailed scenes, and proposes a trilinear point splatting approach that rasterizes points into a screen-space image pyramid, combining ideas from Gaussian splatting with the crisper reconstruction capabilities of neural point-based methods. Where 3DCS addresses geometric expressiveness at the primitive level, TRIPS addresses it at the rasterization and reconstruction level, and both represent efforts to push beyond the quality ceiling imposed by vanilla Gaussian primitives.

The challenge of dynamic scene reconstruction introduces additional complexity, as the static Gaussian representation must be extended to account for temporal variation. A refined 3D Gaussian representation for dynamic scenes [arxiv_2405.17891] addresses this by coupling the explicit Gaussian point cloud with a deformable multi-layer perceptron network that predicts per-point dynamic offsets as a function of time, while expressing color features through hash encoding. This hybrid approach retains the rendering speed advantages of explicit Gaussian representations while borrowing the capacity of neural networks to model continuous deformation fields. The use of hash encoding for color is itself a nod to the efficiency techniques pioneered in the NeRF literature, illustrating how the boundary between explicit and implicit representations is increasingly blurred in practice. The dynamic extension of 3DGS is particularly important for applications in video synthesis, autonomous driving simulation, and augmented reality, where scenes are inherently non-static.

Manipulation and editing of 3DGS scenes represent another active frontier. While NeRF-based methods have been explored for deformation and editing, their implicit nature makes fine-grained control difficult, and their computational cost makes interactive editing impractical [arxiv_2405.17811]. Mani-GS [arxiv_2405.17811] proposes to bridge the gap between the high-fidelity rendering of 3DGS and the controllability afforded by explicit mesh representations. By binding Gaussian primitives to a triangular mesh, the method enables intuitive manipulation of the scene through mesh deformation, with the Gaussians following the mesh surface and preserving rendering quality throughout. This approach is conceptually significant because it leverages the complementary strengths of two representation paradigms: meshes provide structured, editable geometry, while Gaussians provide photorealistic appearance. The result is a system that supports free manipulation while maintaining the visual fidelity that makes 3DGS attractive for content creation workflows.

The problem of novel view synthesis from sparse input is a further challenge that has received considerable attention. Standard 3DGS, like most NeRF-based methods, assumes a dense set of calibrated input images and degrades substantially when only a few views are available. SCGaussian [arxiv_2411.03637] addresses this few-shot setting by introducing matching priors to enforce structural consistency across the sparse input views. The key insight is that the position of Gaussian primitives, which is difficult to constrain directly in vanilla 3DGS due to its non-structured nature, can be regularized through geometric matching cues derived from the available images. The method optimizes scene structure in two complementary ways: by constraining rendering geometry and by directly regularizing the spatial positions of Gaussian primitives through a hybrid Gaussian representation. This dual optimization is particularly important in large scenes, where the underdetermination of the reconstruction problem from sparse views is most severe. SCGaussian thus represents an effort to make 3DGS robust to the data acquisition conditions that prevail in practical deployment scenarios, where dense multi-view capture is often infeasible.

A related but distinct challenge concerns the modeling of scene materials and illumination, which standard 3DGS does not address. Most 3DGS methods, like most NeRF methods, estimate view-dependent appearance as a monolithic function of viewing direction without disentangling the contributions of surface reflectance and incident lighting. This conflation limits the utility of such representations for applications requiring relighting, material editing, or physically accurate rendering. PBR-NeRF [arxiv_2412.09680], while primarily a NeRF-based method, is directly relevant to this discussion because it explicitly identifies the failure to model materials and illumination as a shared limitation of both NeRF and 3DGS approaches. By formulating inverse rendering within a physics-based rendering framework and introducing novel physics-based priors as loss terms, PBR-NeRF achieves state-of-the-art material estimation while maintaining novel view synthesis quality. The authors note that their approach is adaptable to other inverse rendering and 3D reconstruction frameworks, implicitly gesturing toward the applicability of such physics-based constraints to 3DGS-based pipelines. This points to an important open direction: integrating physically grounded material and illumination models into the explicit Gaussian representation to enable the full suite of appearance editing capabilities that downstream applications demand.

Taken together, the works surveyed in this subsection reveal a coherent set of research priorities that have emerged in response to the strengths and limitations of the foundational 3DGS framework. Memory efficiency [arxiv_2312.04564] [arxiv_2405.17083], geometric expressiveness [arxiv_2411.14974] [arxiv_2401.06003], dynamic scene modeling [arxiv_2405.17891], scene manipulation [arxiv_2405.17811], sparse-view robustness [arxiv_2411.03637], and physics-based appearance modeling [arxiv_2412.09680] each represent a distinct axis along which the community has sought to extend and improve upon the original formulation. These directions are not independent: improvements in geometric representation bear on the quality of dynamic reconstruction; sparse-view robustness is closely tied to the structural regularization of Gaussian positions; and memory efficiency is a prerequisite for deployment in the resource-constrained settings where manipulation and dynamic synthesis are most practically relevant. The breadth and interconnectedness of these research threads underscore both the vitality of 3DGS as a research area and the substantial work that remains to be done before the full potential of explicit Gaussian-based scene representations can be realized.

### Evidence theme 3

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for explicit scene representation and novel view synthesis has generated substantial scholarly interest, both in understanding its foundational principles and in addressing the practical limitations that constrain its deployment. This subsection surveys the core literature surrounding 3DGS, situating it within the broader landscape of neural scene representations, examining its key advantages over predecessor methods, and cataloguing the primary challenges—particularly those relating to memory, storage, and generalization—that have motivated a growing body of follow-on research.

To appreciate the significance of 3DGS, it is instructive to first consider the neural radiance field (NeRF) paradigm from which it partly departs. NeRF-based methods represent 3D scenes implicitly through coordinate-based multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) that map spatial coordinates and viewing directions to volumetric density and color values. While this implicit formulation proved remarkably capable of capturing fine geometric detail and view-dependent appearance, it carries a severe computational burden: rendering a single view requires querying the MLP hundreds of times per ray, making real-time performance on commodity hardware essentially unattainable in the original formulation [arxiv_2103.14645]. Efforts to mitigate this bottleneck led to hybrid representations such as the Sparse Neural Radiance Grid (SNeRG), which precomputes and stores, or "bakes," a trained NeRF into a sparse voxel grid with learned feature vectors, thereby enabling real-time rendering while preserving much of NeRF's capacity for fine detail and view-dependent effects [arxiv_2103.14645, ss_478166]. Although such baking strategies represent meaningful progress, they remain fundamentally tied to the implicit, volumetric rendering pipeline and do not fully escape the memory and computational constraints inherent to that paradigm.

3D Gaussian Splatting addresses these limitations through a fundamentally different representational philosophy. Rather than encoding a scene implicitly within network weights, 3DGS represents scenes explicitly as collections of millions of learnable 3D Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients encoding view-dependent color [arxiv_2401.03890]. Crucially, rendering is accomplished not through volumetric ray marching but through a differentiable rasterization pipeline that projects and splats these Gaussians onto the image plane, achieving real-time rendering speeds that NeRF-based methods struggle to match even with baking optimizations [arxiv_2502.19457]. This explicit representation also confers a degree of editability that implicit models lack: individual Gaussians can, in principle, be manipulated, added, or removed, opening avenues for scene editing and interactive applications that are difficult to realize with coordinate-based MLPs [arxiv_2401.03890]. Comprehensive surveys of the field have characterized 3DGS as a potential game-changer for the next generation of 3D reconstruction and representation technologies, noting that its combination of rendering speed and editability distinguishes it sharply from mainstream implicit neural models [arxiv_2401.03890, arxiv_2502.19457].

Despite these compelling advantages, 3DGS is not without significant drawbacks, and the literature has been candid in identifying them. The most persistently cited limitation is the substantial memory and storage footprint that arises from maintaining millions of Gaussian primitives, each carrying a non-trivial number of parameters. As noted across multiple surveys and empirical studies, this storage burden poses serious challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices such as mobile platforms or embedded systems [arxiv_2502.19457, arxiv_2311.13681]. The contrast with NeRF is instructive here: while NeRF's implicit representation is computationally expensive at inference time, its storage requirements are comparatively modest, since the scene is encoded in network weights rather than in an explicit point cloud. 3DGS inverts this trade-off, achieving fast rendering at the cost of large storage, and a significant strand of the literature has consequently focused on compression and compactness as first-order research priorities.

Work on compact 3D Gaussian representations has pursued two complementary strategies: reducing the number of Gaussian primitives required to faithfully represent a scene, and more efficiently encoding the parameters of those primitives that remain. Compact 3D Gaussian Representation for Radiance Field [arxiv_2311.13681] exemplifies the first strategy, placing explicit emphasis on minimizing the Gaussian count while preserving rendering fidelity. The underlying motivation is that the original 3DGS formulation tends to spawn redundant or unnecessary Gaussians during its adaptive densification procedure, and that principled pruning and compaction can substantially reduce memory requirements without proportionate losses in visual quality. A complementary approach is taken by Compact 3D Scene Representation via Self-Organizing Gaussian Grids [arxiv_2312.13299], which reorganizes Gaussian parameters into a 2D grid structure that exploits local homogeneity and perceptual redundancies present in natural scenes. By arranging parameters so that spatially or perceptually similar Gaussians are co-located within the grid, this method enables aggressive compression through standard image or grid compression techniques, achieving drastic reductions in storage while maintaining competitive rendering quality. Together, these works illustrate that the storage problem in 3DGS is tractable through structural reorganization and redundancy exploitation, and they have helped establish compression as one of the most active research fronts in the field [arxiv_2502.19457].

Beyond static scene reconstruction, the literature has also explored the extension of 3DGS to dynamic scenes, where the representational and computational challenges are compounded by the need to model temporal deformation. NeRF-based approaches to dynamic view synthesis typically learn deformations implicitly, which, as noted in the literature, means that the learned deformation fields are not necessarily geometrically coherent and can produce unsatisfactory results for complex motions [arxiv_2404.06270]. The explicit nature of 3DGS offers a potential remedy: because the scene is represented as a collection of discrete Gaussian primitives, geometric constraints can be more directly incorporated into the deformation model. The 3D Geometry-aware Deformable Gaussian Splatting method [arxiv_2404.06270] exploits this property by optimizing each Gaussian to move and rotate over time while explicitly extracting and integrating 3D geometry features to enforce geometric coherence during deformation. This approach demonstrates that the explicit representational substrate of 3DGS is not merely advantageous for static reconstruction but also provides a principled foundation for dynamic scene modeling that implicit methods find difficult to match.

Another challenge that has received growing attention is the performance of 3DGS under sparse input conditions. Like NeRF, 3DGS was originally designed and evaluated under the assumption of dense, well-distributed input views, and its performance degrades when only a small number of training images are available. The tendency to overfit to training views in the absence of sufficient multi-view constraints is a recognized failure mode [arxiv_2409.14316]. MVPGS [arxiv_2409.14316] addresses this by leveraging learning-based multi-view stereo to improve geometric initialization and by introducing forward-warping-based appearance constraints that regularize the optimization under sparse-view conditions. This work situates 3DGS within the broader few-shot novel view synthesis literature, noting that while 3DGS achieves real-time high-quality rendering with its explicit point-based representation, it inherits the overfitting vulnerabilities of NeRF when input data is scarce. The comparison is instructive: both paradigms require careful regularization strategies when training data is limited, but the explicit nature of 3DGS means that geometric initialization quality has a more direct and interpretable impact on final rendering quality than in implicit models.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here traces a coherent arc from the limitations of implicit neural representations, through the development of 3DGS as a faster and more editable alternative, to the identification and partial resolution of 3DGS's own limitations in storage, dynamic modeling, and sparse-view generalization. The surveys by [arxiv_2401.03890] and [arxiv_2502.19457] provide the broadest synthetic overviews, situating individual contributions within the larger trajectory of the field and identifying compression and scalability as the most pressing open problems. More focused works such as [arxiv_2311.13681] and [arxiv_2312.13299] demonstrate concrete progress on the storage front through complementary strategies of primitive reduction and structural reorganization. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2404.06270] and [arxiv_2409.14316] extend the 3DGS framework into more challenging settings—dynamic scenes and sparse inputs, respectively—revealing both the flexibility of the Gaussian primitive representation and the additional constraints needed to make it robust. The contrast with NeRF-based methods, including baking approaches such as SNeRG [arxiv_2103.14645, ss_478166], remains a recurring analytical thread throughout this literature, underscoring that 3DGS does not simply supersede its predecessors but rather occupies a distinct position in the trade-off space between rendering speed, storage efficiency, editability, and generalization.

### Evidence theme 4

The evolution of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has prompted a rich body of research aimed at extending, refining, and addressing the limitations of the original formulation. While the foundational approach demonstrated compelling advantages over Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) in terms of rendering speed and image fidelity, subsequent work has systematically identified and tackled a range of challenges spanning sparse-view reconstruction, geometric accuracy, representational expressiveness, memory efficiency, and the relaxation of preprocessing requirements. This subsection reviews these developments, situating each contribution within the broader trajectory of 3DGS research and drawing comparisons where the evidence supports them.

One of the most practically significant limitations of the original 3DGS framework is its dependence on dense input views. When only a small number of training images are available, the method is prone to overfitting and produces degraded reconstructions, as the Gaussian primitives lack sufficient multi-view constraints to converge to accurate geometry. Two complementary lines of work have emerged to address this sparse-view problem. The first, proposed in [arxiv_2410.11080], introduces a depth-aware variant of 3DGS specifically designed for few-shot novel view synthesis. The authors incorporate monocular depth prediction as a geometric prior, coupling it with a scale-invariant depth loss to constrain the 3D structure when only a handful of input views are available. They additionally restrict color modeling to lower-order spherical harmonics, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting to appearance details that cannot be reliably inferred from limited observations. Crucially, the authors identify a subtle but consequential issue with the standard densification procedure: the periodic removal of low-opacity splats, intended to prune redundant primitives, leads to an excessively sparse point cloud under few-shot conditions, ultimately harming rendering quality. Their solution is to retain all splats throughout training, yielding a denser and more complete representation. The second approach, CoR-GS [arxiv_2405.12110], addresses sparse-view overfitting from a fundamentally different angle, introducing a co-regularization framework. The key insight is that when two independent 3D Gaussian radiance fields are trained on the same sparse set of views, the stochastic nature of the densification process causes them to diverge in ways that are diagnostic of reconstruction quality. The authors formalize this observation by quantifying point disagreement and rendering disagreement between the two fields, demonstrating a negative correlation between these disagreements and reconstruction accuracy. This allows inaccurate regions to be identified without access to ground-truth supervision, and the method suppresses these regions through co-regularization. Comparing the two approaches, [arxiv_2410.11080] relies on external monocular depth priors to inject geometric knowledge, while [arxiv_2405.12110] derives its regularization signal entirely from the internal consistency of two simultaneously trained models, making the latter self-supervised in a meaningful sense. Both strategies, however, converge on the shared diagnosis that standard 3DGS training dynamics are ill-suited to sparse-view settings and require explicit intervention.

A related but distinct challenge concerns scenes where certain regions are poorly covered not merely due to sparse viewpoints but due to structural occlusions or limited camera trajectories. [arxiv_2506.06462] proposes an elegant solution grounded in the observation that real-world environments frequently contain repetitive structural elements, such as windows, columns, or tiles. The method segments repeated instances within a 3DGS reconstruction, registers them into a common coordinate frame, and enables information sharing across instances. This allows well-reconstructed instances to inform the geometry and appearance of poorly observed ones, effectively using scene-level redundancy as a form of self-supervision. The approach also accounts for appearance variations across nominally identical instances, acknowledging that real repetitions are rarely perfectly uniform. This work is conceptually adjacent to the sparse-view methods discussed above in that it addresses incomplete coverage, but it does so through a scene-level structural prior rather than through depth supervision or model-level co-regularization.

Beyond the sparse-view problem, a persistent concern in 3DGS research is the tension between rendering quality and geometric accuracy. The original formulation optimizes Gaussian parameters primarily for photometric fidelity, with no explicit constraint on the underlying surface geometry. This can result in visually plausible renderings that nonetheless correspond to geometrically inconsistent or noisy surfaces. GSDF [arxiv_2403.16964] addresses this by proposing a dual-branch architecture that jointly trains a 3DGS representation and a neural implicit surface (SDF) model. The two branches are coupled through mutual guidance: the SDF provides geometric constraints that regularize the distribution of Gaussian primitives, while the Gaussian representation contributes high-fidelity appearance information that benefits the implicit surface. This bidirectional coupling is designed to overcome the limitations of either representation in isolation, as pure volumetric rendering methods tend to neglect surface geometry while pure implicit surface methods often sacrifice rendering quality. The result is a system that achieves improvements on both fronts simultaneously. This dual-branch philosophy stands in contrast to the approach taken in [arxiv_2406.02720], which proposes a more targeted modification to the Gaussian primitive itself. Rather than augmenting 3DGS with an entirely separate geometric representation, [arxiv_2406.02720] introduces 3D Half-Gaussian (3D-HGS) kernels, which replace the symmetric Gaussian kernel with an asymmetric half-Gaussian formulation. This modification is motivated by the observation that standard 3D Gaussians struggle to represent sharp shape and color discontinuities, as their smooth, symmetric profiles are poorly suited to modeling abrupt transitions. The half-Gaussian kernel is presented as a plug-and-play replacement that can be integrated into existing 3DGS pipelines without compromising rendering speed, and experiments demonstrate state-of-the-art rendering quality. The contrast between GSDF and 3D-HGS is instructive: the former addresses geometric limitations through architectural augmentation with a complementary representation, while the latter addresses them through a principled modification of the core primitive, reflecting a broader methodological divide in the field between system-level and primitive-level interventions.

The question of representational expressiveness is further explored in [arxiv_2511.18873], which argues that the capacity of 3DGS is fundamentally constrained by its reliance on 3D Gaussian kernels to model local scene variations. While prior work had proposed augmenting individual Gaussian primitives with per-splat textures to increase expressiveness, [arxiv_2511.18873] observes that such approaches are primarily effective for dense novel view synthesis with a reduced primitive count and tend to underperform in more general reconstruction scenarios. The proposed Neural Texture Splatting framework aims to achieve consistent improvements across a broader range of tasks, including not only novel view synthesis but also geometry reconstruction and dynamic scene modeling. By embedding richer per-primitive representations, the method seeks to decouple the number of primitives from the representational capacity of the model, allowing high-quality reconstruction without requiring an excessive number of Gaussians. This concern with the trade-off between primitive count and quality connects directly to the memory and storage challenges addressed by [arxiv_2408.03822].

The issue of computational and storage overhead is one of the most practically consequential limitations of 3DGS at scale. The method's reliance on large numbers of Gaussian primitives to maintain rendering fidelity translates into substantial memory requirements, limiting deployment on resource-constrained platforms and complicating the extension to dynamic scenes. [arxiv_2408.03822] addresses this through two complementary strategies: reducing the number of Gaussian points without sacrificing rendering quality, and compressing the attributes associated with each Gaussian, including view-dependent color and covariance. The authors propose a learnable mask strategy to selectively prune Gaussians, enabling the model to identify and discard redundant primitives in a data-driven manner. Importantly, the work extends these compression techniques to dynamic 3D scenes, demonstrating applicability beyond static reconstruction. The relationship between [arxiv_2408.03822] and [arxiv_2511.18873] is particularly noteworthy: while the former seeks to reduce the number of primitives through pruning and compression, the latter seeks to increase the expressiveness of each primitive through richer per-splat representations. Together, they reflect a convergent strategy of doing more with fewer, better-characterized primitives, though they approach this goal from opposite directions.

Finally, a foundational assumption of the original 3DGS pipeline that has received critical scrutiny is its dependence on pre-computed camera poses, typically obtained through Structure-from-Motion (SfM) preprocessing via tools such as COLMAP. This requirement introduces a significant practical bottleneck, as SfM can be computationally expensive, prone to failure in textureless or repetitive environments, and unavailable in online or streaming settings. [arxiv_2312.07504] proposes COLMAP-Free 3DGS, which eliminates this dependency by leveraging the explicit point cloud representation of 3DGS alongside the temporal continuity of input video streams. Rather than processing all frames simultaneously with pre-computed poses, the method processes frames sequentially, progressively growing the Gaussian set one frame at a time. This sequential, incremental approach allows camera poses and scene geometry to be jointly optimized without any SfM preprocessing. The authors argue that the explicit geometric representation of 3DGS, in contrast to the implicit representations used in NeRF-based pose-free methods, provides more tractable optimization landscapes for simultaneous pose and structure estimation. This work thus addresses a prerequisite limitation of the entire 3DGS pipeline rather than a limitation of the rendering or reconstruction quality per se, and its contribution is therefore orthogonal to many of the other works reviewed here.

Taken together, these contributions reveal a research landscape in which the original 3DGS framework is being systematically extended along multiple axes: robustness to sparse and incomplete observations [arxiv_2410.11080, arxiv_2405.12110, arxiv_2506.06462], geometric accuracy [arxiv_2403.16964, arxiv_2406.02720], representational expressiveness [arxiv_2511.18873], computational efficiency [arxiv_2408.03822], and independence from preprocessing pipelines [arxiv_2312.07504]. The diversity of these directions reflects both the breadth of 3DGS's applicability and the depth of the challenges that remain in realizing its full potential across real-world deployment scenarios.

The preceding synthesis has traced the evolution of 3DGS along several well-defined axes, yet a number of equally consequential research directions remain to be examined. Among these, the challenge of rendering view-dependent effects with physical fidelity has attracted sustained attention, motivating a family of methods that augment the standard spherical-harmonic color model with more principled illumination representations. The original 3DGS formulation encodes view-dependent appearance through low-degree spherical harmonics attached to each Gaussian primitive, a choice that prioritizes computational simplicity but sacrifices the ability to model high-frequency specular reflections and complex lighting interactions. [arxiv_2408.03753] addresses this limitation directly by proposing a factorized tensorial illumination model, termed 3iGS, which replaces the per-Gaussian spherical harmonic optimization with a structured, scene-level illumination representation. By factorizing the illumination tensor, the method achieves improved rendering quality for view-dependent effects without incurring the full cost of per-primitive high-degree harmonic expansions. A related but distinct approach is taken in [arxiv_2410.04974], which introduces a six-dimensional spatial-angular Gaussian representation to more faithfully capture directional radiance variation. This 6DGS formulation extends the standard three-dimensional spatial Gaussian with an angular component, enabling the model to represent view-dependent effects as a continuous function over the joint spatial-angular domain rather than as a discrete per-primitive attribute. The contrast between these two approaches is instructive: 3iGS achieves expressiveness through structured scene-level factorization, while 6DGS achieves it through an enriched per-primitive parameterization. Both strategies, however, share the underlying motivation of moving beyond the representational ceiling imposed by low-degree spherical harmonics, and both demonstrate that the standard 3DGS color model constitutes a meaningful bottleneck for scenes with complex illumination.

The question of physically grounded appearance modeling extends further into the domain of inverse rendering, where the goal is not merely to synthesize novel views but to disentangle scene geometry, surface materials, and illumination from multi-view observations. This decomposition is essential for applications such as relighting, material editing, and physically based simulation. [arxiv_2412.09680] approaches this problem within a NeRF framework informed by physics-based rendering theory, jointly estimating geometry, materials, and illumination under a principled image formation model. The authors explicitly identify the failure of most NeRF and 3DGS methods to separate view-dependent appearance from underlying material properties as a key limitation, and their PBR-NeRF framework is designed to address this gap. While the method operates within the NeRF paradigm rather than the 3DGS paradigm, its framing of the inverse rendering problem is directly relevant to the 3DGS community, as the same material-illumination entanglement affects Gaussian-based representations. The broader implication is that achieving physically accurate rendering from 3DGS will likely require incorporating similar disentanglement mechanisms, a direction that remains comparatively underexplored relative to the extensive work on rendering speed and geometric fidelity.

Alongside these advances in appearance modeling, the robotics community has emerged as a particularly active consumer and contributor to 3DGS research, motivated by the method's combination of real-time rendering, explicit scene representation, and differentiable optimization. [arxiv_2410.12262] provides a systematic survey of 3DGS applications in robotics, cataloguing its use across tasks including simultaneous localization and mapping, scene understanding, manipulation planning, and navigation. The survey observes that the explicit radiance field representation of 3DGS confers significant advantages over NeRF-based alternatives in robotics contexts, where the ability to directly query and manipulate scene geometry is often as important as rendering quality. The differentiable rendering pipeline further enables gradient-based optimization of robot behavior with respect to scene appearance, opening avenues for learning-based approaches that were previously impractical. However, the survey also identifies persistent challenges, including the computational cost of real-time Gaussian updates in dynamic environments, the sensitivity of the representation to sensor noise and calibration errors, and the difficulty of maintaining consistent representations across large-scale or long-horizon tasks. These observations connect directly to the pose-free and sparse-view challenges discussed earlier, as robotic platforms frequently operate under conditions of limited viewpoint coverage and imperfect sensor calibration.

The manipulation and editing of 3DGS representations constitutes another active research frontier, driven by the demand for interactive content creation tools that can operate on photorealistic 3D assets. [arxiv_2405.17811] addresses this challenge by proposing Mani-GS, a framework that binds 3D Gaussians to a triangular mesh to enable controllable manipulation. The key insight is that while 3DGS excels at photorealistic rendering, it lacks the structured geometric scaffold that mesh-based representations provide for articulated deformation and user-directed editing. By coupling Gaussians to mesh vertices and faces, Mani-GS inherits the controllability of mesh-based manipulation while retaining the rendering quality of the Gaussian representation. This hybrid strategy is conceptually related to the dual-representation approaches discussed in the context of geometric reconstruction, but is motivated by editability rather than accuracy. The work highlights a recurring tension in the 3DGS literature between the flexibility of the explicit Gaussian representation and its lack of topological structure, which complicates operations such as surface deformation, segmentation, and semantic editing that are natural in mesh-based pipelines.

Dynamic scene reconstruction represents perhaps the most demanding extension of the 3DGS framework, requiring the representation to model not only static geometry and appearance but also their temporal evolution. [arxiv_2404.06270] proposes a geometry-aware deformable Gaussian splatting method that addresses a key limitation of prior dynamic 3DGS approaches: the tendency to learn deformations in a purely implicit manner that ignores the underlying 3D scene geometry. By incorporating geometric constraints into the deformation field, the method produces temporally coherent reconstructions that better respect the physical structure of the scene. [arxiv_2405.17891] approaches the dynamic reconstruction problem from a complementary angle, focusing on refining the Gaussian representation itself to improve both rendering quality and memory efficiency in dynamic settings. The authors observe that the naive extension of static 3DGS to dynamic scenes leads to a multiplicative increase in memory consumption, as a separate set of Gaussians must in principle be maintained for each time step. Their refined representation addresses this through structured temporal parameterization that amortizes the cost across frames. Together, these works illustrate the dual challenge of dynamic 3DGS: achieving geometric coherence across time while managing the combinatorial growth in representational complexity that temporal extension entails.

The compression of 3DGS representations has emerged as a dedicated subfield, reflecting the practical necessity of deploying Gaussian-based models on bandwidth-limited and storage-constrained platforms. [arxiv_2502.19457] provides a comprehensive survey of compression methods for 3DGS, organizing the landscape into approaches based on pruning, quantization, coding, and neural field hybridization. The survey identifies a consistent trade-off between compression ratio and rendering quality, and observes that the most effective methods tend to combine multiple complementary strategies rather than relying on any single technique. [arxiv_2312.04564] contributes to this space through the EAGLES framework, which proposes efficient accelerated 3D Gaussians with lightweight encodings, achieving significant reductions in both training time and storage footprint through quantization-aware optimization. [arxiv_2312.13299] takes a structurally different approach by organizing Gaussian parameters into a 2D grid with local coherence, enabling the application of standard image compression codecs to the resulting parameter maps. [arxiv_2311.13681] similarly targets compact representation through a combination of Gaussian pruning and attribute compression, demonstrating that a substantial fraction of Gaussians in a trained model are redundant and can be removed without perceptible quality loss. [arxiv_2405.17083] extends this line of work by proposing factorized coordinates and representations, decomposing the Gaussian parameter space into lower-dimensional factors that can be stored and transmitted more efficiently. The convergence of these efforts reflects a shared recognition that the storage costs of 3DGS, while more tractable than those of volumetric NeRF representations, remain a meaningful barrier to deployment at scale, particularly on mobile and embedded platforms.

Rendering efficiency at inference time has been addressed through several complementary strategies beyond compression. [arxiv_2412.00578] identifies two specific inefficiencies in the standard 3DGS rasterization pipeline—redundant pixel coverage and redundant primitive evaluation—and proposes targeted algorithmic interventions to address each. The resulting Speedy-Splat framework achieves substantial improvements in rendering throughput without sacrificing reconstruction quality, demonstrating that careful engineering of the rasterization pipeline can yield gains that are orthogonal to those achievable through representation-level compression. [arxiv_2410.17932] addresses the specific demands of virtual reality rendering, where the combination of high resolution, stereoscopic display, and strict latency requirements creates a particularly challenging efficiency target. The proposed VR-Splatting framework leverages foveated rendering, concentrating computational resources on the gaze-directed region of the display while using sparser representations in the periphery, and combines 3DGS with neural point rendering to balance quality and performance across the visual field. These application-specific efficiency optimizations complement the more general compression and pruning strategies surveyed in [arxiv_2502.19457], and together they suggest that the path to practical 3DGS deployment will require co-optimization of representation, algorithm, and application context.

A further dimension of the 3DGS research landscape concerns the exploration of alternative primitive geometries that may offer advantages over the standard Gaussian kernel in specific reconstruction scenarios. [arxiv_2411.14974] proposes 3D Convex Splatting, which replaces Gaussian primitives with smooth convex shapes, arguing that convex primitives are better suited to representing flat surfaces and sharp edges that are common in man-made environments. The authors demonstrate that convex splatting achieves competitive rendering quality with a reduced primitive count in scenes with strong geometric regularity. [arxiv_2505.19175] takes a more radical departure by revisiting the triangle as the fundamental rendering primitive, developing a differentiable renderer that optimizes triangles via end-to-end gradients while retaining the adaptive density properties of splat-based representations. This Triangle Splatting approach is motivated by the observation that triangles, as the dominant primitive in classical computer graphics, offer well-understood geometric properties and efficient hardware support that Gaussian primitives lack. [arxiv_2401.06003] proposes TRIPS, a trilinear point splatting method that addresses specific artifacts of 3DGS—including blurring and cloudy appearance in highly detailed scenes—through a modified splatting formulation that improves high-frequency detail preservation. [arxiv_2510.08491] introduces splattable neural primitives, which seek to combine the expressiveness of neural radiance fields with the rendering efficiency of splatting by embedding learned neural features within each primitive. This hybrid formulation is conceptually related to the neural texture approach of [arxiv_2511.18873] but differs in its emphasis on maintaining the full volumetric rendering semantics of NeRF within a splatting framework. Collectively, these alternative primitive proposals reflect a broader questioning of whether the Gaussian kernel, chosen in the original 3DGS work primarily for its mathematical convenience and differentiability, is in fact the optimal primitive for general scene reconstruction, and suggest that the field has not yet converged on a definitive answer.

The relationship between 3DGS and its NeRF predecessors merits explicit consideration in light of the breadth of extensions reviewed here. [arxiv_2210.00379] provides a comprehensive review of the NeRF literature that has been updated to account for the emergence of Gaussian splatting, and its comparative analysis highlights both the continuities and discontinuities between the two paradigms. The survey observes that 3DGS has largely supplanted NeRF as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis research in terms of community interest and publication volume, driven primarily by its real-time rendering capability and more tractable optimization landscape. However, it also notes that NeRF retains advantages in certain regimes, particularly for scenes with complex volumetric effects, semi-transparent materials, and unbounded outdoor environments where the discrete Gaussian representation can struggle. [arxiv_2502.19318] engages directly with the theoretical relationship between the two frameworks, asking whether the approximate volumetric rendering used in 3DGS—which departs from the principled ray-marching formulation of NeRF—is in fact necessary for achieving high visual quality. The authors find that the approximations introduced in 3DGS are largely benign for typical novel view synthesis benchmarks, but that they can introduce systematic errors in geometrically complex scenes, suggesting that more principled rendering formulations may be warranted for applications requiring geometric accuracy. [arxiv_2509.17083] proposes a hybrid radiance field approach that combines the memory efficiency of neural fields with the rendering speed of 3DGS, addressing the complementary weaknesses of each paradigm. This hybridization strategy, which seeks to inherit the strengths of both NeRF and 3DGS while mitigating their respective limitations, represents a recurring theme in the recent literature and suggests that the long-term trajectory of the field may involve increasingly tight integration between implicit and explicit representations rather than the wholesale replacement of one by the other.
