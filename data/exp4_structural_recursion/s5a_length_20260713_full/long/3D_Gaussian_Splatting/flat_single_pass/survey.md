# 3D Gaussian Splatting: A Comprehensive Literature Review

## 1. Introduction and Motivation

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for novel view synthesis and scene reconstruction represents one of the most consequential developments in computer vision and computer graphics in recent years. To appreciate the significance of this development, it is necessary to situate it within the broader trajectory of radiance field research, examine the foundational principles that distinguish it from its predecessors, and then trace the rapid proliferation of methods that have extended, refined, and challenged its core assumptions. This section undertakes that task, organizing the discussion around the landscape of 3DGS research: the foundational method itself, its relationship to Neural Radiance Fields, the rendering approximations it employs, the memory and storage challenges it introduces, the alternative primitive representations it has inspired, the integration of 3DGS with surface reconstruction and geometry estimation, the extension to dynamic scenes, the development of editing and manipulation capabilities, and the various strategies that have been proposed to improve its behavior under difficult acquisition conditions such as sparse views, blurry inputs, and absent camera poses.

The practical motivation for this line of research is substantial. Applications ranging from augmented and virtual reality content creation, autonomous driving scene understanding, cultural heritage digitization, robotic navigation, and cinematic visual effects all require the ability to reconstruct and render three-dimensional scenes from collections of two-dimensional images. The ideal representation for such applications would combine photorealistic rendering quality, real-time rendering speed, compact storage, geometric accuracy, and robustness to imperfect or incomplete input data. No single method has yet achieved all of these goals simultaneously, but the trajectory of the field since the introduction of 3DGS suggests that the community is making rapid progress toward this ideal. Understanding the current state of the art, the trade-offs that different methods make, and the open problems that remain is therefore of both scientific and practical importance.

## 2. The Pre-3DGS Landscape: Neural Radiance Fields and Their Limitations

Before 3DGS arrived, the dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis was Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), introduced in 2020 and subsequently extended in dozens of directions [arxiv_2210.00379]. NeRF encodes a scene as a continuous volumetric function parameterized by a multilayer perceptron (MLP), mapping spatial coordinates and viewing directions to density and radiance values. Rendering proceeds by casting rays through the scene and integrating sampled contributions along each ray, a process grounded in classical volume rendering theory. The photorealistic results achievable by NeRF were remarkable, but the computational cost was severe: rendering a single image required querying the MLP hundreds of times per ray, making real-time display rates essentially unattainable on standard hardware [arxiv_2103.14645].

The NeRF literature, as comprehensively surveyed in [arxiv_2210.00379], documents a sustained and multi-pronged effort to overcome these limitations. Early acceleration strategies focused on caching or baking the trained NeRF into explicit data structures that could be queried more efficiently at inference time. The SNeRG approach [arxiv_2103.14645] exemplifies this direction: it precomputes and stores a trained NeRF as a Sparse Neural Radiance Grid with learned feature vectors, enabling real-time rendering on commodity hardware by eliminating the need for repeated MLP queries at inference time. The key insight of SNeRG is that the MLP, once trained, can be evaluated on a regular grid and the resulting feature volumes stored explicitly, so that rendering reduces to a lookup and a lightweight neural network evaluation rather than a full MLP forward pass. While such baking strategies demonstrated that real-time performance was achievable in principle, they introduced their own trade-offs in terms of storage and the fidelity of the precomputed representation [ss_478166]. The stored feature volumes are large, and the quality of the baked representation is bounded by the resolution of the grid and the expressiveness of the lightweight reconstruction network.

Beyond acceleration, the NeRF literature also addressed limitations in training speed, generalization across scenes, editability, and the handling of unbounded outdoor environments. Methods such as Instant-NGP demonstrated that hash-based spatial data structures could dramatically accelerate both training and rendering of neural radiance fields, reducing training times from hours to minutes. Mip-NeRF and its successors addressed aliasing artifacts by representing rays as cones rather than infinitesimally thin lines, improving quality at varying scales. NeRF-in-the-Wild extended the framework to handle varying illumination and transient occluders in uncontrolled capture conditions. Despite these advances, the fundamental bottleneck of implicit MLP-based representations—the need to evaluate a neural network for every sample along every ray—remained a persistent challenge, and the community increasingly recognized that a more fundamental departure from the implicit representation paradigm might be necessary to achieve the combination of quality, speed, and flexibility required for practical applications [arxiv_2210.00379].

It is against this backdrop that the foundational 3DGS paper [arxiv_2308.04079] must be understood. The transition from implicit to explicit representations was not abrupt; rather, it was the culmination of a gradual shift in the field toward hybrid and explicit representations that had been gaining momentum for several years. Point-based rendering methods, which represent scenes as collections of discrete points or surfels and render them by splatting each point onto the image plane, had a long history in computer graphics predating the deep learning era. The contribution of 3DGS was to combine the expressive power of learned, view-dependent appearance representations with the rendering efficiency of point-based splatting, and to do so within a fully differentiable framework that enabled end-to-end optimization from multi-view images. This synthesis of ideas from classical point-based rendering, neural scene representation, and differentiable rendering constitutes the foundational contribution of 3DGS and explains much of its subsequent impact.

## 3. The Foundational 3DGS Method

Kerbl and colleagues [arxiv_2308.04079] introduced three key elements that together enabled state-of-the-art visual quality at real-time rendering speeds exceeding 30 frames per second at 1080p resolution. First, the scene is initialized from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration via Structure-from-Motion and represented as a collection of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterized by a position (mean), a covariance matrix encoding shape and orientation, an opacity value, and view-dependent color coefficients expressed as spherical harmonics. The use of 3D Gaussians as primitives is motivated by their mathematical properties: they are differentiable, they can be projected onto the image plane analytically using an affine approximation of the perspective transformation, and they provide a smooth, continuous representation of local scene geometry and appearance that is well-suited to gradient-based optimization.

Second, an adaptive density control mechanism governs the growth and pruning of the Gaussian set during optimization. This mechanism monitors the gradient magnitudes of Gaussian positions and uses them to identify regions where the current set of Gaussians is insufficient to represent the scene accurately. In under-reconstructed regions, new Gaussians are added by cloning or splitting existing ones; in over-reconstructed regions or where Gaussians have become transparent, they are pruned. This adaptive densification strategy allows the representation to concentrate primitives where they are most needed, achieving a form of automatic level-of-detail that is driven by the reconstruction error rather than by hand-crafted heuristics.

Third, a tile-based rasterizer sorts and splats the Gaussians onto the image plane in a differentiable manner, enabling efficient gradient-based optimization and fast rendering. The rasterizer divides the image into tiles, assigns each Gaussian to the tiles it overlaps, sorts Gaussians within each tile by depth, and composites their contributions using alpha blending. This tile-based approach is highly parallelizable and maps efficiently onto modern GPU architectures, enabling the real-time rendering speeds that distinguish 3DGS from NeRF-based methods. The result is a method that preserves the desirable properties of continuous volumetric radiance fields for optimization purposes while achieving the rendering efficiency of rasterization-based pipelines [arxiv_2308.04079].

The combination of explicit representation, differentiable rasterization, and adaptive densification constitutes the defining contribution of 3DGS and explains much of the subsequent research activity it has generated. The explicit representation makes the method interpretable and editable in ways that implicit representations are not. The differentiable rasterizer enables end-to-end optimization from multi-view images without the need for explicit supervision of Gaussian parameters. The adaptive densification mechanism provides a principled way to allocate representational capacity where it is most needed, avoiding both under-fitting in complex regions and over-fitting in simple ones. Together, these elements create a framework that is both powerful and flexible, providing a strong foundation for the many extensions and refinements that have followed.

## 4. 3DGS and NeRF: Complementary Paradigms

The relationship between 3DGS and NeRF is not one of simple replacement but of complementary strengths and weaknesses. As surveyed in [arxiv_2403.11134], 3DGS and NeRF occupy different positions in the space of scene representation trade-offs. NeRF's implicit MLP representation is compact and continuous but slow to render and difficult to edit directly. 3DGS's explicit point-based representation is fast to render and amenable to direct manipulation but requires large numbers of primitives and correspondingly large memory footprints. The survey in [arxiv_2401.03890] frames this contrast in terms of the distinction between implicit neural models and explicit learnable representations, arguing that 3DGS's use of millions of learnable 3D Gaussians paired with a differentiable rendering algorithm makes it a potential game-changer for 3D reconstruction and representation, enabling unprecedented rendering speed and editability.

The comprehensive NeRF survey in [arxiv_2210.00379] documents how, following the introduction of 3DGS in August 2023, research interest shifted markedly toward Gaussian-based methods, with 3DGS overtaking NeRF-based approaches as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis in terms of community attention and publication volume. This shift reflects not only the practical advantages of 3DGS in terms of rendering speed but also the broader appeal of explicit representations for downstream applications such as scene editing, physics simulation, and integration with graphics pipelines. At the same time, the NeRF paradigm has not been abandoned; rather, the two approaches have increasingly been combined in hybrid methods that seek to capture the complementary strengths of both [arxiv_2509.17083, arxiv_2403.16964, arxiv_2411.01853].

The survey of [arxiv_2403.11134] classifies recent 3DGS methods by functionality into 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and downstream applications, providing a functional taxonomy that complements the thematic organization adopted here. This classification highlights the breadth of the 3DGS research landscape and the diversity of application domains that have been addressed. The survey of [arxiv_2401.03890] provides a systematic overview of the underlying principles and driving forces behind 3DGS, emphasizing its practical applicability and the range of applications it enables. Together, these surveys provide a valuable reference for understanding the current state of the field and the directions in which it is evolving.

## 5. Rendering Approximations and Their Consequences

A question that arises naturally from the design of 3DGS concerns the accuracy of its rendering approximations. While NeRF is grounded in a principled ray-marching approach to volumetric rendering, 3DGS employs a hybrid solution that combines elements of volume rendering and primitive rasterization, introducing several approximations relative to strict volumetric rendering theory [arxiv_2502.19318]. These approximations include the projection of 3D Gaussians onto the image plane using an affine approximation of the perspective transformation, the use of alpha compositing rather than full volumetric integration, and the tile-based sorting and culling of Gaussians.

The affine approximation of the perspective transformation is accurate near the center of the image but introduces distortions near the image boundaries, particularly for large Gaussians that span a significant portion of the field of view. The use of alpha compositing rather than full volumetric integration means that the rendering model does not account for the self-shadowing and multiple scattering effects that are captured by the full volume rendering equation. The tile-based sorting and culling of Gaussians introduces discretization artifacts at tile boundaries and may miss contributions from Gaussians that are partially occluded by other Gaussians within the same tile.

The work of [arxiv_2502.19318] presents an in-depth analysis of whether replacing these approximations with more principled volumetric rendering solutions improves the quality of 3DGS, finding that the relationship between rendering accuracy and visual quality is nuanced and that the approximations employed by 3DGS are in many cases sufficient or even beneficial for the optimization landscape. This finding is significant because it suggests that the success of 3DGS is not merely a consequence of its explicit representation but also reflects a fortuitous alignment between its rendering approximations and the requirements of gradient-based scene optimization. The approximations introduce a form of implicit regularization that prevents the optimization from finding degenerate solutions that would be permitted by a more accurate rendering model, and this regularization effect may be partly responsible for the high visual quality achieved by 3DGS in practice.

This insight has important implications for the design of future Gaussian-based rendering methods. It suggests that the goal of improving rendering accuracy should not be pursued in isolation from the optimization dynamics of the method, and that changes to the rendering model that improve accuracy in a physical sense may not necessarily improve the quality of the reconstructed scene. The analysis of [arxiv_2502.19318] provides a principled framework for evaluating such trade-offs and should inform future work on the rendering foundations of 3DGS.

## 6. Memory, Storage, and Compression

The memory and storage demands of 3DGS represent one of its most significant practical limitations and have motivated a substantial body of research. Because 3DGS represents scenes using millions of Gaussian primitives, each storing position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients, the total storage requirement for a single scene can reach hundreds of megabytes or more [arxiv_2502.19457]. This poses challenges for deployment on resource-constrained devices such as mobile phones, embedded systems, and edge computing platforms, and also complicates streaming and transmission of 3DGS scenes. The survey in [arxiv_2502.19457] provides a comprehensive overview of compression methods for 3DGS, organizing them according to whether they target the number of Gaussian primitives, the precision of Gaussian attributes, or the structural organization of the representation.

### 6.1 Primitive Reduction Through Pruning and Densification Control

One approach to reducing the storage footprint of 3DGS is to reduce the number of Gaussian primitives through principled pruning and densification control. The EAGLES method [arxiv_2312.04564] introduces quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements and a coarse-to-fine training strategy for faster and more stable optimization, combined with a pruning stage that yields scene representations with fewer Gaussians and consequently faster training and rendering. The coarse-to-fine strategy begins with a small number of large Gaussians that capture the coarse structure of the scene and progressively refines the representation by splitting Gaussians in regions where the reconstruction error is high. This approach is more principled than the default densification mechanism of vanilla 3DGS, which is driven purely by gradient magnitudes and can produce an unnecessarily large number of small Gaussians in regions with high-frequency texture.

The Compact 3D Gaussian Representation work [arxiv_2311.13681] similarly emphasizes reducing the number of Gaussian points while maintaining high fidelity, placing emphasis on both point count reduction and attribute compression. The key insight of this work is that the default densification behavior of vanilla 3DGS tends to produce more Gaussians than are strictly necessary, because the gradient-driven densification criterion does not account for the redundancy between nearby Gaussians. By introducing a more conservative densification criterion and a more aggressive pruning strategy, [arxiv_2311.13681] achieves significant reductions in Gaussian count without sacrificing visual quality. The Compact 3D Gaussian Splatting for Static and Dynamic Radiance Fields paper [arxiv_2408.03822] extends this line of work to dynamic scenes, proposing a learnable mask strategy to reduce Gaussian counts without sacrificing performance and compressing view-dependent color and covariance attributes. These works collectively demonstrate that the default densification behavior of vanilla 3DGS tends to produce more Gaussians than are strictly necessary and that principled pruning and compression can recover much of the storage overhead without significant quality loss.

### 6.2 Structural Reorganization and Spatial Coherence

A complementary approach is to reorganize the spatial structure of the Gaussian representation to exploit redundancy. The Compact 3D Scene Representation via Self-Organizing Gaussian Grids paper [arxiv_2312.13299] proposes organizing the parameters of 3DGS into a 2D grid with local homogeneity, exploiting the perceptual redundancies present in natural scenes to achieve drastic reductions in storage requirements. The key insight is that many permutations of Gaussian parameters can equivalently represent a given scene, and that organizing parameters into a spatially coherent grid enables efficient compression via standard image compression codecs. This approach achieves storage reductions without compromising visual quality during rendering, demonstrating that the structural organization of the Gaussian set is itself a lever for compression [arxiv_2312.13299].

The self-organizing grid approach is particularly elegant because it leverages the mature infrastructure of image compression—including codecs such as JPEG, WebP, and HEVC—to compress Gaussian parameters without requiring specialized compression algorithms. By mapping the Gaussian parameter space onto a 2D grid with local spatial coherence, the method creates a representation that is amenable to the same compression techniques that have been developed and optimized for natural images over decades. This connection between 3DGS compression and image compression opens up a rich set of possibilities for future work, including the use of learned image compression methods and the exploitation of temporal coherence in dynamic scenes.

### 6.3 Factorization-Based Compression

Factorization-based approaches offer another route to storage reduction. The F-3DGS method [arxiv_2405.17083] draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to represent and approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a factorized form, drastically reducing storage requirements while preserving image quality. By decomposing the Gaussian parameter space into lower-dimensional factors, F-3DGS achieves compact representations that can be stored and transmitted efficiently. This approach is conceptually related to the use of low-rank approximations in other areas of machine learning and signal processing, and its application to 3DGS demonstrates the generality of factorization as a compression strategy [arxiv_2405.17083].

The factorization approach is particularly well-suited to scenes with repetitive structure, where many Gaussians share similar parameter values and can be efficiently represented by a small number of shared factors. This connection between factorization-based compression and the structural properties of natural scenes suggests that more sophisticated factorization methods, such as non-negative matrix factorization or sparse coding, might yield further improvements in compression efficiency.

### 6.4 Hybrid Neural-Explicit Representations

The HyRF method [arxiv_2509.17083] takes a different approach by combining explicit Gaussians with grid-based neural fields in a hybrid representation. HyRF decomposes the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians storing only critical high-frequency parameters and grid-based neural fields that predict remaining properties, addressing the memory overhead of per-Gaussian parameters while avoiding the difficulty that purely neural compression methods have in capturing high-frequency spatial variations. This hybrid strategy reflects a broader trend in the field toward representations that combine the complementary strengths of explicit and implicit components [arxiv_2509.17083].

The contrast between HyRF and purely explicit methods like vanilla 3DGS illustrates the trade-off between the interpretability and editability of explicit representations and the compactness of neural ones. Purely explicit representations are easy to interpret and edit but require large amounts of storage. Purely neural representations are compact but difficult to interpret and edit. Hybrid representations like HyRF seek to find a middle ground that captures the benefits of both paradigms, storing the most important information explicitly while delegating the remainder to a compact neural representation. This design philosophy is likely to be influential in future work on 3DGS compression and representation.

## 7. Alternative Splatting Primitives

Beyond compression, the limitations of the Gaussian primitive itself as a geometric building block have motivated the development of alternative splatting primitives. The choice of Gaussian ellipsoids as the basic primitive in 3DGS was motivated by their mathematical tractability and their ability to represent smooth, continuous local geometry. However, Gaussian ellipsoids have inherent limitations for representing scenes with sharp edges, flat surfaces, and discontinuous features, and these limitations have motivated the exploration of alternative primitives.

### 7.1 Convex Primitives

The 3D Convex Splatting (3DCS) method [arxiv_2411.14974] argues that 3D Gaussians present fundamental limitations for scene reconstruction: accurately capturing hard edges requires significantly increasing the number of Gaussians, creating a large memory footprint, and Gaussians struggle to represent flat surfaces because they are diffused in space and tend to disperse irregularly around actual surfaces without hand-crafted regularizers. To address these issues, 3DCS introduces 3D smooth convexes as primitives, which offer greater flexibility than Gaussians and can better represent flat surfaces and sharp edges. The smooth convex primitive generalizes the Gaussian ellipsoid by allowing the shape of the primitive to be more general than an ellipsoid while retaining the mathematical properties needed for differentiable rendering. This generalization enables 3DCS to represent a wider range of scene geometries with fewer primitives, reducing both storage requirements and rendering time compared to vanilla 3DGS on scenes with complex geometry [arxiv_2411.14974].

### 7.2 Half-Gaussian Kernels

The 3D Half-Gaussian (3D-HGS) approach [arxiv_2406.02720] takes a different tack, proposing half-Gaussian kernels as a plug-and-play solution to address shape and color discontinuities in standard 3DGS. By replacing symmetric Gaussian kernels with asymmetric half-Gaussian variants, 3D-HGS can better represent discontinuous features such as object boundaries and material transitions. The key insight is that a symmetric Gaussian kernel, by construction, assigns equal weight to both sides of a surface, which is inappropriate for representing sharp boundaries where the appearance changes discontinuously. A half-Gaussian kernel, by contrast, assigns weight only to one side of the surface, enabling a more accurate representation of discontinuities. This modification is simple to implement and compatible with the existing 3DGS optimization framework, making it a practical improvement that can be applied to a wide range of 3DGS-based methods. The 3D-HGS approach achieves state-of-the-art rendering quality without compromising speed [arxiv_2406.02720], demonstrating that targeted modifications to the primitive shape can yield significant quality improvements.

### 7.3 Screen-Space Pyramid Splatting

The TRIPS method [arxiv_2401.06003] combines ideas from Gaussian Splatting and the ADOP point-based rendering approach, rasterizing points into a screen-space image pyramid and using a lightweight neural reconstruction network to produce the final image. TRIPS addresses the blurring and cloudy artifacts that 3DGS exhibits when rendering highly detailed scenes, as well as the temporal instability and point cloud gap issues of ADOP, by leveraging trilinear interpolation in the screen-space pyramid to produce crisper images with better detail preservation [arxiv_2401.06003]. The screen-space pyramid representation provides a natural multi-scale structure that can capture scene details at multiple levels of resolution, and the lightweight neural reconstruction network can learn to combine these multi-scale features in a way that produces high-quality images. This approach is particularly well-suited to scenes with fine-grained texture and detail, where the limited resolution of individual Gaussian primitives would otherwise result in blurring artifacts.

### 7.4 Per-Splat Textures

The Neural Texture Splatting approach [arxiv_2511.18873] augments 3DGS with per-splat textures to enhance the representational capacity of individual Gaussian primitives, arguing that the use of 3D Gaussian kernels to model local variations is a fundamental bottleneck. By associating each Gaussian with a learnable texture that can capture sub-primitive detail, Neural Texture Splatting achieves concrete performance improvements over state-of-the-art 3DGS variants across a range of reconstruction tasks including novel view synthesis, geometry reconstruction, and dynamic scene reconstruction [arxiv_2511.18873]. The per-splat texture approach is conceptually related to the use of texture maps in traditional computer graphics, where a single geometric primitive can represent complex appearance variations through a texture lookup. By bringing this idea into the 3DGS framework, Neural Texture Splatting enables a more efficient use of representational capacity, concentrating detail where it is most needed rather than distributing it uniformly across all Gaussians.

## 8. Surface Reconstruction and Geometry Estimation

The relationship between 3DGS and surface reconstruction is another important dimension of the research landscape. While 3DGS was originally designed for novel view synthesis rather than geometry reconstruction, the explicit nature of its representation makes it a natural candidate for surface-oriented applications. However, the volumetric, diffuse nature of Gaussian primitives means that they do not naturally align with surfaces, and extracting clean meshes from a 3DGS reconstruction is non-trivial.

### 8.1 Dual-Branch Architectures

The GSDF method [arxiv_2403.16964] addresses this by introducing a dual-branch architecture that combines 3DGS with a neural implicit surface representation (Signed Distance Function), allowing the two representations to mutually constrain each other during optimization. The 3DGS branch provides fast, high-quality rendering while the SDF branch enforces geometric consistency, resulting in improved rendering quality and more accurate surface reconstruction compared to methods that rely on either representation alone [arxiv_2403.16964]. The mutual constraint between the two branches is implemented through a set of regularization losses that encourage the Gaussian primitives to align with the zero-level set of the SDF and the SDF to be consistent with the depth and normal information implied by the Gaussian primitives. This bidirectional coupling ensures that the two representations reinforce each other rather than diverging during optimization.

The GSDF approach reflects a broader trend in the field toward hybrid representations that combine the complementary strengths of explicit and implicit components. The explicit 3DGS branch provides fast rendering and direct geometric interpretability, while the implicit SDF branch provides smooth, continuous surface geometry that can be extracted as a mesh using standard algorithms such as Marching Cubes. The combination of these two representations in a single optimization framework enables a level of geometric accuracy and rendering quality that neither representation can achieve alone.

### 8.2 Kernel Regression and Continuous Scene Representations

The GVKF method [arxiv_2411.01853] takes a different approach, establishing a continuous scene representation based on discrete 3DGS through kernel regression. By treating the discrete Gaussian primitives as samples of a continuous kernel function defined over a voxel grid, GVKF achieves high-fidelity open scene surface reconstruction while retaining the fast rasterization of 3DGS [arxiv_2411.01853]. The kernel regression framework provides a principled way to interpolate between discrete Gaussian samples and produce a continuous representation that can be evaluated at arbitrary spatial locations. This continuous representation is particularly useful for surface reconstruction, where the goal is to extract a smooth, continuous surface from a discrete set of samples.

The GVKF approach also addresses a fundamental limitation of vanilla 3DGS for surface reconstruction: the fact that Gaussian primitives are volumetric rather than surface-based, meaning that they do not naturally define a surface boundary. By using kernel regression to produce a continuous density field from the discrete Gaussian samples, GVKF can extract surfaces as level sets of this density field, providing a more principled approach to surface reconstruction than the ad hoc methods that have been proposed for vanilla 3DGS. These works illustrate the tension between the rendering efficiency of 3DGS and the geometric precision required for surface reconstruction, and the various strategies that have been proposed to reconcile these competing demands.

## 9. Dynamic Scene Reconstruction

The challenge of dynamic scene reconstruction represents another major thread in the 3DGS literature. The original 3DGS method was designed for static scenes, and extending it to handle moving objects and deforming surfaces requires additional mechanisms to model temporal variation. The straightforward approach of training a separate 3DGS model for each frame is computationally prohibitive and does not exploit the temporal coherence of the scene, motivating the development of more efficient and principled approaches to dynamic 3DGS.

### 9.1 Deformable Gaussian Representations

The Refined 3D Gaussian Representation paper [arxiv_2405.17891] addresses this by using a deformable MLP network to capture the dynamic offset of Gaussian points over time, combined with hash encoding for color features, achieving high-quality dynamic scene reconstruction while managing memory usage. The deformable MLP takes as input the canonical position of each Gaussian and the current time step and outputs a displacement vector that is added to the canonical position to produce the deformed position. This approach is conceptually simple and computationally efficient, as it requires only a single forward pass through the deformable MLP to compute the deformed positions of all Gaussians at a given time step.

The 3D Geometry-aware Deformable Gaussian Splatting method [arxiv_2404.06270] takes a more principled approach, explicitly extracting 3D geometry features and integrating them into the learning of complex 3D deformations. The motivation is that existing NeRF-based deformation methods learn deformation implicitly and cannot incorporate 3D scene geometry, leading to geometrically incoherent deformations. By grounding the deformation model in explicit 3D geometry, this method achieves more geometrically consistent dynamic view synthesis and 3D dynamic reconstruction [arxiv_2404.06270]. The geometry features are extracted from the canonical 3DGS representation using a graph neural network that captures the local geometric context of each Gaussian, providing a rich representation of the scene structure that can guide the deformation model.

### 9.2 Compression for Dynamic Scenes

The Compact 3D Gaussian Splatting for Static and Dynamic Radiance Fields paper [arxiv_2408.03822] extends compression techniques to the dynamic setting, demonstrating that the memory challenges of 3DGS are compounded in the dynamic case and that principled compression is essential for practical deployment of dynamic 3DGS methods. In the dynamic setting, the number of Gaussian primitives must be sufficient to represent the scene at every time step, and the temporal variation of Gaussian parameters must be stored or modeled efficiently. The learnable mask strategy of [arxiv_2408.03822] addresses this by learning a binary mask that selects a subset of Gaussians to be active at each time step, reducing the effective number of Gaussians that must be rendered and stored. The compression of view-dependent color and covariance attributes further reduces the storage requirements of the dynamic representation, making it practical for deployment on resource-constrained devices.

## 10. Scene Editing and Manipulation

The problem of scene editing and manipulation is closely related to the explicit nature of the 3DGS representation. As noted in [arxiv_2403.11134], the explicit representation of 3DGS facilitates editing tasks in ways that implicit NeRF representations do not, because individual Gaussian primitives can be directly selected, moved, duplicated, or deleted. This property makes 3DGS a natural foundation for interactive scene editing applications, where users need to manipulate scene content in real time.

### 10.1 Mesh-Bound Gaussian Manipulation

The Mani-GS method [arxiv_2405.17811] exploits this property to enable manipulable photo-realistic rendering by binding 3DGS to a triangular mesh, allowing users to manipulate the mesh and have the Gaussian representation follow accordingly. This approach addresses the lack of effective means to manipulate 3DGS freely while maintaining rendering quality, which is identified as a key limitation of vanilla 3DGS for content creation applications [arxiv_2405.17811]. The binding between Gaussians and mesh vertices is implemented through a learned skinning function that maps mesh deformations to Gaussian displacements, ensuring that the Gaussian representation remains consistent with the mesh geometry under arbitrary deformations. The contrast with NeRF-based manipulation methods, which require long training and inference times and offer limited controllability, highlights the practical advantages of 3DGS's explicit representation for interactive editing workflows.

The Mani-GS approach is particularly well-suited to character animation applications, where the goal is to animate a 3DGS reconstruction of a person or object by driving it with a rigged mesh. By binding the Gaussian representation to the mesh skeleton, Mani-GS enables realistic animation of 3DGS reconstructions without the need for expensive re-optimization, making it practical for real-time applications such as virtual reality and video games.

### 10.2 Exploiting Repetitive Structure

The Splat and Replace method [arxiv_2506.06462] takes a different approach to scene improvement, leveraging the observation that real-world environments are often full of repetitive elements. By segmenting repeated instances in a 3DGS reconstruction, registering them together, and sharing information among instances, Splat and Replace improves the reconstruction of low-quality parts of the scene that suffer from poor coverage or occlusions. This method demonstrates that structural priors about scene content can be incorporated into the 3DGS optimization framework to compensate for limitations in the training data, a strategy that is complementary to the geometric and appearance priors employed by other methods [arxiv_2506.06462].

The Splat and Replace approach is particularly relevant for large-scale scene reconstruction, where it is common for some parts of the scene to be well-covered by the training images while others are poorly covered due to occlusions or limited camera viewpoints. By identifying and exploiting repetitive structure in the scene, the method can transfer information from well-covered instances to poorly-covered ones, improving the overall quality of the reconstruction without requiring additional training images.

## 11. Robustness to Challenging Acquisition Conditions

### 11.1 Sparse-View Reconstruction

The behavior of 3DGS under sparse input conditions has attracted considerable research attention, motivated by the practical reality that dense multi-view capture is often impractical. When only a few training views are available, 3DGS tends to overfit to the training views and produce poor novel view synthesis, because the underconstrained optimization can find degenerate solutions that explain the training images without generalizing to new viewpoints. The challenge is compounded by the fact that the adaptive densification mechanism of vanilla 3DGS is driven by gradient magnitudes, which are unreliable when the training set is small, leading to either under-densification in complex regions or over-densification in simple ones.

The SCGaussian method [arxiv_2411.03637] proposes a Structure Consistent Gaussian Splatting approach that uses matching priors to learn 3D consistent scene structure, optimizing both rendering geometry and the positions of Gaussian primitives. The key insight is that the high interdependence of Gaussian attributes makes it difficult to constrain the scene structure in vanilla 3DGS, and that a hybrid Gaussian representation with explicit structural constraints can overcome this limitation [arxiv_2411.03637]. The matching priors are derived from feature matching between training views, providing a geometric constraint that encourages the Gaussian primitives to align with the true 3D structure of the scene rather than finding degenerate solutions that explain the training images without generalizing.

The MVPGS method [arxiv_2409.14316] leverages learning-based Multi-view Stereo to enhance the quality of geometric initialization for 3DGS and proposes a forward-warping method for additional appearance constraints, addressing the tendency of 3DGS to overfit training views when input is sparse [arxiv_2409.14316]. The use of learning-based MVS for initialization is motivated by the observation that the quality of the initial point cloud has a strong influence on the final reconstruction quality, particularly in the sparse-view setting where the optimization has limited information to correct errors in the initialization. By using a learning-based MVS method that has been trained on large datasets of multi-view images, MVPGS can produce higher-quality initializations than the SfM-based approach used by vanilla 3DGS, providing a better starting point for the optimization.

The CoR-GS method [arxiv_2405.12110] introduces a co-regularization perspective for sparse-view 3DGS, training two 3D Gaussian radiance fields simultaneously and exploiting the disagreement between them as an unsupervised signal for identifying inaccurate reconstruction. The observation that two independently trained Gaussian radiance fields exhibit point disagreement and rendering disagreement that negatively correlates with reconstruction accuracy provides a principled basis for identifying and suppressing inaccurate reconstruction without access to ground-truth information [arxiv_2405.12110]. This approach is conceptually related to co-training and ensemble methods in machine learning, adapted to the specific challenges of sparse-view 3DGS optimization. The co-regularization signal is used to weight the contribution of each Gaussian to the optimization, reducing the influence of Gaussians that are inconsistent between the two models and therefore likely to be inaccurate.

The depth-aware Gaussian splatting method of [arxiv_2410.11080] addresses sparse-view synthesis by using monocular depth prediction as a prior, combined with a scale-invariant depth loss to constrain the 3D shape under few input views. The method also models color using lower-order spherical harmonics to avoid overfitting and retains all splats rather than periodically removing low-opacity ones, observing that aggressive pruning leads to sparse point clouds and lower-quality rendering in the few-shot setting [arxiv_2410.11080]. The contrast between this approach and the default densification and pruning behavior of vanilla 3DGS illustrates how the optimal hyperparameter settings for 3DGS depend strongly on the data regime. In the dense-view setting, aggressive pruning is beneficial because it removes redundant Gaussians and reduces storage requirements. In the sparse-view setting, however, aggressive pruning can remove Gaussians that are needed to represent parts of the scene that are not well-covered by the training images, leading to gaps in the reconstruction.

### 11.2 Deblurring and Robustness to Image Degradation

The challenge of training 3DGS from blurry images is another important practical concern. In real-world capture scenarios, motion blur from camera shake or object motion and defocus blur from lens characteristics are common, and they degrade the quality of 3DGS reconstructions trained on such images. The standard 3DGS optimization assumes that the training images are sharp and noise-free, and this assumption is violated in many practical capture scenarios.

The Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting method [arxiv_2401.00834] addresses this by modeling the blur process within the 3DGS framework, allowing the method to recover clean and sharp scene representations from blurry training images. The blur model is incorporated into the rendering pipeline as an additional image formation step that convolves the rendered sharp image with a spatially varying blur kernel, enabling the optimization to jointly estimate the sharp scene representation and the blur kernel from the blurry training images. This approach is conceptually related to blind deconvolution in image processing, adapted to the 3D scene reconstruction setting.

The BAD-Gaussians method [arxiv_2403.11831] takes a more principled approach, introducing Bundle Adjusted Deblur Gaussian Splatting that leverages explicit Gaussian representations to accurately recover intricate details from severely motion-blurred images. The motivation for BAD-Gaussians is that implicit NeRF representations struggle to recover fine details from motion-blurred images and cannot achieve real-time rendering, while 3DGS's explicit representation provides a more suitable foundation for joint deblurring and scene reconstruction [arxiv_2403.11831]. The bundle adjustment component of BAD-Gaussians jointly optimizes the camera poses and the scene representation, addressing the fact that motion blur is often accompanied by camera pose uncertainty. Both methods demonstrate that the 3DGS framework is flexible enough to accommodate additional image formation models beyond the standard sharp pinhole camera assumption, extending its applicability to more challenging real-world capture conditions.

### 11.3 Pose-Free Reconstruction

The problem of camera pose estimation is closely related to the robustness of 3DGS to imperfect inputs. Standard 3DGS, like most NeRF methods, relies on accurately pre-computed camera poses obtained from Structure-from-Motion preprocessing, typically using COLMAP. This dependency on accurate camera poses is a significant practical limitation, as SfM can fail or produce inaccurate results in challenging scenarios such as textureless scenes, repetitive patterns, or dynamic content.

The COLMAP-Free 3D Gaussian Splatting method [arxiv_2312.07504] relaxes this constraint by leveraging both the explicit geometric representation of 3DGS and the continuity of input video streams to perform novel view synthesis without any SfM preprocessing. The method processes input frames sequentially, progressively growing the 3D Gaussian set by incorporating one frame at a time, without the need to pre-compute camera poses. The explicit point cloud representation of 3DGS is identified as a key enabler of this approach, providing geometric structure that can be used to estimate camera poses on the fly in a way that the implicit representations of NeRF cannot easily support [arxiv_2312.07504]. The sequential processing strategy exploits the temporal continuity of video streams, using the overlap between consecutive frames to estimate relative camera poses and initialize new Gaussians for regions that are newly visible in each frame.

This work illustrates how the explicit nature of 3DGS opens up new possibilities for pose-free reconstruction that are more difficult to realize with implicit representations. The ability to perform pose-free reconstruction is particularly valuable for applications such as casual video capture, where users cannot be expected to follow a structured capture protocol or use specialized equipment for camera calibration. By eliminating the dependency on SfM preprocessing, COLMAP-Free 3DGS makes the method accessible to a much wider range of users and capture scenarios.

## 12. Physics-Based Rendering and Material Estimation

The intersection of 3DGS with physics-based rendering and material estimation represents an emerging direction that connects novel view synthesis with inverse rendering. The PBR-NeRF method [arxiv_2412.09680], while primarily NeRF-based, addresses a limitation shared by most NeRF and 3DGS approaches: they estimate view-dependent appearance without modeling scene materials and illumination. By introducing physics-based priors for joint estimation of scene geometry, materials, and illumination, PBR-NeRF demonstrates the potential for inverse rendering approaches to produce more physically meaningful scene representations. The relevance to 3DGS is that similar physics-based constraints could be incorporated into Gaussian-based representations to enable material editing and relighting, extending the utility of 3DGS beyond pure novel view synthesis [arxiv_2412.09680].

The challenge of incorporating physics-based rendering into 3DGS is significant, because the alpha compositing model used by 3DGS does not naturally decompose appearance into material and illumination components. Addressing this challenge requires either modifying the rendering model to incorporate physically-based shading or introducing auxiliary representations that model materials and illumination separately from the Gaussian primitives. The latter approach is more compatible with the existing 3DGS framework and has been explored in several recent works that augment 3DGS with learnable material and illumination representations. The connection between these works and the broader literature on inverse rendering and physically-based rendering suggests that this is a promising direction for future research.

## 13. Emerging Directions and Broader Applications

Beyond the core themes discussed above, the 3DGS literature has expanded into a wide range of application domains and methodological directions that reflect the versatility of the Gaussian splatting framework. Several recent works have explored the application of 3DGS to large-scale outdoor scene reconstruction [arxiv_2312.03203, arxiv_2410.02103], where the challenges of unbounded scene extent, varying illumination, and sparse coverage require specialized adaptations of the basic framework. The extension of 3DGS to large-scale scenes requires addressing the memory limitations of the representation, as the number of Gaussians needed to cover a large outdoor environment can be orders of magnitude larger than for a small indoor scene.

The application of 3DGS to autonomous driving scene reconstruction has attracted particular attention, motivated by the need for high-fidelity scene representations that can support sensor simulation and data augmentation for training perception systems [arxiv_2312.03203]. Autonomous driving scenes present unique challenges for 3DGS, including the presence of dynamic objects such as vehicles and pedestrians, the need for accurate geometry reconstruction for safety-critical applications, and the requirement for real-time rendering to support closed-loop simulation. Several works have proposed specialized adaptations of 3DGS for autonomous driving, including methods for separating static and dynamic scene components, incorporating LiDAR depth measurements as additional supervision, and modeling the appearance of reflective surfaces such as wet roads and car bodies.

The integration of 3DGS with generative models represents another emerging direction that has attracted significant research attention. Several works have explored the use of diffusion models and other generative approaches to provide additional supervision for 3DGS optimization, particularly in the sparse-view and single-view settings where the training data is insufficient to constrain the reconstruction [arxiv_2410.04974, arxiv_2410.12262]. The generative prior provides a form of learned regularization that encourages the reconstructed scene to be consistent with the distribution of natural scenes, compensating for the lack of multi-view constraints. This approach is conceptually related to the use of image priors in classical image reconstruction problems, adapted to the 3D scene reconstruction setting.

The application of 3DGS to human body and face reconstruction has also been an active area of research, motivated by the importance of these applications for virtual reality, telepresence, and entertainment. Human bodies and faces present particular challenges for 3DGS due to their complex geometry, articulated motion, and fine-grained appearance details such as hair, skin texture, and clothing wrinkles. Several works have proposed specialized Gaussian representations for human bodies that incorporate anatomical priors such as body shape models and skeletal rigs, enabling more accurate and controllable reconstruction of human subjects [arxiv_2312.12337, arxiv_2403.14244].

The use of 3DGS for scene understanding and semantic segmentation represents yet another direction that extends the framework beyond pure appearance reconstruction. By associating semantic labels or feature vectors with individual Gaussian primitives, it is possible to produce 3DGS representations that encode both appearance and semantic information, enabling applications such as object segmentation, scene parsing, and language-guided scene editing [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2410.17932]. The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation makes it particularly well-suited to semantic annotation, as individual primitives can be labeled and manipulated independently.

Recent work has also explored the application of 3DGS to medical imaging and scientific visualization, where the ability to reconstruct and render three-dimensional structures from two-dimensional projections is of fundamental importance. The connection between 3DGS and tomographic reconstruction is particularly intriguing, as both problems involve recovering a three-dimensional density field from a set of two-dimensional projections. Several works have explored the use of 3DGS for CT and MRI reconstruction, demonstrating that the Gaussian splatting framework can be adapted to the specific characteristics of medical imaging data [arxiv_2408.03356, arxiv_2411.02229].

The theoretical foundations of 3DGS have also received increasing attention, with several works providing rigorous analyses of the convergence properties of the optimization, the expressiveness of the Gaussian representation, and the relationship between the rendering model and the underlying scene geometry [arxiv_2502.19318, arxiv_2410.04974]. These theoretical contributions are important for understanding the fundamental capabilities and limitations of 3DGS and for guiding the development of improved methods. The analysis of the rendering approximations in [arxiv_2502.19318], discussed earlier, is one example of this theoretical work; other examples include analyses of the densification dynamics, the role of spherical harmonics in view-dependent appearance modeling, and the relationship between Gaussian covariance and surface normal estimation.

## 14. Synthesis and Cross-Cutting Themes

Taken together, the works reviewed in this section paint a picture of a field that has rapidly matured around the foundational 3DGS method [arxiv_2308.04079], with research proceeding along several parallel tracks. The first track concerns the fundamental rendering model of 3DGS, questioning whether its approximations are appropriate and exploring whether more principled volumetric rendering can improve quality [arxiv_2502.19318]. The second track addresses the memory and storage demands of 3DGS through compression, pruning, factorization, and hybrid representations [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2311.13681, arxiv_2408.03822, arxiv_2312.13299, arxiv_2405.17083, arxiv_2509.17083, arxiv_2502.19457]. The third track explores alternative primitives that overcome the geometric limitations of Gaussian ellipsoids [arxiv_2411.14974, arxiv_2406.02720, arxiv_2401.06003, arxiv_2511.18873]. The fourth track addresses the integration of 3DGS with surface reconstruction and geometry estimation [arxiv_2403.16964, arxiv_2411.01853]. The fifth track extends 3DGS to dynamic scenes [arxiv_2405.17891, arxiv_2404.06270, arxiv_2408.03822]. The sixth track develops editing and manipulation capabilities [arxiv_2405.17811, arxiv_2506.06462]. The seventh track improves robustness to challenging acquisition conditions including sparse views [arxiv_2411.03637, arxiv_2409.14316, arxiv_2405.12110, arxiv_2410.11080], blurry inputs [arxiv_2401.00834, arxiv_2403.11831], and absent camera poses [arxiv_2312.07504].

Several cross-cutting themes emerge from this review. One is the tension between the explicit, interpretable nature of the Gaussian representation and the compactness and continuity of implicit neural representations. Many of the most interesting recent methods occupy hybrid positions in this space, combining explicit Gaussians with neural fields, implicit surfaces, or learned compression schemes to capture the benefits of both paradigms [arxiv_2509.17083, arxiv_2403.16964, arxiv_2411.01853]. This tension is not unique to 3DGS; it reflects a broader debate in the machine learning and computer vision communities about the relative merits of explicit and implicit representations, and the conditions under which each is preferable. The 3DGS literature provides a rich set of case studies for this debate, as the explicit Gaussian representation has clear advantages for rendering speed and editability but clear disadvantages for compactness and geometric precision.

Another cross-cutting theme is the importance of initialization and geometric priors for 3DGS optimization. Because 3DGS is an explicit representation initialized from sparse point clouds, the quality of the initialization has a strong influence on the final reconstruction, particularly in challenging settings such as sparse views or dynamic scenes [arxiv_2409.14316, arxiv_2404.06270, arxiv_2411.03637]. This sensitivity to initialization is a fundamental characteristic of explicit representations, which lack the implicit regularization provided by the architecture of neural networks. Addressing this sensitivity requires either improving the quality of the initialization, introducing explicit regularization terms that constrain the optimization, or developing optimization strategies that are more robust to poor initialization. The works reviewed in this section have explored all three of these approaches, and the relative merits of each depend on the specific application and data regime.

A third theme is the adaptability of the 3DGS framework to a wide range of image formation models and scene types, as demonstrated by methods that handle blur [arxiv_2401.00834, arxiv_2403.11831], dynamic deformation [arxiv_2405.17891, arxiv_2404.06270], repetitive structure [arxiv_2506.06462], and absent poses [arxiv_2312.07504]. This adaptability reflects the modular nature of the 3DGS framework, which separates the scene representation (the Gaussian primitives and their parameters) from the rendering model (the tile-based rasterizer and alpha compositing) and the optimization strategy (the adaptive densification and gradient-based parameter updates). By modifying one or more of these components, it is possible to adapt the framework to a wide range of scenarios without changing its fundamental structure. This modularity is a key strength of 3DGS and is likely to continue to enable rapid methodological innovation in the future.

A fourth cross-cutting theme is the role of learned priors and data-driven regularization in improving the quality of 3DGS reconstructions. Many of the methods reviewed in this section incorporate learned priors in the form of pre-trained depth estimation networks [arxiv_2410.11080], multi-view stereo networks [arxiv_2409.14316], or generative models [arxiv_2410.04974, arxiv_2410.12262] to provide additional constraints on the optimization. These learned priors encode knowledge about the structure of natural scenes that is not captured by the photometric reconstruction loss alone, and they are particularly valuable in challenging settings where the training data is insufficient to constrain the reconstruction. The use of learned priors in 3DGS optimization is closely related to the broader trend toward foundation models and pre-trained representations in computer vision, and it is likely that future work will increasingly leverage large-scale pre-trained models to improve the quality and robustness of 3DGS reconstructions.

## 15. Surveys, Reviews, and the State of the Field

The surveys and reviews in [arxiv_2403.11134], [arxiv_2401.03890], [arxiv_2210.00379], and [arxiv_2502.19457] provide complementary perspectives on the state of the field. The survey of [arxiv_2403.11134] classifies recent 3DGS methods by functionality into 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and downstream applications, providing a functional taxonomy that complements the thematic organization adopted here. This classification highlights the breadth of the 3DGS research landscape and the diversity of application domains that have been addressed, from autonomous driving and robotics to medical imaging and cultural heritage preservation. The survey of [arxiv_2401.03890] provides a systematic overview of the underlying principles and driving forces behind 3DGS, emphasizing its practical applicability and the range of applications it enables. The comprehensive NeRF survey of [arxiv_2210.00379] situates 3DGS within the broader history of neural rendering, documenting the shift in community attention from NeRF to Gaussian-based methods and the ongoing coexistence of the two paradigms. The compression survey of [arxiv_2502.19457] provides the most detailed treatment of the memory and storage challenges of 3DGS and the methods that have been proposed to address them, offering a valuable reference for researchers working on deployment of 3DGS in resource-constrained settings.

The rapid growth of the 3DGS literature since the publication of the foundational method in August 2023 is itself a remarkable phenomenon that deserves comment. Within less than two years of the foundational paper's publication, hundreds of follow-up works had appeared, addressing virtually every aspect of the method and extending it to a wide range of applications. This rate of methodological innovation is unusual even by the standards of modern machine learning research, and it reflects both the practical importance of the problems addressed by 3DGS and the accessibility of the method as a research platform. The open-source release of the original 3DGS code and the availability of standard benchmarks for evaluation have facilitated rapid iteration and comparison, enabling the community to build on each other's work efficiently.

The breadth of the 3DGS research landscape also reflects the versatility of the Gaussian splatting framework as a general-purpose tool for 3D scene representation and rendering. Unlike NeRF, which is primarily a method for novel view synthesis, 3DGS has proven to be a flexible foundation for a wide range of tasks including surface reconstruction, dynamic scene modeling, scene editing, semantic understanding, and physics-based rendering. This versatility is a consequence of the explicit nature of the Gaussian representation, which makes it easy to integrate with other representations and algorithms, and of the differentiable rendering framework, which enables end-to-end optimization for a wide range of objective functions.

## 16. Conclusion

What emerges from this body of work is a clear picture of 3DGS as a foundational method that has catalyzed a broad and rapidly growing research community. The method's combination of explicit representation, differentiable rasterization, and adaptive densification [arxiv_2308.04079] provides a flexible and powerful foundation that has proven amenable to extension in many directions. At the same time, the limitations of vanilla 3DGS—its memory demands, its geometric imprecision, its sensitivity to initialization and data quality, and its rendering approximations—have provided a rich set of research problems that have motivated substantial methodological innovation.
