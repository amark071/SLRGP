# 3D Gaussian Splatting (long)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS — evidence cluster A

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most significant shifts in the field of 3D scene representation in recent years. To appreciate the magnitude of this shift, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader trajectory of neural rendering research, particularly in relation to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), which dominated the field prior to 2023.

NeRF, introduced in 2020, fundamentally transformed how 3D scenes could be represented and rendered by encoding volumetric scene properties within the weights of a multilayer perceptron conditioned on spatial position and viewing direction [arxiv_2210.00379]. This implicit, neural network-based representation enabled photorealistic novel view synthesis from a sparse set of input images and quickly became the dominant framework for the task. Over the following years, a substantial body of work extended NeRF to address its limitations in training speed, rendering efficiency, and generalizability, with applications spanning robotics, autonomous navigation, urban mapping, and augmented reality [arxiv_2210.00379]. Despite these advances, NeRF-based methods remained fundamentally constrained by the computational cost of ray marching through volumetric representations, which required querying a neural network at numerous sample points along each ray during rendering. This bottleneck made real-time rendering at high resolutions largely infeasible for NeRF-based approaches.

The introduction of 3D Gaussian Splatting by Kerbl et al. in 2023 addressed these limitations through a fundamentally different representational strategy [arxiv_2308.04079]. Rather than encoding scene information implicitly within neural network weights, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of explicit 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterized by a position, covariance matrix, opacity, and view-dependent color encoded via spherical harmonics. Rendering is accomplished by rasterizing these Gaussian primitives onto the image plane through a tile-based differentiable splatting pipeline, enabling real-time novel view synthesis at 1080p resolution at frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second [arxiv_2308.04079]. Crucially, the method is initialized from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration via Structure-from-Motion, and the Gaussian parameters are optimized end-to-end through a differentiable rendering loss. The combination of competitive training times, high visual fidelity, and real-time rendering performance established 3DGS as a landmark contribution that rapidly overtook NeRF-based approaches in terms of research interest and practical adoption [arxiv_2210.00379].

A key conceptual distinction between 3DGS and NeRF lies in their rendering philosophies. NeRF employs a principled ray-marching approach grounded in volumetric rendering theory, integrating radiance and density along each camera ray to produce pixel colors [arxiv_2502.19318]. While 3DGS shares a similar image formation model with NeRF at a high level, its rendering pipeline is a hybrid solution that combines elements of volume rendering with the efficiency of primitive rasterization [arxiv_2502.19318]. This hybridization involves a set of deliberate approximations relative to strict volumetric rendering theory, including the projection of 3D Gaussians onto 2D screen-space Gaussians and the use of alpha compositing in depth-sorted order rather than continuous integration along rays. These approximations are central to the method's computational efficiency, but they raise important questions about the fidelity of the underlying scene representation. Recent work has examined whether replacing these approximations with more principled volumetric rendering solutions could improve reconstruction quality, finding that the relationship between rendering accuracy and visual quality in 3DGS is nuanced and not straightforwardly resolved by stricter adherence to volumetric rendering theory [arxiv_2502.19318].

The explicit nature of the 3DGS representation confers advantages that extend well beyond rendering speed. Because the scene is encoded as a set of discrete, geometrically interpretable primitives rather than distributed across neural network weights, it is substantially more amenable to downstream editing and manipulation tasks [arxiv_2403.11134]. This has motivated a rapidly growing body of work exploiting 3DGS for applications including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry editing, and physical simulation, tasks that are considerably more challenging to perform on implicit neural representations [arxiv_2403.11134]. The survey by Chen et al. provides a comprehensive taxonomy of these developments, classifying recent 3DGS methods into three broad functional categories: 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications, reflecting the breadth of directions the field has pursued since the original method's introduction [arxiv_2403.11134].

The rapid proliferation of 3DGS-based research also reflects the method's accessibility as a foundation for further development. Its explicit parameterization makes it straightforward to incorporate domain-specific inductive biases, to integrate with physical priors, or to extend to new modalities such as LiDAR or event cameras. At the same time, the speed of the field's growth has made comprehensive review challenging; as noted in [arxiv_2403.11134], the number of works building on 3DGS has increased at a pace that demands systematic synthesis to identify trends and open problems.

Taken together, the literature positions 3DGS as a transformative development that has redefined the state of the art in novel view synthesis and opened new avenues for 3D scene understanding and manipulation. Its relationship to NeRF is best understood not as a simple supersession but as a complementary evolution: while NeRF-based methods continue to offer advantages in certain regimes, particularly where geometric accuracy and principled uncertainty quantification are paramount, 3DGS has established itself as the dominant framework for applications demanding real-time performance and interactive editability [arxiv_2210.00379, arxiv_2308.04079]. The ongoing investigation into the theoretical underpinnings of its rendering approximations [arxiv_2502.19318] suggests that the foundational properties of 3DGS remain an active area of inquiry, with implications for both the quality ceiling of the method and its future development.

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS — evidence cluster B

The rapid ascent of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has generated a substantial body of follow-on research addressing its core limitations, ranging from memory inefficiency and surface reconstruction quality to robustness under degraded input conditions. Understanding these contributions requires situating them within the broader tension between the expressive power of explicit representations and the compactness of implicit or hybrid alternatives.

A foundational concern with 3DGS is its memory footprint. Because the method relies on per-Gaussian parameters to encode view-dependent appearance and anisotropic geometry, the number of primitives required to faithfully represent a complex scene can become prohibitively large. HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] directly confronts this problem by proposing a hybrid radiance field that decomposes scene content into two complementary components: a compact set of explicit Gaussians that retain only the most critical high-frequency parameters, and grid-based neural fields responsible for predicting the remaining properties. The motivation behind this decomposition is that not all Gaussian attributes benefit equally from explicit storage; low-frequency, spatially smooth properties are more efficiently encoded by a continuous neural field, while high-frequency spatial variations—which are precisely the features that neural compression schemes struggle to capture—are preserved explicitly. Prior work attempting to compress 3DGS with neural fields is acknowledged by the authors of HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] as suffering from degraded reconstruction of fine details, a failure mode that their hybrid architecture is specifically designed to overcome. This contribution illustrates a broader design principle emerging in the literature: rather than choosing categorically between explicit and implicit representations, researchers are increasingly exploring structured combinations that leverage the strengths of each.

A related but distinct challenge concerns the quality of surface reconstruction, particularly in open or large-scale scenes. The discrete, primitive-based nature of 3DGS means that the reconstructed surface is effectively assembled from a large collection of Gaussian spheres, which introduces two interrelated problems: excessive memory consumption and rough surface detail in regions where Gaussian coverage is sparse. Gaussian Voxel Kernel Functions (GVKF) [arxiv_2411.01853] address this by establishing a continuous scene representation on top of the discrete 3DGS substrate through kernel regression. By integrating fast 3DGS rasterization with implicit scene representations, GVKF achieves high-fidelity surface reconstruction in challenging open-scene settings while mitigating the roughness artifacts that arise when Gaussian primitives are insufficiently dense. The approach can be understood as complementary to HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] in spirit: both works recognize that the raw discrete representation of 3DGS is insufficient on its own and introduce continuous components to fill the gaps, though they target different failure modes—memory and appearance fidelity in the case of HyRF, and geometric surface quality in the case of GVKF.

A separate but equally important line of research addresses the sensitivity of 3DGS to input image quality, particularly the presence of blur. In practical acquisition scenarios, blur arises from multiple sources including lens defocus, object motion, and camera shake, and it is a pervasive obstacle to clean image capture. Standard 3DGS training assumes sharp, well-posed images, and when this assumption is violated the rendering quality degrades severely. Two notable works tackle this problem from related but distinct angles.

Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting [arxiv_2401.00834] situates itself against the backdrop of prior neural field approaches to blur-robust rendering, noting that while several NeRF-based methods have attempted to synthesize sharp novel views from blurry inputs, they inherit the computational costs of volumetric rendering and neural network inference, making real-time application impractical. The explicit nature of 3DGS offers a potential remedy in terms of rendering speed, but the authors observe that naively applying 3DGS to blurry training data leads to severe quality degradation. Their work therefore proposes mechanisms within the 3DGS framework to model and compensate for blur during training, enabling high-quality, real-time novel view synthesis even from imperfect inputs.

BAD-Gaussians [arxiv_2403.11831] addresses a closely related problem with a particular focus on motion blur arising in low-light or long-exposure conditions, where camera poses may themselves be uncertain or inaccurate. The authors note that NeRF-based approaches to motion-blurred reconstruction struggle to recover intricate details from severely degraded images and cannot achieve real-time rendering—a dual limitation that motivates their turn to explicit Gaussian representations. BAD-Gaussians introduces bundle adjusted deblur Gaussian splatting, which jointly optimizes scene geometry and camera trajectories to explain the observed blur as the result of camera motion during exposure. This framing is more physically grounded than approaches that treat blur purely as a post-hoc degradation, and it allows the method to handle severe motion blur that would defeat simpler deblurring strategies. Comparing BAD-Gaussians [arxiv_2403.11831] with Deblurring 3DGS [arxiv_2401.00834], both works share the goal of making Gaussian splatting robust to blurry inputs and both are motivated by the inadequacy of NeRF-based predecessors, but BAD-Gaussians places greater emphasis on the joint estimation of camera pose and scene content, reflecting the insight that blur and pose uncertainty are often entangled in real acquisition scenarios.

Taken together, these works reveal several converging themes in the 3DGS literature. First, the explicit, discrete nature of Gaussian primitives, while enabling real-time rendering, introduces structural limitations in memory efficiency, surface continuity, and robustness to input degradation that require targeted solutions. Second, hybrid architectures that combine explicit Gaussians with continuous neural or grid-based components—as seen in HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] and GVKF [arxiv_2411.01853]—represent a promising direction for addressing multiple limitations simultaneously. Third, the practical deployment of 3DGS in uncontrolled capture conditions necessitates methods that can handle imperfect inputs, with blur robustness [arxiv_2401.00834, arxiv_2403.11831] being a particularly active area of investigation. These threads collectively define a research agenda centered on extending the core strengths of 3DGS—speed and explicit controllability—while systematically closing the gaps that separate it from deployment-ready performance across diverse real-world conditions.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization — evidence cluster A

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) as a paradigm for novel-view synthesis has generated a rapidly expanding body of research addressing its core limitations and extending its applicability across diverse rendering scenarios. While the foundational appeal of 3D-GS lies in its capacity for real-time rendering through differentiable rasterization of explicit Gaussian primitives, subsequent work has revealed several persistent challenges that demand targeted solutions, including excessive memory consumption, storage overhead, degraded performance under sparse input conditions, and an inability to disentangle scene materials from illumination effects. The literature surveyed here reflects these four interrelated threads of inquiry.

A primary concern motivating much recent work is the substantial memory and storage burden imposed by 3D-GS representations. Because high-fidelity scene reconstruction typically requires millions of Gaussian primitives, the per-point memory footprint accumulates to levels that are prohibitive for resource-constrained deployment and real-time applications. Two complementary approaches to this problem are represented in the current evidence. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] addresses memory inefficiency at the level of per-point attribute storage by introducing quantized embeddings that compress the information associated with each Gaussian. Alongside this quantization strategy, EAGLES employs a coarse-to-fine training procedure designed to stabilize and accelerate the optimization of Gaussian point clouds, and incorporates a dedicated pruning stage that reduces the total number of Gaussians retained in the final scene representation. The combined effect is a reduction in both training time and rendering latency without sacrificing reconstruction quality. F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083] approaches the storage problem from a structurally different angle, drawing inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques. Rather than compressing per-point attributes directly, F-3DGS factorizes dense clusters of Gaussians into lower-dimensional coordinate and feature representations, thereby approximating the original point cloud with far fewer stored parameters. Both methods share the overarching goal of making 3D-GS viable in settings where memory budgets are constrained, yet they differ in their technical mechanisms: EAGLES operates through quantization and pruning at the individual primitive level, while F-3DGS restructures the representation itself through factorization of spatial and appearance components. Together, these contributions illustrate that the memory challenge in 3D-GS is amenable to multiple complementary strategies, and that the choice between them may depend on whether the bottleneck lies in per-point attribute size or in the sheer number of primitives required to cover a scene.

A second major limitation of vanilla 3D-GS concerns its degradation under sparse input conditions. When only a small number of training views are available, the optimization of Gaussian attributes becomes severely underconstrained, leading to floaters, structural inconsistencies, and poor generalization to novel viewpoints. SCGaussian [arxiv_2411.03637] directly confronts this challenge by introducing structural consistency as an explicit optimization objective. The authors observe that Gaussian attributes are highly interdependent, and that the position of Gaussian primitives—unlike rendering geometry—cannot be directly constrained within the standard 3D-GS framework due to its non-structural nature. To overcome this, SCGaussian proposes a hybrid Gaussian representation that incorporates matching priors, enabling the method to learn 3D-consistent scene structure even from sparse inputs. By optimizing both rendering geometry and primitive positions in a principled manner, SCGaussian achieves more reliable reconstruction in large scenes where sparse-view methods have historically struggled. This work situates itself within a broader literature on few-shot novel-view synthesis that spans both NeRF-based and Gaussian-based methods, and its contribution is particularly notable for addressing the positional consistency of primitives rather than focusing solely on appearance attributes—a distinction that reflects a deeper understanding of where structural ambiguity originates in the 3D-GS pipeline.

A third limitation, shared by both NeRF and 3D-GS approaches, is the conflation of material properties and illumination within view-dependent appearance models. Most existing methods estimate how a scene looks from a given viewpoint without decomposing that appearance into its physical constituents—surface albedo, roughness, and incident lighting. This conflation limits the utility of reconstructed scenes for applications requiring relighting or material editing. PBR-NeRF [arxiv_2412.09680] addresses this gap within a NeRF framework informed by Physics-Based Rendering (PBR) theory, jointly estimating scene geometry, materials, and illumination through an inverse rendering model. Crucially, PBR-NeRF introduces two novel physics-based priors formulated as loss terms that better constrain the inherently ill-posed inverse rendering estimation, achieving state-of-the-art material decomposition without degrading novel-view synthesis quality. Although PBR-NeRF is primarily a NeRF-based method, the authors explicitly acknowledge that the same limitation—the absence of material and illumination modeling—affects 3D Gaussian Splatting approaches as well, and they note that their framework is adaptable to other inverse rendering and 3D reconstruction pipelines. This positions PBR-NeRF as a relevant reference point for understanding the broader challenge of physically grounded scene representation, a challenge that the 3D-GS community has increasingly begun to address.

Taken together, the works reviewed here delineate the principal axes along which 3D Gaussian Splatting research is currently advancing: compressing memory and storage requirements through quantization and factorization [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2405.17083], improving structural consistency under sparse supervision [arxiv_2411.03637], and moving toward physically interpretable scene decompositions that disentangle appearance from underlying material and lighting properties [arxiv_2412.09680]. These directions are not mutually exclusive; future work integrating factorized representations with physics-based priors, or combining structural consistency constraints with memory-efficient encodings, represents a natural and productive trajectory for the field.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization — evidence cluster B

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm in novel view synthesis has prompted a wave of research aimed at both understanding its foundational limitations and extending its capabilities across a range of challenging scenarios. At its core, 3DGS represents scenes as collections of explicit, point-based Gaussian primitives, enabling real-time rendering and rapid training that stand in marked contrast to the implicit volumetric representations characteristic of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). Yet this shift from implicit to explicit representation, while conferring substantial practical advantages, introduces a distinct set of technical challenges that have motivated numerous refinements and alternative formulations.

One of the most frequently cited limitations of the original 3DGS framework concerns its difficulty in faithfully reconstructing scenes with sharp geometric features. Because Gaussian primitives are, by definition, diffuse and smoothly varying in space, they tend to disperse irregularly around actual surfaces rather than conforming tightly to them. This tendency makes the accurate capture of hard edges and flat surfaces particularly problematic, often requiring a dramatic increase in the number of primitives to approximate fine geometric detail, with a correspondingly large memory footprint [arxiv_2411.14974]. To address these geometric shortcomings, Convex Splatting introduces smooth convex shapes as an alternative primitive type, arguing that such shapes offer greater geometric flexibility than Gaussians while preserving the efficiency advantages of explicit, point-based rendering [arxiv_2411.14974]. This substitution reflects a broader recognition in the field that the choice of primitive geometry is not merely a technical detail but a fundamental design decision with far-reaching consequences for reconstruction quality, memory efficiency, and downstream usability.

Rendering fidelity under conditions of high scene complexity represents another axis along which 3DGS has been found wanting. Blurring and cloudy artifacts can emerge when the method is tasked with scenes containing fine-grained or high-frequency detail, a limitation that has been noted in comparative analyses of point-based rendering approaches [arxiv_2401.06003]. The TRIPS framework, which combines ideas from Gaussian Splatting and the earlier ADOP system, attempts to navigate this trade-off by rasterizing points into a screen-space image pyramid and applying a lightweight reconstruction network, thereby achieving crisper results without the temporal instability that has plagued purely neural reconstruction pipelines [arxiv_2401.06003]. The comparison between TRIPS and standard 3DGS is instructive: while 3DGS benefits from its avoidance of a neural reconstruction stage, this same design choice limits its ability to handle scenes where local point density is insufficient to resolve fine detail. TRIPS accepts a modest computational overhead in exchange for improved sharpness and temporal consistency, illustrating the inherent tension between rendering speed and reconstruction quality that pervades this area of research [arxiv_2401.06003].

Memory consumption constitutes a third persistent concern. The explicit storage of per-Gaussian attributes—including position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients—scales with the number of primitives, and complex scenes can require millions of Gaussians to achieve acceptable fidelity. This issue is compounded in dynamic scene reconstruction, where the representation must account not only for spatial complexity but also for temporal variation. Addressing both concerns simultaneously, one line of work proposes a refined 3DGS representation that employs a deformable multi-layer perceptron network to capture the dynamic offset of Gaussian points across time, while also introducing hash encoding for color feature representation as a means of reducing memory overhead without sacrificing rendering quality [arxiv_2405.17891]. This approach situates itself at the intersection of two active research threads: the extension of 3DGS to dynamic settings and the compression of Gaussian-based representations for practical deployment. The use of a deformable MLP to model temporal offsets is conceptually related to deformation-field approaches developed in the NeRF literature, suggesting that the boundary between implicit and explicit representations is more porous in practice than the categorical distinction between NeRF and 3DGS might imply [arxiv_2405.17891].

Beyond reconstruction quality and efficiency, a further limitation of 3DGS that has attracted significant attention is its relative inflexibility for interactive manipulation and content editing. NeRF-based methods, despite their high rendering fidelity, have long been criticized for the difficulty of performing intuitive edits to the underlying scene representation, and early attempts to address this through canonical-space deformation or mesh-guided manipulation were hampered by slow training and inference times [arxiv_2405.17811]. The transition to 3DGS was expected to alleviate some of these difficulties by virtue of its explicit, point-based structure, yet in practice the manipulation of Gaussian primitives in a geometrically coherent and visually plausible manner has proven non-trivial. The Mani-GS framework responds to this gap by proposing a method that binds Gaussian primitives to a triangular mesh, using the mesh as a control structure through which high-level manipulations can be propagated to the underlying Gaussian representation [arxiv_2405.17811]. This hybrid strategy—combining the rendering efficiency of 3DGS with the geometric controllability of explicit mesh representations—exemplifies a recurring theme in the literature, namely the search for representations that inherit the strengths of multiple paradigms while mitigating their respective weaknesses.

Taken together, these contributions reveal a field in active dialogue with the limitations of its own foundational methods. Whether through the substitution of alternative primitive geometries [arxiv_2411.14974], the integration of lightweight neural components to recover lost detail [arxiv_2401.06003], the extension to dynamic scenes via deformable networks [arxiv_2405.17891], or the coupling of Gaussian representations to mesh-based control structures for interactive editing [arxiv_2405.17811], researchers have consistently sought to expand the envelope of what 3DGS can achieve. The diversity of these approaches underscores that no single modification has yet resolved all of the method's known limitations, and that the trade-offs among rendering quality, geometric fidelity, memory efficiency, temporal coherence, and editability remain central organizing tensions in the ongoing development of 3D Gaussian Splatting and its successors.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) represents a significant departure from the implicit, coordinate-based paradigms that dominated novel view synthesis for several years prior. To appreciate the novelty and implications of 3DGS, it is necessary to situate it within the broader trajectory of scene representation research, particularly in relation to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and the persistent tension between rendering fidelity and computational efficiency.

Neural Radiance Fields, introduced as a method for learning to represent 3D scenes from collections of 2D images, demonstrated remarkable capacity for synthesizing photorealistic views from previously unobserved viewpoints [arxiv_2103.14645]. The core mechanism of NeRF relies on a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that maps spatial coordinates and viewing directions to volumetric density and color values, which are then integrated along camera rays through differentiable volumetric rendering. While this implicit representation proved highly expressive, its computational demands rendered it fundamentally incompatible with real-time applications. As noted by Hedman and colleagues, rendering a single view from a trained NeRF requires querying the MLP hundreds of times per ray, making interactive frame rates on commodity hardware essentially unattainable [arxiv_2103.14645]. This bottleneck motivated a wave of research aimed at accelerating NeRF inference without sacrificing the visual quality that made the approach compelling in the first place.

One influential strategy for bridging the gap between NeRF's expressive power and the demands of real-time rendering was the concept of "baking" — precomputing and caching the learned scene representation into a more efficiently queryable data structure. The Sparse Neural Radiance Grid (SNeRG) approach exemplifies this direction, reformulating NeRF's architecture and storing the resulting scene as a sparse voxel grid with learned feature vectors [arxiv_2103.14645]. By decoupling the expensive training phase from the rendering phase, SNeRG demonstrated that NeRF's ability to capture fine geometric details and view-dependent appearance could be largely preserved while enabling real-time rendering on standard hardware [ss_478166]. This baking paradigm effectively converted an implicit, query-intensive representation into a more explicit, spatially indexed structure, foreshadowing the broader shift toward explicit representations that 3DGS would later embody more fully.

Despite such advances, NeRF-based methods — including their accelerated variants — continued to rely on volumetric rendering pipelines that impose inherent computational ceilings. The fundamental challenge is that volumetric rendering requires integrating contributions along rays through a continuous or discretized volume, a process that scales poorly with scene complexity and resolution. These limitations set the stage for 3DGS, which abandons the volumetric rendering paradigm entirely in favor of a rasterization-based pipeline operating over explicit geometric primitives [arxiv_2502.19457]. Rather than encoding scene information implicitly within the weights of a neural network, 3DGS represents scenes using millions of learnable 3D Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients encoding view-dependent color. This explicit representation enables differentiable rendering through efficient splatting — projecting the 3D Gaussians onto the image plane and compositing their contributions — a process that maps naturally onto modern GPU rasterization hardware and achieves real-time rendering speeds that volumetric approaches struggle to match [arxiv_2502.19457].

The shift from implicit to explicit representation also confers significant advantages in terms of scene editability. Because individual Gaussian primitives are directly interpretable geometric entities with well-defined spatial extents, they can be manipulated, removed, or modified in ways that are far more tractable than editing the distributed, entangled weights of an MLP [arxiv_2502.19457]. This property positions 3DGS as a particularly attractive foundation for downstream applications in interactive graphics, scene editing, and augmented reality, where the ability to modify scene content at runtime is essential.

However, the transition to explicit Gaussian-based representations introduces its own set of challenges, most notably with respect to memory and storage. Maintaining high rendering fidelity with 3DGS typically requires representing scenes with very large numbers of Gaussian primitives — often in the millions — each carrying a substantial parameter footprint due to the full covariance matrix and spherical harmonic coefficients [arxiv_2311.13681]. The resulting memory and storage requirements can be prohibitive, particularly for deployment on resource-constrained devices such as mobile platforms or embedded systems. This concern is not merely theoretical; as surveys of the 3DGS compression literature have documented, the storage demands of 3DGS models constitute one of the primary obstacles to their practical deployment [arxiv_2502.19457].

Efforts to address this limitation have proceeded along two complementary axes: reducing the number of Gaussian primitives required to represent a scene at a given quality level, and compressing the per-primitive parameter representations more efficiently [arxiv_2311.13681]. The former direction involves developing more principled densification and pruning strategies during training, so that Gaussians are allocated where they contribute most to rendering quality rather than proliferating indiscriminately. The latter direction draws on classical compression techniques — including quantization, entropy coding, and learned compact representations — adapted to the specific statistical structure of Gaussian parameter distributions [arxiv_2502.19457]. Together, these approaches reflect a recognition that the raw expressive power of 3DGS must be balanced against practical constraints if the representation is to fulfill its promise as a deployable technology for real-time 3D reconstruction and rendering.

In summary, 3DGS emerges from a lineage of research that progressively moved scene representations from purely implicit neural formulations toward more explicit, spatially structured alternatives capable of supporting real-time rendering. While approaches such as SNeRG demonstrated that baking implicit representations into explicit structures could recover much of the efficiency lost to volumetric rendering [arxiv_2103.14645, ss_478166], 3DGS takes this logic further by adopting explicit Gaussian primitives as the primary representational unit from the outset [arxiv_2502.19457]. The resulting framework achieves compelling rendering quality and speed, but the associated memory costs motivate an active and growing body of work on compact and compressed Gaussian representations [arxiv_2311.13681, arxiv_2502.19457].

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has catalyzed a broad wave of research addressing its core limitations and extending its applicability across diverse visual computing tasks. As surveyed comprehensively by [arxiv_2401.03890], 3DGS distinguishes itself from mainstream implicit neural representations by employing millions of learnable 3D Gaussians to encode scene geometry and appearance explicitly. This explicit formulation, paired with a differentiable rasterization pipeline, enables real-time rendering at high visual fidelity and introduces a degree of editability that implicit neural radiance field (NeRF) models struggle to match. The survey characterizes 3DGS as a potential game-changer for 3D reconstruction and representation, underscoring the breadth of downstream applications it has unlocked, ranging from virtual environments to interactive content creation [arxiv_2401.03890]. Yet despite this promise, the literature reveals a consistent set of challenges that researchers have actively sought to address: the handling of dynamic scenes, the scarcity of input views, and the substantial storage overhead associated with explicit Gaussian representations.

One of the most actively explored frontiers concerns the extension of 3DGS to dynamic scene modeling. Static scene reconstruction, while well-served by the original formulation, does not naturally accommodate temporal deformation. Prior NeRF-based approaches to dynamic view synthesis learn deformation fields implicitly, a strategy that, as argued by [arxiv_2404.06270], fails to incorporate 3D scene geometry in any principled way. The consequence is that learned deformations are not necessarily geometrically coherent, leading to artifacts in synthesized views and degraded quality in 3D dynamic reconstruction. The method proposed in [arxiv_2404.06270] addresses this by treating each 3D Gaussian as a geometric primitive that moves and rotates over time, with deformation governed by explicitly extracted 3D geometry features. By integrating these geometric constraints directly into the deformation learning process, the approach enforces spatial consistency that purely implicit methods cannot guarantee. This geometry-aware strategy represents a meaningful departure from NeRF-based dynamic modeling and illustrates how the explicit nature of Gaussian representations can be leveraged not merely for rendering efficiency but also for structural fidelity in temporally varying scenes.

A separate but equally pressing challenge is the performance of 3DGS under sparse input conditions. Novel view synthesis from few images is a longstanding problem in 3D vision, and while NeRF-based methods have made considerable progress in this regime, they remain burdened by slow training and inference times [arxiv_2409.14316]. The explicit point-based representation of 3DGS offers real-time rendering as a significant advantage, yet it introduces its own vulnerability: without sufficient input views, the optimization tends to overfit to the training perspectives, producing poor generalization to unseen viewpoints. MVPGS [arxiv_2409.14316] confronts this limitation by incorporating multi-view priors derived from learning-based Multi-view Stereo (MVS) to improve the quality of geometric initialization for the Gaussian optimization. Additionally, the method introduces a forward-warping strategy to generate supplementary appearance constraints, mitigating the overfitting that arises from sparse supervision. The reliance on MVS-derived geometry as an initialization prior reflects a broader trend in the field toward hybridizing learned geometric priors with the explicit Gaussian representation, compensating for the data-hungry nature of the optimization when input coverage is limited. Comparing [arxiv_2409.14316] with [arxiv_2404.06270], both works share the insight that geometric information external to the basic Gaussian optimization loop is essential for robust performance—whether that geometry pertains to temporal deformation or to spatial initialization from sparse views.

Storage efficiency constitutes a third major axis of concern in the 3DGS literature. The explicit representation of a scene through millions of Gaussian primitives, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients, results in storage footprints that are substantially larger than those of comparable implicit representations [arxiv_2312.13299]. This overhead poses a practical barrier to deployment on resource-constrained devices and limits the scalability of 3DGS to large or complex scenes. The work presented in [arxiv_2312.13299] addresses this by organizing Gaussian parameters into a 2D grid structure that exploits local homogeneity among neighboring Gaussians. The central insight is that natural scenes exhibit perceptual redundancies, and that many permutations of Gaussian parameters can yield equivalent scene representations. By arranging parameters to maximize local coherence within the grid, the method achieves substantial compression without sacrificing rendering quality. The approach is notable for its explicit exploitation of scene-level statistical regularities, a strategy that contrasts with post-hoc compression schemes and instead embeds compactness as a structural property of the representation itself.

Taken together, these works illuminate the multi-dimensional nature of the challenges surrounding 3DGS. The survey by [arxiv_2401.03890] provides the conceptual scaffolding for understanding why these challenges arise—the very explicitness that grants 3DGS its rendering speed and editability also introduces sensitivity to initialization quality, temporal incoherence in dynamic settings, and memory demands that scale with scene complexity. The targeted contributions of [arxiv_2404.06270], [arxiv_2409.14316], and [arxiv_2312.13299] each address a distinct facet of this landscape, yet they collectively reinforce a common theme: that the full potential of 3DGS as a scene representation framework depends on augmenting its core formulation with principled geometric constraints, learned priors, and structural regularization. The trajectory of the field suggests that future advances will increasingly integrate these complementary strategies, moving toward representations that are simultaneously efficient, geometrically faithful, and robust across varied capture conditions.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has catalyzed a broad range of research efforts aimed at addressing its core limitations while simultaneously extending its applicability to increasingly challenging scenarios. The foundational appeal of 3DGS lies in its explicit point-cloud-based representation, which enables real-time, high-quality rendering at computational costs substantially lower than those associated with Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) [arxiv_2410.11080]. However, this baseline capability has exposed several critical weaknesses that subsequent work has sought to remedy, including poor performance under sparse input conditions, prohibitive memory and storage requirements, degraded reconstruction quality in occluded or poorly covered regions, and a dependency on pre-computed camera poses that constrains deployment in unconstrained settings.

One of the most practically significant limitations of vanilla 3DGS is its reliance on dense multi-view input. When only a small number of training views are available, the quality of the rendered output degrades substantially, as the optimization lacks sufficient geometric constraints to recover accurate 3D structure. To address this, depth-aware approaches have been proposed that incorporate monocular depth predictions as geometric priors during training [arxiv_2410.11080]. By coupling these priors with a scale-invariant depth loss, such methods constrain the 3D shape even under severely limited viewpoint coverage. An additional insight from this line of work concerns the handling of low-opacity Gaussian splats: whereas the original 3DGS framework periodically prunes splats with low opacity to maintain a compact representation, this pruning strategy can result in an excessively sparse point cloud when training data is scarce, ultimately degrading rendering quality. Retaining all splats, combined with lower-order spherical harmonics for color modeling to prevent overfitting, has been shown to yield more robust reconstructions in the few-shot regime [arxiv_2410.11080]. This finding highlights a nuanced tension between compactness and completeness that runs through much of the 3DGS literature.

The question of memory and storage efficiency represents another major axis of ongoing research. Standard 3DGS and its extensions require a large number of Gaussian primitives to faithfully reproduce fine scene details, and the associated attributes—including view-dependent color encoded via spherical harmonics and full covariance matrices—impose substantial memory overhead [arxiv_2408.03822]. This problem is further compounded when 3DGS is extended to dynamic scenes, where temporal variation demands even greater representational capacity. Compact 3DGS approaches have tackled this challenge through two complementary strategies: reducing the total number of Gaussian points without sacrificing perceptual quality, and compressing the per-Gaussian attributes themselves [arxiv_2408.03822]. Learnable masking strategies, which allow the model to selectively retain only the most informative Gaussians during training, have proven effective in achieving this balance. The explicit nature of the Gaussian representation, which is often cited as a rendering advantage, thus also enables principled compression schemes that would be more difficult to apply to the implicit volumetric representations used in NeRF-based methods.

A distinct but related challenge concerns the reconstruction of scene regions that are poorly observed due to occlusion or insufficient viewpoint coverage—a problem that affects both dense and sparse input settings. An innovative approach to this problem exploits the structural regularity present in many real-world environments [arxiv_2506.06462]. The observation that natural and man-made scenes frequently contain repetitive elements—such as windows, tiles, or architectural ornaments—motivates a method that segments individual repeated instances within a 3DGS reconstruction, registers them into a shared coordinate frame, and enables information sharing across instances. This cross-instance information transfer allows well-reconstructed instances to inform the geometry and appearance of poorly observed counterparts, effectively compensating for coverage gaps and occlusions [arxiv_2506.06462]. Crucially, the method also accounts for appearance variations across nominally identical instances, acknowledging that real-world repetitions are rarely perfectly uniform. This work stands in contrast to the depth-prior approach of [arxiv_2410.11080], which addresses sparse-view limitations through external geometric supervision, whereas [arxiv_2506.06462] instead leverages internal scene structure to propagate information laterally across the reconstruction.

Perhaps the most fundamental constraint on 3DGS deployment in practice is its dependence on Structure-from-Motion (SfM) preprocessing—specifically, the use of tools such as COLMAP to estimate camera poses before training can begin. This requirement not only introduces significant computational overhead but also limits applicability in scenarios where accurate pose estimation is infeasible, such as in-the-wild video capture. Efforts to eliminate this dependency have drawn on the explicit geometric nature of the Gaussian representation as a key enabler [arxiv_2312.07504]. By processing input video frames sequentially and incrementally growing the Gaussian set one frame at a time, pose-free 3DGS methods can jointly optimize scene structure and camera parameters without any SfM initialization. This sequential, online processing strategy exploits the temporal continuity of video streams as a structural prior, providing the geometric coherence that SfM would otherwise supply [arxiv_2312.07504]. The contrast with NeRF-based pose-free methods is instructive: the implicit representations used in NeRF make the joint optimization of structure and pose considerably more challenging, whereas the explicit point-cloud representation of 3DGS provides a more tractable optimization landscape for this problem [arxiv_2312.07504].

Taken together, these research directions reveal a coherent pattern in the evolution of 3DGS methodology. The explicit, point-based representation that defines 3DGS simultaneously enables its core strengths—fast rendering, geometric interpretability, and amenability to compression—and motivates the specific technical strategies employed to address its weaknesses. Whether through geometric priors for sparse-view settings, learnable compression for memory efficiency, structural regularities for occlusion handling, or sequential optimization for pose-free reconstruction, the field is progressively expanding the conditions under which 3DGS can deliver high-quality novel view synthesis.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

The representational foundations of 3D Gaussian Splatting have attracted sustained scholarly attention, with researchers probing both the geometric primitives at the core of the framework and the regularization strategies needed to ensure robust reconstruction across diverse imaging conditions. A recurring theme in this body of work is the tension between rendering fidelity and geometric accuracy, a tension that motivates several complementary lines of inquiry into how Gaussian-based representations can be extended, constrained, or augmented to overcome their inherent limitations.

A foundational concern is that the standard 3D Gaussian primitive, while computationally efficient and amenable to real-time rasterization, is a smooth, symmetric kernel that struggles to represent sharp boundaries, surface discontinuities, and fine geometric detail. This limitation manifests most visibly at object edges and in regions where color or reflectance changes abruptly. The work on 3D Half-Gaussian Splatting directly confronts this problem by replacing the conventional symmetric Gaussian kernel with a half-Gaussian formulation that can model discontinuities in both shape and appearance [arxiv_2406.02720]. Crucially, the authors frame their contribution as a plug-and-play module, meaning it can be integrated into existing 3DGS pipelines without requiring wholesale architectural redesign. Empirical results reported in that work demonstrate state-of-the-art rendering quality without sacrificing the speed advantage that distinguishes 3DGS from volumetric neural rendering approaches such as Neural Radiance Fields. This positions the half-Gaussian kernel as a targeted surgical intervention rather than a paradigm shift, acknowledging that the broader 3DGS ecosystem has value worth preserving while still addressing a concrete representational bottleneck.

A related but distinct line of inquiry concerns the relationship between the density or radiance field learned by Gaussian splatting and the underlying scene geometry. Neural volumetric rendering methods, including standard 3DGS, aggregate color contributions from point or primitive distributions without explicitly encoding surface geometry, which tends to produce visually compelling renderings that nonetheless lack geometrically coherent surfaces. GSDF addresses this disconnect by coupling a 3DGS representation with a Signed Distance Function in a dual-branch architecture [arxiv_2403.16964]. The motivation is that each representation compensates for the weaknesses of the other: the Gaussian branch excels at capturing high-frequency appearance detail and supports fast rendering, while the SDF branch enforces geometric regularity and provides a well-defined surface. Prior approaches that attempted to impose geometric discipline on Gaussian splatting typically did so either by constraining the distribution of density fields or by restricting the shape of individual primitives, and the GSDF authors argue that both strategies degrade rendering quality or introduce surface artifacts, particularly in large and geometrically complex scenes [arxiv_2403.16964]. By maintaining two complementary representations that interact during training, GSDF seeks to achieve high rendering quality and accurate surface reconstruction simultaneously, a goal that single-representation methods find difficult to satisfy concurrently.

The question of representational capacity is taken up from a different angle in Neural Texture Splatting, which argues that the per-primitive expressiveness of standard 3DGS is fundamentally limited because each Gaussian kernel can only encode a relatively simple local appearance model [arxiv_2511.18873]. Earlier work had proposed augmenting individual splats with per-primitive textures to increase expressiveness, but Neural Texture Splatting observes that such approaches were primarily validated on dense novel view synthesis tasks and tend to underperform when applied to broader reconstruction scenarios including geometry estimation and dynamic scene reconstruction [arxiv_2511.18873]. The paper's contribution is to develop a more general augmentation strategy that yields consistent improvements across this wider range of tasks, rather than optimizing narrowly for one benchmark. This comparative framing is instructive: it suggests that the community should be cautious about evaluating representational enhancements solely on novel view synthesis metrics, since gains on that task do not necessarily transfer to the geometric and temporal reconstruction problems that are equally important for downstream applications.

Whereas the preceding works focus on the expressive power of individual primitives or the coupling between appearance and geometry, CoR-GS addresses a different failure mode that emerges when training data is sparse [arxiv_2405.12110]. Under sparse-view conditions, 3DGS is prone to overfitting, producing radiance fields that fit the available training images well but generalize poorly to novel viewpoints. CoR-GS introduces a co-regularization strategy grounded in the observation that when two independent 3DGS radiance fields are trained on the same sparse data, the stochastic nature of the densification process causes them to diverge in ways that are diagnostically informative [arxiv_2405.12110]. Specifically, the authors identify two forms of disagreement between the twin fields—point disagreement and rendering disagreement—and demonstrate empirically that both are negatively correlated with reconstruction accuracy. This correlation is exploited to identify and suppress inaccurate reconstructions without requiring access to ground-truth geometry or additional supervision signals, making the approach self-supervised in a meaningful sense. The co-regularization perspective is conceptually distinct from the geometric coupling strategy of GSDF or the kernel redesign of 3D-HGS, yet all three works share the underlying diagnosis that vanilla 3DGS lacks sufficient inductive bias to reliably recover accurate scene structure.

Taken together, these works illuminate a landscape in which the core 3DGS framework is being refined along several parallel axes: the shape of individual primitives [arxiv_2406.02720], the coupling between appearance and geometry [arxiv_2403.16964], the per-primitive representational capacity [arxiv_2511.18873], and the regularization of training under data-scarce conditions [arxiv_2405.12110]. While each contribution targets a distinct failure mode, they collectively reflect a shared recognition that the original Gaussian primitive and its associated training procedure leave significant room for improvement in both rendering quality and geometric fidelity. The diversity of proposed solutions also suggests that no single modification is likely to be universally sufficient, and that future progress may require combining insights from multiple lines of work.

The preceding discussion has concentrated primarily on representational and geometric refinements to the core 3DGS framework, but an equally consequential body of work addresses the computational and storage costs that arise when millions of Gaussian primitives must be maintained, transmitted, and rendered in real-time settings. The original 3DGS formulation already demonstrated a decisive speed advantage over NeRF-based volumetric rendering [arxiv_2308.04079], yet the explicit parameterization that enables this speed simultaneously imposes substantial memory overhead, since every primitive carries its own position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients. Surveys of compression strategies for 3DGS document a rapidly growing body of techniques that attack this overhead through quantization, pruning, neural codebooks, and structured reparameterization [arxiv_2502.19457]. A recurring finding across these approaches is that a large fraction of Gaussians contribute negligibly to the final rendered image and can be removed or merged without perceptible quality loss, suggesting that the densification heuristics inherited from the original method systematically over-populate the scene. Speedy-Splat formalizes this intuition by identifying two distinct sources of inefficiency—redundant primitives and redundant pixel coverage—and proposes targeted mechanisms to address both simultaneously, yielding substantial reductions in rendering time and model size without sacrificing visual fidelity [arxiv_2412.00578]. The practical implication is that the memory-speed trade-off that has been treated as an inherent property of explicit Gaussian representations is in fact partly an artifact of suboptimal training dynamics, and that more disciplined densification and culling strategies can recover much of the lost efficiency.

Complementary to pruning-based approaches, several works have explored structured reparameterizations that reduce the effective parameter count by exploiting redundancy across primitives rather than eliminating them outright. EAGLES encodes Gaussian attributes using lightweight quantized representations, demonstrating that the high-dimensional spherical harmonic coefficients used to model view-dependent color can be compressed aggressively without proportional degradation in rendering quality [arxiv_2312.04564]. The Compact 3D Gaussian Representation work pursues a related strategy, replacing the full spherical harmonic basis with a more compact appearance model and coupling this with a learned mask that suppresses unnecessary primitives during training [arxiv_2311.13681]. Self-Organizing Gaussian Grids take a structurally different approach by reorganizing Gaussian parameters into a 2D grid with local spatial coherence, enabling standard image compression codecs to be applied to the resulting representation [arxiv_2312.13299]. F-3DGS further decomposes Gaussian coordinates and appearance attributes into factorized components, drawing on the tensor decomposition ideas that proved effective in grid-based NeRF representations [arxiv_2405.17083]. Collectively, these compression-oriented works reveal a productive tension: the explicit, unstructured nature of Gaussian point clouds is precisely what enables fast differentiable rasterization, yet it also prevents the exploitation of spatial regularity that structured representations naturally afford. Bridging this tension—preserving the rendering efficiency of splatting while recovering the compressibility of structured representations—remains an active and unresolved challenge.

Memory efficiency is also a central concern in the context of hybrid representations that combine Gaussian primitives with neural fields. HyRF proposes such a hybrid, retaining explicit Gaussians for coarse scene structure while delegating the modeling of high-frequency spatial variations and view-dependent effects to a lightweight neural component [arxiv_2509.17083]. The motivation is that pure Gaussian representations require very large numbers of primitives to capture fine detail, while pure neural field representations sacrifice the real-time rendering advantage; a carefully designed hybrid can in principle achieve both goals simultaneously. This architectural philosophy echoes earlier work on baking neural radiance fields into explicit representations for real-time rendering [arxiv_2103.14645], but the direction of the hybrid is reversed: rather than distilling a neural field into an explicit structure after training, HyRF maintains both components jointly throughout optimization. The Splat the Net framework pursues a similar philosophy through splattable neural primitives that augment each Gaussian with a small learned network, enabling higher representational capacity per primitive without the full cost of a global neural field [arxiv_2510.08491]. These hybrid approaches collectively suggest that the binary framing of explicit versus implicit representations, which dominated early comparisons between 3DGS and NeRF [arxiv_2210.00379], is giving way to a more nuanced design space in which the two paradigms are combined rather than opposed.

Beyond static scene reconstruction, the extension of 3DGS to dynamic scenes introduces additional challenges that interact with the compression and efficiency concerns described above. Dynamic Gaussian representations must track the temporal evolution of primitive parameters across frames, which multiplies the already substantial storage requirements of static 3DGS. The Compact 3D Gaussian Splatting work for dynamic radiance fields addresses this by learning a compact motion representation that is shared across primitives, reducing the per-frame parameter overhead while preserving temporal coherence [arxiv_2408.03822]. The 3D Geometry-aware Deformable Gaussian Splatting method takes a complementary approach, incorporating explicit geometric constraints into the deformation field to ensure that learned motions respect the underlying scene structure rather than producing geometrically implausible deformations [arxiv_2404.06270]. A Refined 3D Gaussian Representation for dynamic scenes further argues that the standard densification strategy is poorly suited to dynamic settings because primitives that are appropriate for one frame may be redundant or misplaced in another, motivating an adaptive densification scheme that responds to temporal as well as spatial reconstruction error [arxiv_2405.17891]. Taken together, these dynamic extensions illustrate that the challenges of 3DGS—overfitting, geometric incoherence, and memory overhead—are amplified rather than diminished when the temporal dimension is introduced, and that solutions developed for static scenes do not transfer automatically to the dynamic setting.

The application of 3DGS to robotics and embodied perception represents another frontier that places distinctive demands on the representation. Unlike the novel view synthesis benchmarks that dominate academic evaluation, robotic applications require representations that support not only rendering but also collision detection, path planning, and real-time scene updating as the robot moves through its environment [arxiv_2410.12262]. The explicit, spatially localized nature of Gaussian primitives is advantageous in this context because individual primitives can be added, removed, or updated incrementally without retraining the entire representation, a property that implicit neural fields lack. However, the same survey notes that current 3DGS methods struggle with the noisy, incomplete observations that are typical of robotic sensing, and that the densification heuristics designed for offline multi-view reconstruction do not adapt gracefully to the online, sequential observation regime characteristic of robotic deployment [arxiv_2410.12262]. This gap between the conditions under which 3DGS was developed and the conditions under which it must operate in practice motivates a growing body of work on robust initialization, incremental updating, and uncertainty-aware densification, though these directions remain less mature than the rendering-focused research that has dominated the field to date.
