# Graph Neural Networks (long)

### Graph Pooling Methods — evidence cluster A

The aggregation and summarization of node-level representations into a single graph-level embedding constitutes one of the most consequential design decisions in graph neural network (GNN) architectures. This operation, broadly referred to as graph pooling, must contend with two fundamental challenges that distinguish graph-structured data from more regular domains: the variable sizes of graphs across a dataset and the isomorphic structures that may arise when graphs are topologically equivalent yet represented differently. How different pooling strategies address these challenges has attracted sustained research attention, and the resulting literature reveals a rich landscape of methodological trade-offs between expressiveness, computational tractability, and empirical performance.

A foundational concern motivating much of this work is the expressive power of GNNs in general. The Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test has long served as a theoretical benchmark for assessing whether a GNN can distinguish non-isomorphic graphs, and many early architectures were shown to be bounded by this test's discriminative capacity. Relational Pooling (RP) was proposed as a principled departure from this constraint [arxiv_1903.02541]. Drawing on the theory of finite partial exchangeability, RP provides a framework that can, somewhat counterintuitively, endow existing graph representation models with expressive power exceeding that of the WL isomorphism test. Crucially, RP is not tied to a specific base architecture; it can be applied on top of existing models and even allows recurrent neural networks and convolutional neural networks to be incorporated into graph classification pipelines in a theoretically grounded manner. Empirical evaluations reported in that work demonstrate improved performance over state-of-the-art methods across multiple graph classification tasks, suggesting that the theoretical gains in representational capacity translate meaningfully into practice [arxiv_1903.02541].

While RP addresses expressiveness from a theoretical vantage point, a complementary strand of research has focused on the practical question of which pooling mechanisms yield the best classification accuracy when combined with standard GNN backbones. A systematic empirical evaluation of several pooling methods across three architectures—GCN, TAGCN, and GraphSAGE—offers instructive findings in this regard [arxiv_2004.03519]. The study confirms that hierarchical pooling, and DiffPool in particular, tends to improve classification accuracy on widely used benchmark datasets. Notably, the results also reveal that TAGCN achieves comparable or superior accuracy relative to GCN and GraphSAGE, with the advantage being especially pronounced for datasets characterized by larger and sparser graph structures [arxiv_2004.03519]. This finding is practically significant because it suggests that the choice of GNN backbone interacts non-trivially with the pooling strategy, and that evaluations conducted on dense or small graphs may not generalize to the sparse, large-graph settings that are common in real-world applications.

A distinct limitation shared by many conventional pooling approaches is their reliance on first-order statistics—typically simple summation, averaging, or maximum aggregation over node feature vectors. While computationally efficient, these operations discard potentially informative higher-order relationships among node representations. Second-order pooling has been proposed as a remedy, leveraging the covariance structure of node embeddings to capture richer statistical dependencies [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785]. The core intuition is that second-order statistics encode pairwise interactions between feature dimensions, providing a more expressive summary of the node feature distribution than mean or sum pooling alone. The authors of this approach demonstrate that naively applying second-order pooling to GNNs introduces practical difficulties, including issues related to matrix size and numerical stability, and they propose two novel global pooling methods—bilinear mapping-based approaches—to overcome these obstacles [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785]. The resulting methods are shown to outperform first-order alternatives on standard benchmarks, reinforcing the argument that the statistical order of the pooling operation is a meaningful axis of variation in GNN design.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate several important tensions in the graph pooling literature. The work on Relational Pooling [arxiv_1903.02541] prioritizes theoretical expressiveness and demonstrates that the WL bound is not an immutable ceiling for GNN architectures, but the computational cost of exhaustively considering node permutations may limit scalability. The empirical benchmarking study [arxiv_2004.03519] takes a more pragmatic stance, revealing that DiffPool's hierarchical coarsening strategy reliably benefits classification performance across multiple backbone architectures, though the interaction between pooling method and graph sparsity warrants careful attention in deployment contexts. Second-order pooling [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785] occupies a middle ground, offering a theoretically motivated enhancement to global pooling that remains computationally feasible through careful design, yet introduces its own engineering challenges around matrix dimensionality and stability.

It is also worth noting that the variable-size and isomorphism challenges identified in the second-order pooling literature [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785] are precisely the structural properties that motivate the permutation-invariant framework underlying Relational Pooling [arxiv_1903.02541], suggesting a degree of conceptual convergence across these otherwise methodologically distinct lines of inquiry. Both recognize that a pooling operation must be invariant to the ordering of nodes while remaining sensitive to the graph's structural properties—a requirement that simple readout functions satisfy only partially.

In summary, the literature on graph pooling reflects an ongoing negotiation between expressiveness and efficiency. Theoretical frameworks push the boundaries of what GNNs can represent [arxiv_1903.02541], empirical studies clarify which methods generalize across architectures and graph types [arxiv_2004.03519], and statistically motivated innovations expand the information captured during aggregation [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785]. Understanding how these dimensions interact remains an open and practically consequential research question.

### Graph Pooling Methods — evidence cluster B

The emergence of graph neural networks (GNNs) as a dominant paradigm for learning on structured, non-Euclidean data has been driven by the growing recognition that many real-world phenomena—from social interactions to molecular structures—are most naturally represented as graphs. Deep learning has achieved remarkable success across tasks defined on Euclidean data, including image classification, speech recognition, and natural language understanding, yet the complexity of graph-structured data, characterized by irregular topology, variable node degrees, and intricate interdependencies, poses fundamental challenges that conventional architectures are ill-equipped to address [arxiv_1901.00596]. This recognition has catalyzed a substantial body of research aimed at extending the representational power of neural networks to graphs, resulting in a rich and rapidly evolving landscape of methods.

At the broadest level, GNNs can be understood as a family of approaches that learn node, edge, or graph-level representations by exploiting the relational structure encoded in the graph. A comprehensive taxonomy proposed in the literature organizes the state-of-the-art into four principal categories: recurrent graph neural networks, convolutional graph neural networks, graph autoencoders, and spatio-temporal graph neural networks [arxiv_1901.00596]. This taxonomy reflects both the diversity of architectural choices and the breadth of application domains that GNNs have come to serve. Social and information networks, knowledge graphs, and dynamic relational systems all benefit from graph-based representation, and the ability to capture relationships among potentially billions of entities makes GNNs particularly attractive for large-scale learning problems [arxiv_2102.02026]. The representations learned in lower-dimensional spaces can subsequently be leveraged for a variety of downstream tasks, including node classification, link prediction, and graph classification [arxiv_2102.02026].

The most widely studied class of GNNs operates through a message-passing framework, wherein nodes iteratively aggregate feature information from their local neighborhoods and update their own representations accordingly. Message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) have demonstrated strong empirical performance across a range of real-world applications, yet they are not without fundamental limitations. Two critical weaknesses have been identified in the aggregation mechanisms that underpin most MPNNs: first, the aggregation process tends to discard structural information about the relative positions of nodes within their neighborhoods, effectively treating the neighborhood as an unordered set; second, MPNNs struggle to capture long-range dependencies, particularly in disassortative graphs where connected nodes tend to belong to different classes or exhibit dissimilar features [arxiv_2002.05287]. These limitations are consequential because they constrain the expressiveness of the learned representations and may lead to suboptimal performance in settings where structural context and distant relational signals are informative.

To address these shortcomings, Geom-GCN introduces a geometric aggregation scheme that draws inspiration from both classical neural network design principles and the geometry of the underlying network space [arxiv_2002.05287]. The central insight is that graph aggregation can be enriched by appealing to a continuous latent space that underlies the discrete graph structure. The proposed scheme is permutation-invariant and decomposes into three functional modules: a node embedding component that maps nodes into a continuous geometric space, a structural neighborhood definition that leverages this embedding to organize neighborhood information, and a bilinear aggregation mechanism that preserves relative positional relationships among neighbors. By grounding aggregation in geometric structure rather than treating the neighborhood as a flat, unordered collection, Geom-GCN offers a principled means of retaining the spatial context that standard MPNNs discard [arxiv_2002.05287]. This geometric perspective represents a meaningful departure from prior aggregation strategies and highlights the importance of incorporating inductive biases that reflect the underlying topology of the data.

While much of the early GNN literature focused on node-level tasks, the problem of graph-level representation learning—wherein a single vector must summarize the properties of an entire graph—has received increasing attention. Graph classification requires not only informative node representations but also an effective mechanism for aggregating those representations into a coherent global descriptor. A naive approach is to apply a global pooling operation, such as summing or averaging node features, but this strategy fails to preserve the rich hierarchical and structural information present in the graph [arxiv_1904.13107]. Motivated by the success of hierarchical pooling in convolutional neural networks for image data, researchers have sought analogous procedures for graphs. EigenPooling addresses this need by proposing a hierarchical pooling method grounded in spectral graph theory, specifically leveraging the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian to define pooling operations that respect the underlying community structure of the graph [arxiv_1904.13107]. By organizing nodes into clusters informed by spectral properties and then coarsening the graph progressively, EigenPooling enables the network to capture structural patterns at multiple scales, a capability that flat aggregation schemes fundamentally lack.

Taken together, these contributions illustrate several converging themes in the GNN literature. There is a persistent tension between the computational simplicity of local, permutation-invariant aggregation and the need to encode richer structural and long-range information. Geom-GCN and EigenPooling each respond to this tension from different angles: the former by enriching neighborhood aggregation with geometric embeddings [arxiv_2002.05287], and the latter by introducing hierarchical coarsening to preserve multi-scale structure [arxiv_1904.13107]. Meanwhile, survey-level analyses underscore that no single architectural family dominates across all tasks and data regimes, and that the choice of GNN variant must be guided by the specific structural properties of the graph and the requirements of the downstream application [arxiv_1901.00596]. The growing importance of knowledge graphs and dynamic relational data further motivates continued development of flexible, expressive GNN architectures capable of scaling to complex, real-world graph datasets [arxiv_2102.02026].

### Graph Convolutional Network Architectures — evidence cluster A

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning representations over non-Euclidean, graph-structured data, addressing a fundamental limitation of classical deep learning architectures that were originally designed for grid-like inputs such as images or sequential text. Traditional convolutional neural networks, as noted by [arxiv_2307.00865], are inherently constrained to Euclidean space and therefore fail to capture the relational and topological complexity present in real-world graph data such as transportation networks, social networks, and citation graphs. This recognition has motivated a substantial body of research aimed at extending the convolutional operation to irregular graph domains, giving rise to a diverse ecosystem of graph-based learning methods.

One of the foundational challenges in this space is defining a meaningful analogue of the local receptive field that makes convolutional networks so effective for image data. [arxiv_1605.05273] addressed this challenge by proposing a general framework for learning convolutional neural networks on arbitrary graphs, including undirected, directed, and attributed graphs. Their approach extracts locally connected regions from graphs in a manner that is conceptually analogous to the spatially local patches used in image convolution. By doing so, the framework enables the learning of feature representations that are competitive with established graph kernel methods while remaining computationally efficient. This work was significant in demonstrating that the inductive biases of convolutional architectures could be meaningfully transferred to the graph domain without requiring the rigid regularity of grid-structured inputs.

Building on the general motivation to learn over graph-structured data, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) grounded in spectral graph theory became a dominant approach. These methods define convolution through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, enabling the aggregation of neighborhood information in a principled mathematical framework. However, as [arxiv_1906.02174] rigorously analyze, existing GCN formulations suffer from limited expressive power stemming from constraints imposed by their activation functions and architectural designs. Specifically, the authors demonstrate that standard GCNs are bounded in their capacity to exploit multi-scale information and that deeper architectures do not straightforwardly yield richer representations due to issues such as over-smoothing. To address these limitations, [arxiv_1906.02174] generalize spectral graph convolution and deep GCN architectures within block Krylov subspace forms, proposing two distinct architectures that are each capable of being scaled to greater depth while leveraging multi-scale structural information in complementary ways. Notably, the authors establish conditions under which these two architectures are theoretically equivalent, providing a unifying perspective on multi-scale deep graph convolution. Empirical evaluations on node classification benchmarks confirm that these architectures outperform prior methods, underscoring the practical value of breaking the expressive ceiling imposed by earlier designs.

The question of what information GNNs should encode is not limited to node-level features. [ss_477653] highlight a critical gap in most existing GCN methods: they predominantly focus on node embeddings while neglecting the rich information carried by edges. Since edges in many real-world graphs encode meaningful relational attributes—such as the strength of a social tie or the type of a molecular bond—ignoring them constitutes a significant loss of representational capacity. [ss_477653] propose a co-embedding framework that jointly learns representations for both nodes and edges within a unified graph neural network architecture, enabling end-to-end optimization that captures the full relational structure of the graph. This stands in contrast to earlier approaches like that of [arxiv_1605.05273], which, while accommodating graphs with both discrete and continuous node and edge attributes, do not explicitly co-optimize node and edge representations in a deeply integrated manner. The co-embedding perspective of [ss_477653] thus represents a meaningful extension of the representational scope of GNNs.

From a broader architectural standpoint, [arxiv_2307.00865] provide a comprehensive survey of GNN models, cataloguing developments in graph convolutional operators, graph pooling operators, attention mechanisms, and autoencoder-based architectures. Their review situates GNNs within a wide range of downstream tasks including node classification, graph classification, and link prediction, offering a taxonomy that helps clarify the relationships among the many model variants that have proliferated in the literature. The attention to graph pooling in particular is noteworthy, as pooling is essential for graph-level tasks that require aggregating node-level information into a single graph representation—a challenge that has no direct parallel in the node classification setting addressed by [arxiv_1906.02174].

Taken together, these works illuminate several persistent tensions in the GNN literature. The tension between depth and expressiveness, foregrounded by [arxiv_1906.02174], reflects a broader challenge of scaling neural architectures without sacrificing the diversity of learned representations. The tension between efficiency and completeness of representation, evident in the contrast between the locally connected region extraction of [arxiv_1605.05273] and the joint node-edge co-embedding of [ss_477653], points to ongoing trade-offs in model design. And the tension between specialized architectures and general-purpose frameworks, illustrated by the survey of [arxiv_2307.00865], underscores the need for principled criteria when selecting or designing GNN models for specific applications. Collectively, these contributions establish the theoretical and empirical foundations upon which more advanced graph learning systems continue to be built, and they define the key axes—expressiveness, scalability, representational completeness, and task adaptability—along which progress in the field is measured.

### Graph Convolutional Network Architectures — evidence cluster B

The development of graph neural networks (GNNs) has emerged as one of the most consequential advances in deep learning, driven by the ubiquity of graph-structured data across domains ranging from social network analysis to computational biology. Graphs provide a natural and expressive representation for relational data, yet their non-Euclidean and high-dimensional character poses fundamental challenges for conventional machine learning pipelines. Graph embedding methods address this challenge by transforming complex graph structures into low-dimensional continuous spaces that are amenable to downstream learning tasks [arxiv_2010.13242]. The progression from statistical embedding approaches to deep learning frameworks, particularly graph convolutional networks (GCNs), has been marked by consistent empirical gains, with deep learning methods generally outperforming their traditional counterparts across standard graph learning benchmarks by enabling end-to-end optimization of task-specific loss functions [arxiv_2010.13242]. This trajectory has motivated a rich body of research aimed at refining the core architectural components of GNNs, including convolution operations, pooling mechanisms, and the representational scope of learned embeddings.

A central concern in the GNN literature is the design of graph convolution operations that faithfully capture the structural properties of a node's local neighborhood. Most existing GCN formulations aggregate information from immediate neighbors in a manner that is agnostic to important structural attributes such as node degree, an omission that can limit the expressiveness of learned representations. DEMO-Net directly confronts this limitation by proposing a degree-specific graph neural network architecture motivated by the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, which recursively identifies one-hop neighborhood structures [arxiv_1906.02319]. The authors argue that existing graph convolution operations lack three desirable properties: being seed-oriented, degree-aware, and order-free. By explicitly encoding degree-specific structural information into the convolution process, DEMO-Net is designed to distinguish structurally distinct neighborhoods that would otherwise be conflated by degree-agnostic aggregation schemes [arxiv_1906.02319]. This work thus highlights a broader theoretical gap in the field, namely the absence of rigorous analysis of what graph convolution operations actually compute and what structural invariances they preserve or discard.

A complementary line of inquiry concerns the extension of convolution operations beyond simple one-hop message passing to incorporate longer-range structural dependencies. The path integral-based convolution framework, originally introduced for homogeneous graphs and subsequently extended to heterogeneous graph settings, proposes a convolution operation that aggregates information across every path connecting a message sender and receiver, with learnable weights parameterized by path length [arxiv_2302.13399]. This formulation corresponds to the maximal entropy random walk and yields a generalized transition matrix termed the maximal entropy transition (MET) matrix. A notable property of this construction is that the diagonal entries of the MET matrix are directly related to subgraph centrality, which provides a principled basis for node selection in pooling operations [arxiv_2302.13399]. The path integral framework thus unifies convolution and pooling within a single coherent mathematical structure, offering a theoretically grounded alternative to the largely heuristic pooling strategies prevalent in the literature. Compared to DEMO-Net's focus on degree-aware local aggregation [arxiv_1906.02319], the path integral approach prioritizes global structural information encoded through multi-hop path statistics, suggesting that these two perspectives address complementary aspects of the graph representation problem.

The question of how to aggregate node-level representations into a single graph-level embedding is itself a major research challenge, particularly for tasks such as graph classification. Naive pooling strategies such as sum, mean, or max aggregation discard structural information and are insensitive to the topology of the graph. More sophisticated hierarchical pooling methods attempt to coarsen the graph progressively, but their theoretical justification is often unclear, a point explicitly raised in the context of DEMO-Net's analysis of graph-level pooling schemes [arxiv_1906.02319]. An alternative paradigm is proposed through function space pooling, which maps a set of vertex representations not to a fixed-dimensional vector or a sequence, but to a representation in a function space [arxiv_1905.06259]. This approach is conceptually distinct from both flat aggregation methods and hierarchical coarsening strategies, and experimental results on graph classification benchmarks demonstrate that it generally outperforms most baseline pooling methods, achieving best performance in several cases [arxiv_1905.06259]. The function space pooling framework thus broadens the design space for graph-level readout operations and suggests that the choice of target representation space is itself a meaningful architectural decision.

Beyond the question of node embeddings, a further limitation of many GCN architectures is their exclusive focus on node-level representations, with edges treated merely as conduits for message passing rather than as first-class objects with their own semantic content. This asymmetry is problematic in applications where edge attributes carry substantial information, such as in molecular graphs where bond types are critical to property prediction. Co-embedding frameworks that jointly learn representations for both nodes and edges within a unified GNN architecture address this gap, enabling the model to capture relational information that is invisible to node-only approaches [arxiv_2010.13242]. The co-embedding perspective aligns naturally with the heterogeneous graph extension of path integral convolution, which must similarly contend with the presence of multiple node and edge types and the need to represent their interactions coherently [arxiv_2302.13399].

Taken together, these contributions reflect a maturing field grappling with the expressiveness, scalability, and theoretical foundations of graph neural network architectures. The convergence of degree-aware convolution [arxiv_1906.02319], path integral-based aggregation [arxiv_2302.13399], function space pooling [arxiv_1905.06259], and joint node-edge co-embedding [arxiv_2010.13242] illustrates the multidimensional nature of the challenges involved in learning from graph-structured data, and underscores the need for principled architectural choices grounded in both empirical performance and theoretical understanding.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

The development of graph neural networks (GNNs) has been substantially shaped by efforts to generalize convolutional operations from regular grid-structured data to the irregular domain of graphs. Early and influential work in this area drew on spectral graph theory, formulating convolution through the lens of the graph Laplacian and its eigendecomposition. However, polynomial spectral filters, which underpin many popular GNN architectures, carry inherent limitations: their frequency responses are constrained by the degree of the polynomial, making them less adaptable to complex spectral patterns and more susceptible to noise. To address these shortcomings, a line of research has proposed filters inspired by signal processing theory that offer richer representational capacity.

One particularly notable contribution in this direction is the introduction of graph convolutional layers based on auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) filters [ss_476188][arxiv_1901.01343]. Unlike polynomial filters, which are purely finite impulse response constructs, ARMA filters incorporate both moving average and auto-regressive components, yielding a more flexible frequency response that can better approximate a wider range of spectral profiles. The authors argue that this flexibility translates into improved robustness to noise and a stronger ability to capture global graph structure, properties that polynomial filters struggle to provide due to their localized and bounded nature. The proposed implementation achieves this through a recursive and distributed formulation, which ensures that the resulting layer remains computationally tractable, localized in node space, and transferable across different graph topologies at test time. Empirical evaluations spanning semi-supervised node classification, graph signal classification, graph classification, and graph regression tasks confirm that ARMA-based layers achieve competitive or superior performance relative to polynomial filter baselines [ss_476188][arxiv_1901.01343]. The convergence of these two independently published but substantively identical contributions underscores the significance of the ARMA perspective as a meaningful departure from the polynomial filter paradigm that had previously dominated spectral GNN design.

While spectral approaches focus on the frequency domain properties of graph convolution, spatial and structural considerations have also driven important architectural innovations. A recurring challenge in applying deep learning to graph-structured data is the design of effective pooling mechanisms that can reduce graph size in a principled and information-preserving manner, analogous to downsampling in image-based convolutional networks. Standard approaches to graph pooling, such as those based on node clustering or fixed topological criteria, often fail to account simultaneously for both node feature content and the underlying graph topology. Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool) addresses this gap by proposing a pooling method grounded in self-attention, where graph convolution is used to compute attention scores that reflect both node features and structural context [arxiv_1904.08082]. By retaining only the most salient nodes according to these jointly informed scores, SAGPool produces hierarchical graph representations that are sensitive to the interplay between topology and feature content. The authors conduct controlled comparisons against existing pooling methods using identical training procedures and model architectures, thereby isolating the contribution of the pooling mechanism itself. Their results demonstrate that self-attention-based pooling yields consistent improvements in graph classification benchmarks, reinforcing the view that pooling is a critical and underexplored component of graph deep learning pipelines [arxiv_1904.08082]. Compared to the ARMA filter work, which focuses on the quality of the convolution operation itself, SAGPool targets the complementary problem of hierarchical graph representation, suggesting that advances in GNN expressiveness require progress on both fronts simultaneously.

A further dimension of architectural development concerns the treatment of edge features within graph neural networks. Many GNN formulations concentrate exclusively on propagating and transforming node-level representations, treating edges merely as structural conduits for message passing rather than as carriers of independent semantic information. This asymmetry is particularly limiting in datasets where edge attributes encode meaningful relational properties. Addressing this gap, new layer architectures have been proposed that extend convolutional, pooling, and fully connected operations to incorporate multi-dimensional edge features more comprehensively [arxiv_1811.00052]. By designing explicit operations for edge features alongside vertex features, these architectures enable a more complete exploitation of the information encoded in graph-structured data. Benchmark evaluations demonstrate that incorporating edge-aware layers leads to measurable improvements in classification accuracy over prior state-of-the-art methods [arxiv_1811.00052]. This contribution stands in productive contrast to the ARMA filter approach: whereas the latter refines the spectral quality of node-level convolution, the former broadens the scope of what graph convolution operates upon, extending it from vertices to the full relational structure of the graph.

Taken together, these contributions illuminate three distinct but interrelated axes along which GNN architectures have been advanced: the spectral expressiveness of convolutional filters [ss_476188][arxiv_1901.01343], the design of topology-aware pooling mechanisms [arxiv_1904.08082], and the integration of edge feature learning into the convolutional framework [arxiv_1811.00052]. Each line of work identifies a specific limitation of earlier architectures and proposes targeted solutions that have been validated empirically across diverse graph learning tasks. The cumulative picture that emerges is one of a field progressively expanding its representational toolkit, moving beyond simple polynomial spectral filters and node-centric message passing toward richer, more holistic models of graph structure and content.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The intersection of graph-structured data and deep learning has generated a rich body of research that seeks to reconcile the expressive power of neural networks with the irregular, non-Euclidean nature of graphs. A central challenge in this domain is that conventional convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which have achieved remarkable success in computer vision and related fields, are designed to operate on grid-structured data and cannot be directly applied to graphs without significant adaptation [arxiv_2008.01247]. This fundamental incompatibility has motivated two broad lines of inquiry: extending CNN-style operations to graph domains through principled mathematical frameworks, and developing alternative representations that allow standard CNN architectures to process graph data without modification.

The signal processing perspective offers one of the most theoretically grounded approaches to bridging CNNs and graphs. Graph signal processing (GSP) provides a coherent framework for generalizing convolution and pooling operations by leveraging the spectral properties of the graph Laplacian [arxiv_2008.01247]. Under this view, the topology or structure of the underlying data graph is not merely an incidental feature but a primary design consideration that should govern architectural choices. This perspective is particularly valuable in domains such as social networks and biological systems, where relational structure encodes meaningful information that Euclidean methods would discard [arxiv_2008.01247]. By grounding graph neural network design in GSP principles, researchers gain both theoretical justification for architectural decisions and practical guidance on how to adapt components such as pooling layers to irregular graph structures.

A complementary approach sidesteps the challenge of extending CNN operations to graphs by instead transforming graph representations into formats that vanilla 2D CNNs can process directly. Rather than proposing a new graph-specific neural network variant, this strategy introduces a novel way to encode graphs as multi-channel image-like structures, effectively mapping the graph classification problem back into the domain where standard CNNs already excel [arxiv_1708.02218]. Empirical evaluations on real-world datasets demonstrate that this approach achieves accuracy superior to state-of-the-art graph kernels and graph CNNs on four out of six benchmarks, both with and without continuous node attributes, while also offering favorable time complexity compared to kernel methods [arxiv_1708.02218]. This result is notable because it suggests that careful representation engineering can sometimes outperform architecturally sophisticated graph-specific models, challenging the assumption that graph CNNs necessarily require fundamentally new computational primitives.

The comparison between graph kernel methods and graph neural networks is a recurring theme in the literature. Graph kernels have historically dominated graph classification tasks, but they are constrained by their reliance on hand-crafted combinatorial features, which limits their representational capacity and adaptability to diverse problem settings [arxiv_2201.00491]. Graph neural networks, particularly those based on the Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN) framework, have emerged as the state-of-the-art alternative due to their ability to learn task-relevant representations directly from data [arxiv_2201.00491]. However, MPNNs are not without their own theoretical limitations; research has established that they cannot exceed the discriminative power of the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) algorithm in the graph isomorphism test, imposing a ceiling on their expressive capacity [arxiv_2201.00491]. This theoretical bound motivates the search for architectures that transcend the MPNN paradigm while retaining its practical advantages.

One promising direction involves integrating graph kernels directly into the message passing process of GNNs, yielding a hybrid framework that seeks to combine the interpretability and theoretical grounding of kernel methods with the learning capacity of neural networks. The Kernel Graph Neural Networks (KerGNNs) framework embodies this philosophy, drawing inspiration from convolution filters in CNNs to incorporate graph kernels into the aggregation step [arxiv_2201.00491]. By doing so, KerGNNs address the limitations of both standalone graph kernels, which suffer from fixed feature representations, and standard MPNNs, which are bounded by WL expressiveness [arxiv_2201.00491]. This synthesis represents a broader trend in the field toward hybrid architectures that leverage the complementary strengths of different methodological traditions.

Beyond expressiveness, practical challenges associated with deep graph neural networks have received considerable attention. Stacking multiple graph convolution layers, in analogy with deep CNNs, is a natural strategy for increasing representational power, but it introduces well-documented pathologies including vanishing gradients, overfitting, and over-smoothing, wherein node representations become indistinguishable as depth increases [arxiv_2105.01510]. These problems are less pronounced in shallow networks, yet shallow architectures sacrifice expressive power, creating a difficult trade-off [arxiv_2105.01510]. The Multipath Graph Convolutional Neural Network addresses this dilemma by aggregating the outputs of multiple distinct shallow networks rather than relying on a single deep stack, thereby capturing multi-scale structural information without incurring the instabilities associated with depth [arxiv_2105.01510]. Experimental results on node property prediction benchmarks indicate that this approach not only improves test accuracy but also reduces training requirements, suggesting that architectural diversity across parallel shallow paths can serve as an effective substitute for depth [arxiv_2105.01510].

Taken together, these contributions illuminate the multidimensional nature of the challenges facing graph neural network research. The theoretical gap between MPNN expressiveness and the WL bound [arxiv_2201.00491], the practical difficulties of training deep graph networks [arxiv_2105.01510], the need for principled frameworks grounded in graph signal processing [arxiv_2008.01247], and the surprising competitiveness of representation-based approaches that bypass graph-specific architectures entirely [arxiv_1708.02218] collectively define a landscape in which no single paradigm has achieved uncontested dominance. Progress in the field has consequently been driven by a diversity of strategies, ranging from theoretical extensions of classical kernel methods to pragmatic architectural innovations, each addressing a distinct facet of the broader problem of learning effectively from graph-structured data.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The capacity of graph neural networks to operate on heterogeneous and structurally complex data has motivated a substantial body of work aimed at extending the foundational message-passing paradigm in several complementary directions. These directions include the handling of diverse edge and node types, the enrichment of typed relational reasoning, the aggregation of multi-scale structural information, and the design of pooling mechanisms that faithfully preserve graph-level semantics. Together, these lines of inquiry reveal both the remarkable versatility of GNN-based architectures and the persistent tensions between local and global representation, between fixed and learned graph topology, and between computational efficiency and expressive power.

A foundational challenge in applying GNNs to real-world data is that many graphs are inherently heterogeneous, comprising multiple types of nodes and edges whose relational semantics differ substantially. Standard GNN formulations typically assume a homogeneous graph structure, an assumption that becomes problematic when the graph is either misspecified or contains diverse relational types. Graph Transformer Networks (GTNs), introduced by Yun and colleagues, address this limitation by learning to generate new graph structures in an end-to-end fashion [arxiv_1911.06455]. Rather than operating on a fixed adjacency structure, GTNs employ a Graph Transformer layer that performs a soft selection over edge types and learns composite relations, effectively identifying useful connections between nodes that are not directly linked in the original graph. This capacity to synthesize new adjacency matrices from heterogeneous edge types represents a significant departure from prior GNN designs, which treat the graph topology as a static input. By allowing the network to discover which multi-hop or cross-type connections are most informative for a given task, GTNs achieve state-of-the-art performance on heterogeneous graph benchmarks while maintaining end-to-end differentiability [arxiv_1911.06455]. The approach thus reframes graph structure not as a fixed constraint but as a learnable component of the representation pipeline.

A related but distinct perspective on relational diversity is offered by Typed Graph Networks (TGNs), which address the problem of parameter sharing in the presence of typed relational data [arxiv_1901.07984]. Drawing an analogy to convolutional neural networks, which enforce weight sharing over a grid topology, TGNs instantiate trainable neural modules that are assembled according to the typed structure of the graph, thereby enforcing parameter sharing over relational configurations rather than spatial positions. In this framework, vertices are projected into a high-dimensional real space and iteratively refined through message-passing iterations in a fully differentiable architecture [arxiv_1901.07984]. The emphasis in TGNs is on the principled instantiation of typed modules, ensuring that the inductive biases encoded in the architecture reflect the relational structure of the domain. While GTNs focus on learning which graph structures to construct from heterogeneous edge types, TGNs focus on how to parameterize message-passing operations when node and edge types are known a priori. Both approaches, however, share the recognition that homogeneous GNN formulations are insufficient for the richness of real-world relational data.

Beyond the question of graph topology and relational typing, a second major axis of GNN research concerns the aggregation of information across multiple scales of graph structure. The standard message-passing procedure propagates information one hop per layer, meaning that lower layers capture local neighborhoods while higher layers integrate more global structural context [arxiv_2103.01488]. This sequential expansion of receptive fields creates a fundamental tension: deep models are required to capture long-range dependencies, yet increasing depth leads to well-documented performance degradation as nodes' local features are progressively diluted through repeated aggregation. Multi-Level Attention Pooling (MLAP), proposed by Noutahi and colleagues, addresses this tension directly by designing a pooling mechanism for graph-level classification that unifies representations from multiple layers, each corresponding to a different locality of information [arxiv_2103.01488]. By attending to representations at different depths simultaneously, MLAP avoids the forced choice between local and global information that afflicts single-scale pooling strategies. The multi-level attention mechanism weights contributions from different layers adaptively, allowing the model to emphasize the most task-relevant scale of structural information without discarding the others.

The question of how to pool node-level representations into a single graph-level embedding is itself a rich subproblem that has attracted dedicated theoretical and empirical attention. Simple aggregation strategies such as sum or mean pooling treat all node features as equally relevant, ignoring both task-specific importance and structural dependencies among nodes [arxiv_2102.11533]. Hierarchical pooling methods, while more expressive in principle, have been shown to suffer from a critical limitation: they can assign identical representations to graphs that are distinguishable by the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, indicating a fundamental loss of structural information during the coarsening process [arxiv_2102.11533]. Graph Multiset Pooling (GMPool), proposed by Baek and colleagues, reframes the pooling problem as one of multiset encoding with auxiliary structural information, enabling the model to capture both node feature relevance and inter-node dependencies when constructing the graph-level representation. This formulation is theoretically grounded and empirically validated, achieving strong performance on graph classification benchmarks [arxiv_2102.11533].

Comparing MLAP and GMPool reveals complementary emphases within the pooling literature. MLAP is primarily concerned with the temporal or depth dimension of information aggregation, ensuring that representations from multiple message-passing depths are jointly considered [arxiv_2103.01488], while GMPool focuses on the quality of the pooling operation at a given level of representation, ensuring that the mapping from a set of node embeddings to a graph embedding is both expressive and structure-aware [arxiv_2102.11533]. Together, these contributions suggest that robust graph-level representation requires attention both to the scale at which node features are computed and to the mechanism by which they are subsequently aggregated.

Taken collectively, the works reviewed here illuminate a coherent set of challenges that motivate ongoing innovation in GNN design: the need to handle heterogeneous and dynamically structured graphs [arxiv_1911.06455], the importance of typed relational parameterization [arxiv_1901.07984], the tension between local and global information in deep architectures [arxiv_2103.01488], and the expressiveness requirements of graph-level pooling [arxiv_2102.11533]. Each contribution addresses a distinct bottleneck in the standard GNN pipeline, and their juxtaposition underscores that progress in graph representation learning is necessarily multifaceted.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

The aggregation mechanisms underlying graph convolutional networks have been subject to sustained scrutiny, with researchers questioning whether the standard paradigm of propagating scalar node features across a single relational dimension is expressive enough to capture the full complexity of real-world graph-structured data. A growing body of work has sought to enrich the representational capacity of graph neural networks by reconsidering the nature of the graph itself, the structure of the features being propagated, and the mathematical objects used to model inter-node relationships. These efforts collectively reveal that the conventional one-dimensional, homogeneous view of graph convolution imposes significant constraints on what can be learned, and that relaxing those constraints opens productive avenues for both theoretical and empirical improvement.

A foundational limitation identified in the literature concerns the exclusive reliance on the object-link graph during convolution. Standard graph convolutional networks aggregate neighbor representations using only the adjacency structure, treating node attributes as passive cargo rather than as a source of relational information in their own right. This design choice, while computationally convenient, ignores potentially informative correlations among attribute dimensions. Addressing this gap directly, the dimensionwise separable 2-D graph convolution framework proposed in [arxiv_1909.12038] introduces a 2-D convolution operator that jointly models object links and attribute relations. By operating simultaneously along the node dimension and the feature dimension, the approach generalizes the standard 1-D graph convolution and is shown theoretically to reduce intra-class variation in the learned representations. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that this richer convolution can be implemented in a computationally efficient manner through dimensionwise separability, making the additional expressiveness practically viable. The motivation is particularly compelling for noisy and sparse real-world networks, where relying solely on the link graph may be insufficient to distinguish nodes that share structural neighborhoods but differ meaningfully in their attribute profiles.

A complementary line of inquiry questions not the dimensionality of the convolution operator but the static nature of the graph itself. In most graph convolutional architectures, the graph topology is fixed prior to training and held constant across all layers, an assumption that may be poorly suited to tasks where the relevant relational structure evolves as features are transformed through successive network layers. The framework introduced in [arxiv_1909.04931] addresses this by proposing joint dynamic learning of both the graph and the node features within each layer of the network. The graph optimization is cast as a distance metric learning problem, with pairwise node similarities computed via a Mahalanobis distance whose metric matrix is factored into a low-dimensional form for efficiency. This layer-wise adaptation allows the network to construct a graph that is appropriate for the feature geometry at each stage of processing, rather than imposing a single fixed topology throughout. Empirical evaluations on point cloud and citation network benchmarks support the claim that this flexibility yields meaningful performance gains. Comparing this approach to that of [arxiv_1909.12038], both works can be understood as responses to the rigidity of standard graph convolution, but they intervene at different points: the former enriches the convolution operator while keeping the graph structure more conventional, whereas the latter enriches the graph itself while the convolution remains more standard in form.

The question of what relational structures a graph can encode becomes even more pressing when one considers that many real-world networks involve relations that are asymmetric, signed, or varying in their nature across different node pairs. The standard graph Laplacian, which underlies diffusion-based graph convolutional networks, is ill-suited to represent such heterogeneous inter-node relationships. Sheaf neural networks, introduced in [arxiv_2012.06333], address this limitation by replacing the graph Laplacian with the sheaf Laplacian, a generalization that attaches linear maps to edges rather than scalar weights, thereby encoding richer relational structure parameterized by the underlying graph. The resulting diffusion operation can represent non-constant, asymmetric, and dimensionally varying relationships between nodes, which the standard Laplacian cannot. The authors demonstrate that sheaf neural networks outperform conventional graph convolutional networks in settings where such asymmetric and signed relations are present. This contribution is theoretically significant because it identifies a structural deficiency in the mathematical foundation of standard graph convolution rather than merely proposing an architectural modification, and it provides a principled remedy grounded in the mathematics of cellular sheaves.

A fourth perspective on the limitations of standard graph convolution concerns the treatment of multi-relational data. Most graph neural network architectures are designed for single-dimensional graphs in which any pair of nodes is connected by at most one type of relation. However, many real-world networks are inherently multi-dimensional, with multiple distinct relation types linking the same pairs of nodes. The multi-dimensional graph convolutional network framework presented in [arxiv_1808.06099] explicitly models this structure by treating each relation type as a separate dimension and designing convolution operators that can exploit the richer interactions that arise across dimensions. The authors argue that collapsing a multi-dimensional graph into a single-dimensional representation prior to applying convolution discards information that can be critical for both node-level and graph-level tasks. This work can be productively compared with [arxiv_2012.06333] in that both are motivated by the inadequacy of single-type relational representations, but they differ in their mathematical formalisms and in the specific kinds of relational heterogeneity they target: multi-dimensional graphs capture multiplexity in the sense of distinct relation categories, while sheaf neural networks capture variability in the linear-algebraic structure of individual pairwise relationships.

Taken together, these four contributions delineate a coherent research agenda centered on expanding the expressive power of graph neural networks by moving beyond the simplifying assumptions embedded in standard graph convolution. Whether the intervention concerns the dimensionality of the convolution operator [arxiv_1909.12038], the dynamic adaptation of the graph topology [arxiv_1909.04931], the algebraic generalization of the diffusion operator [arxiv_2012.06333], or the explicit modeling of multi-relational structure [arxiv_1808.06099], each approach responds to a specific inadequacy of the baseline framework and offers both theoretical justification and empirical evidence for the value of the proposed generalization.

The expressiveness of graph neural networks has been subjected to rigorous theoretical scrutiny, most influentially through the connection to the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test established in [arxiv_1810.00826]. That work demonstrates that the representational power of any GNN based on neighborhood aggregation is upper-bounded by the 1-WL test, meaning that two graphs indistinguishable by 1-WL cannot be distinguished by such a GNN regardless of the depth or width of the network. The authors further show that the graph isomorphism network (GIN), which uses sum aggregation and a learned perturbation of the central node's feature, achieves this upper bound and is therefore maximally powerful within the MPNN class. This theoretical characterization has had substantial downstream consequences for the field, motivating a wave of work seeking to exceed the WL bound through architectural innovations. One such direction is pursued in [arxiv_1903.02541], which proposes Relational Pooling (RP) as a framework grounded in the theory of finite partial exchangeability. By marginalizing over node orderings, RP can in principle make any existing GNN more powerful than the 1-WL test, including achieving higher-order WL expressiveness. The contrast between these two contributions is instructive: whereas [arxiv_1810.00826] establishes a ceiling for a broad and practically dominant class of architectures, [arxiv_1903.02541] provides a constructive route to breaking through that ceiling, albeit at increased computational cost due to the combinatorial nature of the permutation marginalization. A further attempt to surpass WL-bounded expressiveness is offered by [arxiv_2201.00491], which integrates graph kernel computations directly into the message-passing framework through KerGNNs, enabling subgraph-level feature extraction that standard MPNNs cannot perform. Together, these works illustrate that the theoretical limits of GNN expressiveness are not fixed but depend critically on architectural choices, and that the WL hierarchy provides a productive organizing principle for comparing and situating novel designs.

The challenge of aggregating node-level representations into a single graph-level embedding—the graph pooling problem—has attracted considerable independent attention, and the diversity of proposed solutions reflects genuine disagreement about what properties a good pooling operator should possess. Hierarchical pooling methods, which iteratively coarsen the graph by clustering nodes, have been among the most studied. The DiffPool approach, evaluated empirically alongside other methods in [arxiv_2004.03519], learns soft cluster assignments end-to-end and has been shown to improve classification accuracy on standard benchmarks, particularly when combined with architectures such as TAGCN. EigenPooling [arxiv_1904.13107] takes a spectrally motivated approach, using the eigenvectors of subgraph Laplacians to define pooling regions that preserve local spectral information. Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool), introduced in [arxiv_1904.08082], instead uses a graph convolutional layer to compute attention scores that determine which nodes are retained in a top-k selection scheme, thereby coupling the pooling decision to the learned node representations. Hierarchical Graph Pooling with Structure Learning [arxiv_1911.05954] further augments this paradigm by jointly learning the pooling assignment and the graph structure at each coarsening step, addressing the concern that fixed-topology pooling may discard structurally important information. A unifying perspective on these hierarchical approaches is offered by the multi-level attention pooling framework of [arxiv_2103.01488], which explicitly reconciles representations obtained at different levels of the hierarchy by attending over multiple localities simultaneously. Complementing hierarchical methods, flat pooling operators such as second-order pooling [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785] capture covariance structure among node features rather than relying on simple summation or averaging, providing richer statistical summaries of the node set. The Graph Multiset Transformer proposed in [arxiv_2102.11533] approaches pooling as a set-to-vector problem and employs self-attention over the full node set to produce a permutation-invariant graph representation that accounts for structural dependencies among nodes. Function space pooling [arxiv_1905.06259] offers yet another perspective by mapping the set of node embeddings into a function space representation, enabling a form of pooling that is sensitive to the distributional properties of the node feature set. The breadth of these contributions underscores that pooling is not a peripheral concern but a central design decision whose consequences for downstream task performance are substantial and task-dependent, and that no single method dominates across all settings [arxiv_2205.15555, arxiv_2307.00865].

A recurring practical concern that cuts across both the convolution and pooling literature is the phenomenon of over-smoothing, whereby repeated neighborhood aggregation causes node representations to converge to indistinguishable vectors, effectively erasing the local structural information that the network is intended to capture. This pathology imposes a practical depth limit on many GNN architectures and has motivated several architectural responses. The multipath graph convolutional network proposed in [arxiv_2105.01510] addresses over-smoothing by routing information through multiple parallel paths of varying depth, allowing the network to simultaneously exploit both shallow and deep aggregation without forcing all information through the same number of hops. The multi-scale deep graph convolutional network analyzed in [arxiv_1906.02174] provides a complementary theoretical treatment, demonstrating that the expressive power of GCNs is constrained not only by depth but also by the choice of activation function, and proposing architectural modifications that permit deeper networks to remain discriminative. The multi-channel graph convolutional network of [arxiv_1912.08306] addresses a related but distinct issue: rather than simply deepening the network, it maintains multiple parallel graph representations that are combined through a learned fusion mechanism, thereby preserving complementary structural signals that a single-channel hierarchical architecture would discard. These contributions collectively suggest that the depth-expressiveness trade-off in GNNs is more nuanced than in standard CNNs, and that naive depth scaling is insufficient without accompanying architectural safeguards against over-smoothing and information loss.
