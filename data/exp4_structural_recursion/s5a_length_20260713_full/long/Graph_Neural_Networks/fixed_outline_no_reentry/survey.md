# Graph Neural Networks (long)

### Graph Pooling Methods

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a transformative paradigm for learning from graph-structured data, extending the representational power of deep learning beyond Euclidean domains to complex relational structures. The foundational motivation for this line of research stems from the ubiquity of graph-structured data across scientific and engineering disciplines, including social networks, citation networks, molecular graphs, and knowledge graphs. As articulated in the comprehensive survey by [arxiv_1901.00596], deep learning has traditionally excelled on data representable in Euclidean space, such as images and text, but a growing number of real-world applications involve data generated from non-Euclidean domains, represented as graphs with intricate relationships and interdependencies between entities. This complexity has imposed significant challenges on classical machine learning algorithms, motivating the development of GNNs as a principled framework for graph representation learning. The survey proposes a taxonomy that organizes the state-of-the-art GNN architectures into four broad categories, reflecting the diversity of methodological approaches that have emerged in the field [arxiv_1901.00596]. Similarly, [arxiv_2102.02026] situates GNNs within the broader context of social and information networks, emphasizing that knowledge representation through graphs must preserve as many characteristics of the original data as possible, and surveys graph convolutional neural networks, graph autoencoders, and spatio-temporal GNNs as mechanisms for learning lower-dimensional representations suitable for downstream tasks such as graph classification, node classification, and link prediction.

A central architectural component of GNNs is the message-passing mechanism, wherein node representations are iteratively updated by aggregating information from local neighborhoods. This paradigm, commonly referred to as message-passing neural networks (MPNNs), has achieved considerable empirical success across a variety of real-world applications. However, [arxiv_2002.05287] identifies two fundamental weaknesses in the aggregation strategies employed by standard MPNNs: the loss of structural information about nodes within their neighborhoods, and an inability to capture long-range dependencies in disassortative graphs, where connected nodes tend to belong to different classes. To address these limitations, [arxiv_2002.05287] proposes Geom-GCN, a geometric aggregation scheme that draws inspiration from classical neural networks and network geometry. The core insight is that aggregation on a graph can benefit from an underlying continuous geometric space, and the proposed scheme incorporates three modules—node embedding, structural neighborhood definition, and a bi-level aggregation operator—that together yield a permutation-invariant aggregation capable of preserving structural information and capturing disassortative patterns. This work highlights that the design of the aggregation function is not merely an implementation detail but a fundamental determinant of a model's representational capacity.

The question of representational power is further examined through the lens of graph isomorphism and the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) algorithm, which has served as a theoretical benchmark for evaluating the discriminative capacity of GNNs. [arxiv_1903.02541] generalizes GNNs beyond architectures grounded in the WL algorithm, graph Laplacians, and diffusion processes by introducing Relational Pooling (RP), a framework derived from the theory of finite partial exchangeability. Notably, RP can be applied on top of existing graph representation models and, somewhat counterintuitively, can render them more powerful than the original WL isomorphism test. A particularly compelling aspect of this approach is its theoretical flexibility: RP permits architectures such as Recurrent Neural Networks and Convolutional Neural Networks to be employed in a theoretically sound manner for graph classification, thereby broadening the toolkit available to practitioners. Empirical evaluations reported in [arxiv_1903.02541] demonstrate improved performance over state-of-the-art methods across multiple graph classification benchmarks, underscoring the practical value of the theoretical generalization.

Once node-level representations have been learned through message-passing or related mechanisms, a critical challenge arises in aggregating these representations into a single graph-level embedding suitable for graph classification or regression. This process, broadly termed graph pooling, must contend with the variable sizes and isomorphic structures of graphs, making it a non-trivial design problem. [arxiv_2004.03519] provides an empirical evaluation of several pooling methods in combination with three distinct GNN architectures: GCN, TAGCN, and GraphSAGE. The study confirms that graph pooling, and DiffPool in particular, improves classification accuracy on popular graph classification datasets. Importantly, the results indicate that TAGCN achieves comparable or superior accuracy relative to GCN and GraphSAGE, especially on datasets characterized by larger and sparser graph structures, suggesting that the choice of backbone architecture interacts meaningfully with the pooling strategy employed [arxiv_2004.03519].

Hierarchical pooling methods, which progressively coarsen the graph while preserving structural information, represent an important subclass of pooling approaches. [arxiv_1904.13107] motivates hierarchical pooling by observing that globally combining node representations, while common, overlooks rich structural information encoded in the graph topology. Drawing an analogy to the pooling step in conventional convolutional neural networks for image processing, the authors propose EigenPooling, a method that leverages spectral graph theory—specifically, the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian—to define pooling operations that are sensitive to local subgraph structure. This approach enables the hierarchical learning of graph representations that retain topological information at multiple scales, addressing a key limitation of flat, global pooling strategies. The connection to spectral methods situates EigenPooling within a broader tradition of graph signal processing approaches to GNN design [arxiv_1904.13107].

An alternative perspective on graph pooling is offered by second-order pooling methods, which seek to capture higher-order statistical dependencies among node representations. [arxiv_2007.10467] and its companion publication [ss_477785] propose using second-order pooling as a graph-level aggregation mechanism, arguing that it naturally addresses the challenges posed by variable graph sizes and isomorphic structures. In contrast to first-order pooling methods such as sum, mean, or max aggregation, second-order pooling collects second-order statistics from all nodes, thereby capturing pairwise interactions among node features and providing a richer characterization of the graph. The authors acknowledge, however, that the direct application of second-order pooling to GNNs introduces practical difficulties, including computational cost and numerical instability, and propose two novel global pooling methods—bilinear mapping-based approaches—to overcome these issues [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785]. The convergence of these two publications on the same methodological contribution reflects the growing recognition that the statistical order of the pooling operation is a meaningful design dimension, complementing architectural choices such as the depth of the message-passing network or the type of aggregation function.

Taken together, these contributions delineate a rich and multifaceted research landscape in which GNNs are developed and evaluated along several interacting dimensions: the design of the message-passing or aggregation mechanism, the theoretical characterization of representational power, the development of pooling strategies for graph-level tasks, and the empirical validation of these choices across diverse benchmark datasets. The interplay between theoretical grounding and empirical performance is a recurring theme. For instance, while [arxiv_1903.02541] grounds its contribution in the theory of partial exchangeability and demonstrates improvements over WL-based methods, [arxiv_2004.03519] takes a more empirical stance, systematically comparing pooling methods and backbone architectures to identify practically effective combinations. Similarly, [arxiv_2002.05287] identifies structural limitations of existing MPNNs through theoretical analysis and proposes a geometrically motivated remedy, whereas [arxiv_1904.13107] draws on spectral graph theory to construct pooling operators with desirable structural properties.

The broader survey literature, as represented by [arxiv_1901.00596] and [arxiv_2102.02026], provides essential context for situating these individual contributions within the overall trajectory of the field. Both works emphasize the versatility of GNNs across tasks—node classification, link prediction, and graph classification—and the diversity of architectural paradigms that have been proposed, from convolutional and recurrent approaches to autoencoders and spatio-temporal models. This diversity reflects the adaptability of the GNN framework to different data modalities and task requirements, and underscores the importance of principled methods for evaluating and comparing approaches across a common set of benchmarks. The cumulative body of work reviewed here establishes graph pooling and aggregation design as central open problems in GNN research, with ongoing contributions from both theoretical and empirical perspectives continuing to advance the state of the art.

### Graph Convolutional Network Architectures

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a powerful paradigm for learning representations over non-Euclidean, graph-structured data, and the literature has rapidly expanded to address a wide range of architectural, theoretical, and applicative concerns. The foundational challenge motivating much of this work is that classical convolutional neural networks are inherently designed for Euclidean data such as images and sequences, and their direct application to graphs is non-trivial. As [arxiv_2307.00865] observes, traditional convolutional neural networks are limited to handling Euclidean space data, overlooking the vast realm of real-life scenarios represented as graph data, including transportation networks, social networks, and reference networks. The pivotal step in transferring convolutional neural networks to graph data therefore lies in the construction of appropriate graph convolutional operators and graph pooling operators. This recognition has driven a substantial body of research aimed at generalizing convolution, pooling, and embedding mechanisms to the graph domain, each contribution addressing distinct limitations of prior approaches.

One of the earliest and most influential directions in this space concerns the extension of convolutional learning to arbitrary graph structures. [arxiv_1605.05273] proposes a framework for learning convolutional neural networks for arbitrary graphs, accommodating undirected and directed graphs as well as those with both discrete and continuous node and edge attributes. Analogous to image-based convolutional networks that operate on locally connected regions of the input, their approach presents a general method for extracting locally connected regions from graphs, yielding learned feature representations that are competitive with state-of-the-art graph kernels while remaining computationally efficient. This work established an important precedent: that the inductive bias of local connectivity, so productive in image processing, could be meaningfully translated to the irregular topology of graphs. The efficiency and generality of this framework made it a reference point against which subsequent spectral and spatial methods would be compared.

Building on the general framework of graph convolution, a significant strand of research has focused on the theoretical expressiveness and depth of graph convolutional networks. [arxiv_1906.02174] provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of how existing GCNs have limited expressive power due to constraints imposed by activation functions and architectural choices. The authors generalize spectral graph convolution and deep GCN in block Krylov subspace forms, devising two architectures that each have the potential to be scaled deeper while exploiting multi-scale information in different ways. Crucially, they demonstrate that the equivalence of these two architectures can be established under certain conditions, providing a unifying theoretical perspective. The work directly addresses the bottleneck of shallow architectures that has constrained many prior GCN models, arguing that the advantages of multi-scale information and deep architectures have not been sufficiently exploited in earlier designs. This theoretical grounding distinguishes [arxiv_1906.02174] from more empirically motivated proposals, as it offers principled guidance for architectural choices rather than relying solely on benchmark performance.

Complementing this theoretical perspective, [arxiv_1906.02319] identifies a set of specific limitations in existing graph neural networks that are both structural and conceptual in nature. The authors note that most existing models offer limited analysis of graph convolution properties such as seed-orientation, degree-awareness, and order-freedom; that node degree-specific graph structure is not explicitly expressed in graph convolution for distinguishing structure-aware node neighborhoods; and that the theoretical explanation of graph-level pooling schemes remains unclear. In response, they propose DEMO-Net, a degree-specific graph neural network motivated by the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, which recursively identifies one-hop neighborhood structures. By making degree-specific information an explicit component of the convolution operation, DEMO-Net addresses a structural asymmetry that uniform aggregation schemes overlook: nodes of different degrees occupy fundamentally different structural positions within a graph, and treating them identically during message passing conflates structurally distinct contexts. This degree-aware perspective offers a meaningful contrast to the more uniform aggregation strategies implicit in many spectral GCN formulations, including those analyzed in [arxiv_1906.02174].

A parallel and equally important line of inquiry concerns the representation of graph elements beyond nodes. Most early GCN methods focus exclusively on node-level representations, but many real-world tasks require reasoning about edges as first-class entities. [arxiv_2010.13242] and its companion publication [ss_477653] directly address this gap by proposing a co-embedding framework for nodes and edges within a unified GCN architecture. The authors observe that while deep learning approaches generally outperform traditional methods on graph learning benchmarks by building end-to-end frameworks that optimize loss functions directly, most existing GCN methods can only handle node features and fail to explicitly model edge information. Their co-embedding approach encodes both nodes and edges in a shared representational space, enabling the network to capture relational information that purely node-centric models would miss. This is particularly consequential in domains such as biology and social network analysis, where edge attributes carry semantically rich information. The dual publication of this work across venues [arxiv_2010.13242] and [ss_477653] reflects the broad relevance of the co-embedding problem and the community's recognition that edge representations are not merely auxiliary but often central to the learning task.

The question of how to aggregate node-level representations into a single graph-level representation—the pooling problem—has received sustained attention in the literature, and several distinct approaches have been proposed. [arxiv_1905.06259] introduces a novel pooling method that maps a set of vertex representations to a function space representation, in contrast to existing pooling methods that perform mappings to either a vector or sequence space. The authors argue that function space pooling captures richer structural information by representing the distribution of vertex embeddings as a function rather than collapsing it into a fixed-dimensional vector. Experimental results on graph classification benchmarks demonstrate that this method generally outperforms most baseline pooling methods and in some cases achieves best performance. This contribution is significant because it reframes pooling not merely as a dimensionality reduction step but as a representational choice with direct implications for the expressiveness of the resulting graph embedding.

A different approach to the pooling problem is offered by [arxiv_2302.13399], which extends the path integral based convolution and pooling framework to heterogeneous graphs. The path integral formulation involves a convolution operation that considers every path linking a message sender and receiver, with learnable weights depending on path length, corresponding to the maximal entropy random walk. This yields a new transition matrix called the maximal entropy transition (MET), whose diagonal entries are directly related to subgraph centrality, providing a principled mechanism for pooling based on centrality scores. Importantly, [arxiv_2302.13399] extends this framework beyond the original homogeneous graph setting to heterogeneous graphs, which contain multiple types of nodes and edges. This extension is non-trivial because heterogeneous graphs require the convolution and pooling operations to respect type distinctions, and the path integral formulation provides a natural way to encode path-length-dependent structural information across heterogeneous topologies. Comparing [arxiv_1905.06259] and [arxiv_2302.13399], both address the pooling problem but from fundamentally different perspectives: the former approaches it as a function space mapping problem, while the latter grounds it in the structural notion of subgraph centrality derived from random walk theory. Together, they illustrate the diversity of principled strategies available for graph-level representation learning.

The survey perspective offered by [arxiv_2307.00865] provides a useful integrative view of these developments, elaborating on the fundamentals of graph convolutional neural networks and elucidating models based on attention mechanisms and autoencoders, while summarizing their application across node classification, graph classification, and link prediction tasks. This breadth of coverage underscores a key feature of the GNN literature: the same architectural innovations tend to find application across multiple task types, with node classification, graph classification, and link prediction each placing distinct demands on the representational capacity of the model. Attention-based models, for instance, allow the network to weight the contributions of different neighbors adaptively, addressing a limitation of uniform aggregation that is relevant both to node-level and graph-level tasks.

Taken together, the works reviewed here trace a coherent arc of development in the GNN literature. Early contributions such as [arxiv_1605.05273] established the feasibility of learning convolutional representations on arbitrary graphs. Subsequent work identified specific architectural limitations—whether related to depth and expressiveness [arxiv_1906.02174], degree-specific structure [arxiv_1906.02319], or the neglect of edge information [arxiv_2010.13242, ss_477653]—and proposed targeted solutions. Concurrently, the pooling problem attracted dedicated attention, with [arxiv_1905.06259] and [arxiv_2302.13399] offering complementary function-space and centrality-based approaches respectively. The survey [arxiv_2307.00865] contextualizes these contributions within the broader landscape of graph learning tasks. What emerges from this body of work is a picture of a field that has moved progressively from demonstrating the basic viability of graph convolution toward a more nuanced understanding of the structural, theoretical, and representational factors that determine the effectiveness of GNN architectures across diverse problem settings.

### Evidence theme 3

The application of convolutional neural network principles to graph-structured data has generated a rich and rapidly evolving body of literature, with researchers pursuing diverse architectural strategies to overcome the fundamental challenge that graphs, unlike images or sequences, lack a regular Euclidean structure. This subsection surveys the principal directions in graph neural network design, examining spectral filtering approaches, spatial and message-passing formulations, pooling mechanisms, and hybrid frameworks that integrate classical graph kernels with modern deep learning.

A foundational tension in the field concerns whether convolution on graphs should be defined in the spectral domain or the spatial domain. Spectral approaches ground their operations in graph signal processing theory, treating node feature vectors as signals defined on the vertices of a graph and leveraging the graph Laplacian's eigendecomposition to perform filtering in the frequency domain [arxiv_2008.01247]. The graph signal processing perspective, as articulated by Ortega and colleagues, provides a principled framework for extending the core components of convolutional neural networks—convolution, pooling, and architectural topology—to irregular graph domains [arxiv_2008.01247]. Within this tradition, the most widely adopted early models relied on polynomial spectral filters, which approximate the full spectral decomposition through truncated Chebyshev expansions or similar polynomial bases, thereby avoiding the computationally prohibitive eigendecomposition of large Laplacian matrices. While polynomial filters offer computational tractability and locality in the node domain, they carry inherent limitations: their frequency responses are constrained by the polynomial form, making it difficult to achieve sharp or highly selective filtering, and they can be sensitive to noise in the graph structure [arxiv_1901.01343].

To address these shortcomings, Bianchi and colleagues proposed replacing polynomial spectral filters with auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) filters, drawing an analogy to classical signal processing where ARMA filters are known to provide richer frequency responses than finite impulse response (FIR) filters of comparable order [ss_476188]. The ARMA graph convolutional layer is formulated recursively and distributedly across nodes, yielding a layer that remains localized in the node space and can be transferred to new graphs at inference time without retraining—a property of considerable practical importance for inductive learning settings [arxiv_1901.01343]. The spectral analysis reported in these works demonstrates that ARMA filters can better approximate ideal frequency responses and are more robust to perturbations in the graph topology, which is particularly valuable when working with noisy real-world networks [ss_476188]. Empirical evaluations spanning semi-supervised node classification, graph signal classification, graph classification, and graph regression confirm that ARMA-based networks consistently match or outperform polynomial filter baselines across these diverse tasks [arxiv_1901.01343]. The convergence of results across the two closely related presentations of this work [ss_476188] and [arxiv_1901.01343] reinforces the robustness of the ARMA filter's advantages and underscores the broader principle that richer filter parameterizations can yield meaningful performance gains without sacrificing the desirable properties of locality and transferability.

Parallel to spectral developments, a separate line of research has pursued spatial or message-passing formulations of graph convolution, in which node representations are updated by aggregating information from local neighborhoods. The message passing neural network (MPNN) framework has become the dominant paradigm in this spatial tradition, unifying a wide range of graph neural network architectures under a common computational template [arxiv_2201.00491]. Despite their empirical success, MPNN-based models have been shown to be theoretically bounded in their expressive power by the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test, meaning that certain pairs of non-isomorphic graphs will produce identical representations regardless of the depth or width of the network [arxiv_2201.00491]. This theoretical ceiling motivates ongoing efforts to augment or replace the standard message-passing mechanism with more powerful computational primitives.

One such effort is the Kernel Graph Neural Networks (KerGNNs) framework, which integrates graph kernels directly into the message-passing process [arxiv_2201.00491]. Rather than treating graph kernels and graph neural networks as competing paradigms, KerGNNs synthesize their complementary strengths: graph kernels provide interpretable, combinatorially rich structural features, while neural networks supply the capacity for end-to-end representation learning. Inspired by the role of convolutional filters in image-based CNNs, KerGNNs use trainable hidden graphs as filters and compute kernel values between these filters and subgraphs encountered during message passing, thereby enriching the information aggregated at each node [arxiv_2201.00491]. This approach directly addresses the WL expressiveness bottleneck while preserving the interpretability that has historically made graph kernels attractive for practitioners. The KerGNNs work thus occupies an interesting position in the literature, bridging the historically separate communities of kernel methods and deep learning on graphs.

A complementary challenge to the design of convolutional layers is the design of pooling operations that can coarsen graph representations in a principled way. In image-based CNNs, pooling is straightforward because the regular grid structure permits uniform downsampling; on graphs, the absence of a canonical ordering makes pooling substantially more difficult [arxiv_1904.08082]. Several pooling strategies have been proposed, including methods based on graph clustering, differentiable node scoring, and attention mechanisms. The Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool) method addresses this challenge by using graph convolution itself to compute attention scores for each node, which are then used to select a subset of nodes to retain in the pooled graph [arxiv_1904.08082]. Because the attention scores are computed using graph convolution, they incorporate both node feature information and graph topology, enabling the pooling operation to make structurally informed decisions about which nodes are most informative for the downstream task. Experimental results reported in that work demonstrate that SAGPool achieves competitive or superior performance compared to existing pooling methods on standard graph classification benchmarks, while maintaining a relatively modest parameter count [arxiv_1904.08082]. The self-attention mechanism also provides a degree of interpretability, as the learned attention scores can be inspected to understand which parts of a graph the model considers most relevant.

Beyond the design of individual layers, several works have examined how graph neural network architectures should be composed and structured at a higher level. A recurring concern is the phenomenon of over-smoothing, whereby stacking many graph convolutional layers causes node representations to converge to indistinguishable values as information is diffused across the graph [arxiv_2105.01510]. This problem is exacerbated by the vanishing gradient issue that affects deep networks generally, and by overfitting when model capacity is increased without sufficient regularization. Shallow networks avoid these pathologies but sacrifice expressive power. The Multipath Graph Convolutional Neural Network (MPGCNN) addresses this dilemma by aggregating the outputs of multiple shallow networks operating in parallel rather than stacking layers sequentially [arxiv_2105.01510]. This multipath design allows the model to capture information at multiple scales of neighborhood aggregation simultaneously, without the degradation associated with deep sequential architectures. Evaluations on node property prediction benchmarks show that MPGCNN achieves higher test accuracy than single-path counterparts while also requiring fewer training epochs to converge, suggesting that the multipath structure provides both a representational and an optimization advantage [arxiv_2105.01510].

Another architectural consideration concerns the treatment of edge features, which carry important relational information in many real-world graphs but are neglected by many standard graph neural network formulations that focus exclusively on node features. Monti and colleagues proposed new formulations for convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers that make more comprehensive use of multi-dimensional edge information [arxiv_1811.00052]. By explicitly incorporating edge features into the convolution operation rather than treating edges merely as structural connectors, these layers can capture richer relational patterns and achieve improved classification accuracy on benchmark graph datasets compared to methods that process node features alone [arxiv_1811.00052]. This work highlights a general principle that the full information content of a graph—residing in both vertices and edges—should ideally be exploited by graph neural network architectures.

A notably different approach to graph classification sidesteps the question of graph-specific neural network design altogether by transforming graphs into image-like representations that can be processed by standard two-dimensional CNNs [arxiv_1708.02218]. This method encodes graphs as multi-channel images, preserving structural and attribute information in a format amenable to vanilla CNN processing. The motivation is partly pragmatic: 2D CNNs are highly optimized, well-understood, and benefit from extensive tooling and pretrained models. Experiments reported in that work show that this image-based approach outperforms state-of-the-art graph kernels and graph CNNs on four out of six real-world datasets, including both attributed and non-attributed graphs, and compares favorably in terms of time complexity [arxiv_1708.02218]. While this approach is elegant in its simplicity, it raises questions about the fidelity of the graph-to-image transformation and whether structural information is faithfully preserved across diverse graph types.

Taken together, the works surveyed in this subsection reveal a field characterized by productive tension between competing design philosophies. Spectral methods grounded in graph signal processing theory [arxiv_2008.01247] offer principled frequency-domain interpretations and have been extended through richer filter parameterizations such as ARMA filters [ss_476188, arxiv_1901.01343]. Spatial message-passing frameworks have achieved broad empirical success but face theoretical expressiveness limits that motivate hybrid approaches integrating graph kernels [arxiv_2201.00491]. Pooling remains an active research frontier, with attention-based methods such as SAGPool [arxiv_1904.08082] offering promising directions. Architectural innovations including multipath designs [arxiv_2105.01510] and explicit edge feature modeling [arxiv_1811.00052] address complementary limitations of standard layer-stacking approaches, while unconventional strategies such as graph-to-image conversion [arxiv_1708.02218] demonstrate that competitive performance can sometimes be achieved through creative problem reformulation rather than bespoke graph-specific architectures. The diversity of these contributions reflects both the richness of the graph learning problem and the absence of a single dominant solution applicable across all graph types and downstream tasks.

### Evidence theme 4

The structural assumptions embedded in graph neural network architectures have profound consequences for the kinds of relational data they can effectively model. A substantial body of work has therefore been devoted to relaxing or generalizing the foundational design choices of early GNN frameworks, particularly the assumption that graphs are homogeneous, single-relational, and fixed in topology. The papers reviewed in this subsection collectively address these limitations through a range of complementary strategies, including multi-relational and typed graph modeling, dynamic graph structure learning, sheaf-theoretic generalizations of diffusion, and more expressive pooling mechanisms for graph-level representation.

One of the most persistent constraints in standard graph convolutional networks is their reliance on a single, static adjacency structure. When the graph is misspecified or when the true relational structure is heterogeneous, the representational capacity of conventional GNNs degrades substantially. Graph Transformer Networks (GTNs) directly confront this problem by learning to generate new graph structures in an end-to-end fashion [arxiv_1911.06455]. Rather than accepting the input graph topology as given, GTNs employ a Graph Transformer layer that performs a soft selection over edge types and computes composite relations, effectively identifying useful connections between nodes that are not directly linked in the original graph. This capacity to synthesize new adjacency matrices from combinations of existing edge-type channels is particularly valuable in heterogeneous graph settings, where the semantically meaningful paths between nodes may span multiple edge types. The GTN framework thus treats graph structure not as a fixed input but as a latent variable to be optimized jointly with node representations, a conceptual shift that distinguishes it from the majority of prior GNN architectures.

A related but distinct approach to handling typed and relational structure is offered by Typed Graph Networks [arxiv_1901.07984], which situate the problem within the broader message-passing paradigm and emphasize the role of parameter sharing across typed entities. Drawing an analogy to how convolutional neural networks enforce weight sharing over a spatial grid, Typed Graph Networks instantiate trainable neural modules that are assembled according to the typed structure of the graph, allowing the model to respect the heterogeneity of node and edge types while still benefiting from the inductive biases of shared parameters. Whereas GTNs [arxiv_1911.06455] focus on generating new graph structures through soft edge-type selection, Typed Graph Networks [arxiv_1901.07984] take the typed structure as given and concentrate on how to parameterize message passing in a way that is sensitive to those types. Together, these two approaches illustrate a complementary pair of strategies: one that learns which structural connections are relevant, and one that learns how to process information along connections whose types are already known.

The challenge of multi-relational structure is also addressed from a graph-theoretic perspective in Multi-dimensional Graph Convolutional Networks [arxiv_1808.06099], which model graphs in which pairs of nodes may be connected by multiple distinct relation types, each constituting a separate dimension of the graph. This multi-dimensional formulation is well-suited to real-world networks such as social platforms, where users may be connected by friendship, co-authorship, and shared group membership simultaneously. By treating each relation type as a separate graph layer and designing convolutional operations that aggregate information across both the node neighborhood and the relational dimensions, this approach captures richer interaction patterns than single-relational GNNs. The multi-dimensional framework shares with GTNs [arxiv_1911.06455] a concern for heterogeneous relational structure, but whereas GTNs learn to compose and reweight edge types dynamically, multi-dimensional GCNs [arxiv_1808.06099] operate on a fixed multi-relational input and focus on the design of cross-dimensional aggregation operators.

A fundamentally different generalization of graph convolution is proposed in Sheaf Neural Networks [arxiv_2012.06333], which replace the standard graph Laplacian with the sheaf Laplacian, a mathematical object from algebraic topology that encodes richer relational structure between nodes. In a sheaf neural network, each edge is associated with a linear map between the feature spaces of its endpoint nodes, allowing the model to represent asymmetric, signed, and dimensionally varying relationships that cannot be captured by a scalar edge weight. The sheaf Laplacian generalizes the graph Laplacian by parameterizing these inter-node relations, and the resulting diffusion operation is correspondingly more expressive. Empirical results reported in [arxiv_2012.06333] demonstrate that sheaf neural networks outperform standard graph convolutional networks in settings where node relationships are asymmetric and signed, precisely the settings where the homogeneity assumption of conventional GNNs is most damaging. Compared to GTNs [arxiv_1911.06455] and Typed Graph Networks [arxiv_1901.07984], which address heterogeneity primarily through edge-type selection and typed parameterization respectively, sheaf neural networks offer a more algebraically principled generalization that operates at the level of the diffusion operator itself.

The question of how to learn appropriate graph structure when it is not reliably provided as input is taken up from a different angle in the work on joint learning of graph representations and node features in graph convolutional neural networks [arxiv_1909.04931]. This approach casts graph structure learning as a distance metric learning problem, using the Mahalanobis distance to capture pairwise feature similarities and constructing a dynamic adjacency matrix at each layer. Crucially, the graph is not fixed across layers; instead, it is re-estimated at each depth level to reflect the evolving feature representations produced by successive convolutions. This layer-wise adaptivity addresses a recognized limitation of architectures that apply a single learned or fixed graph throughout the network, since the optimal connectivity structure for aggregating low-level features may differ substantially from that appropriate for higher-level representations. The decomposition of the metric matrix into a low-dimensional factor further ensures computational tractability. This work shares with GTNs [arxiv_1911.06455] the goal of learning graph structure rather than accepting it as fixed, but differs in that it operates on homogeneous graphs and focuses on continuous metric-based structure learning rather than discrete edge-type composition.

Complementing these structural generalizations, a separate line of work addresses the challenge of capturing information at multiple scales of graph structure, which is particularly relevant for graph-level classification tasks. Standard message-passing GNNs propagate information one hop per layer, meaning that global structural information is only accessible in deeper layers, while local information may be lost as depth increases. This tension between local and global representations is a well-documented source of performance degradation in deep GNNs. Multi-Level Attention Pooling (MLAP) [arxiv_2103.01488] addresses this by aggregating node representations from multiple layers before performing graph-level pooling, using attention mechanisms to weight the contributions of different layers adaptively. By unifying representations with multiple localities, MLAP avoids the need to choose between shallow models that capture local structure and deep models that capture global structure, instead learning to combine both. This approach is conceptually related to the residual and jumping knowledge connections proposed in other GNN architectures, but is specifically designed for graph-level classification and integrates the multi-scale aggregation directly into the pooling step.

The graph pooling problem more broadly is the subject of Graph Multiset Pooling [arxiv_2102.11533], which formulates pooling as a multiset encoding problem and argues that existing methods, including both simple global averaging and hierarchical pooling approaches, fail to adequately capture structural dependencies among node representations. Simple averaging treats all nodes as equally relevant regardless of their task importance, while hierarchical methods may produce identical representations for structurally distinct graphs that are nevertheless distinguishable by the Weisfeiler-Lehman test. Graph Multiset Pooling addresses both failure modes by encoding the full set of node representations with auxiliary structural information, producing a compact graph-level representation that is both expressive and task-aware. Compared to MLAP [arxiv_2103.01488], which focuses on multi-scale aggregation across layers, Graph Multiset Pooling [arxiv_2102.11533] focuses on the expressiveness of the pooling operation itself at a single level of representation, and the two approaches are therefore complementary rather than competing.

Finally, the work on Dimensionwise Separable 2-D Graph Convolution [arxiv_1909.12038] introduces a further structural generalization by proposing to perform convolution jointly over both the object-link graph and the attribute-relation graph, yielding a 2-D graph convolution that captures dependencies along both dimensions simultaneously. Standard GCN variants operate only on the object-link graph, ignoring potentially informative relational structure among node attributes. By modeling attribute relations as a second graph and applying separable convolutions across both dimensions, this approach reduces intra-class variation and improves robustness to noisy and sparse real-world networks. The dimensionwise separability ensures that the computational cost remains manageable despite the expanded structural modeling. This work is particularly relevant in the context of unsupervised and semi-supervised learning, where the additional signal from attribute relations can compensate for the scarcity of labeled data.

Taken together, the works reviewed in this subsection reveal a consistent trajectory in GNN research toward architectures that are more structurally flexible, relationally expressive, and multi-scale in their representational capacity. Whether through typed parameterization [arxiv_1901.07984], soft edge-type composition [arxiv_1911.06455], multi-dimensional relational modeling [arxiv_1808.06099], sheaf-theoretic diffusion [arxiv_2012.06333], dynamic structure learning [arxiv_1909.04931], 2-D attribute-aware convolution [arxiv_1909.12038], or more expressive pooling [arxiv_2102.11533, arxiv_2103.01488], each contribution identifies a specific limitation of the standard GNN paradigm and proposes a principled mechanism for overcoming it. The diversity of these approaches underscores both the richness of the problem space and the absence of a single universal solution.

The preceding discussion has focused primarily on architectural innovations within the GNN paradigm, but a complementary and equally important strand of research concerns the theoretical foundations that determine what GNNs can and cannot represent. The seminal analysis of GNN expressive power by Xu et al. [arxiv_1810.00826] establishes a formal connection between the discriminative capacity of message-passing GNNs and the Weisfeiler-Lehman (WL) graph isomorphism test. The central result is that any GNN following the standard neighborhood aggregation scheme is at most as powerful as the 1-WL test in distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs, and that this upper bound is achievable when the aggregation and combination functions are injective. This theoretical ceiling has profound practical implications: it means that GNNs cannot distinguish certain pairs of structurally distinct graphs that are nevertheless indistinguishable by 1-WL, such as regular graphs of the same degree. The Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) proposed in the same work instantiates the theoretical maximum by using sum aggregation with a learnable epsilon parameter, and empirically outperforms mean- and max-based aggregators on graph classification benchmarks. This analysis provides a unifying lens through which many of the architectural choices discussed elsewhere in this review can be evaluated: methods that go beyond 1-WL expressiveness, such as Relational Pooling [arxiv_1903.02541], do so by breaking the symmetry assumptions that constrain standard message passing, while methods such as KerGNNs [arxiv_2201.00491] integrate graph kernel computations within the GNN framework to capture substructure information that eludes the WL hierarchy. KerGNNs are particularly notable in that they bridge the historically separate traditions of graph kernels and graph neural networks, using trainable hidden graphs as kernel prototypes within each layer and thereby combining the interpretability of kernel methods with the representational flexibility of deep learning.

The expressiveness question is also central to the design of pooling operators, which must preserve sufficient structural information to support downstream classification while reducing the variable-size graph to a fixed-size representation. The EigenPooling method [arxiv_1904.13107] addresses this by exploiting the spectral properties of graph Laplacians within local subgraphs, using eigenvectors to define pooling operations that are sensitive to the local community structure. Because the eigenvectors of the Laplacian encode the connectivity patterns of a subgraph, EigenPooling retains structural information that purely feature-based pooling methods discard. This spectral grounding connects EigenPooling to the broader tradition of graph signal processing [arxiv_2008.01247], which provides a rigorous framework for understanding convolution and pooling on graphs in terms of frequency-domain operations. From the signal processing perspective, pooling corresponds to graph coarsening, and the quality of a pooling operator can be assessed by how faithfully the coarsened graph preserves the spectral properties of the original. Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool) [arxiv_1904.08082] takes a different approach, using graph convolutional layers to compute attention scores that determine which nodes to retain in a hierarchical pooling step. By conditioning the attention scores on both node features and graph structure, SAGPool produces pooled graphs that reflect task-relevant structural patterns, and the hierarchical application of this operator enables the construction of deep graph classification architectures. Hierarchical Graph Pooling with Structure Learning [arxiv_1911.05954] extends this line of work by jointly learning the pooling assignment and a refined graph structure at each hierarchical level, arguing that the graph topology used for pooling should itself be optimized rather than inherited unchanged from the input. The combination of structure learning and hierarchical pooling in a single framework represents a significant step toward end-to-end optimization of the full graph representation pipeline.

Second-order pooling methods [arxiv_2007.10467, ss_477785] offer yet another perspective on the expressiveness of graph-level representations. Whereas first-order pooling methods such as sum, mean, or attention-weighted aggregation produce representations that are linear functions of the node feature matrix, second-order pooling computes the covariance or outer-product structure of the node representations, capturing pairwise feature interactions that are invisible to first-order methods. This is directly analogous to the use of second-order statistics in image recognition, where covariance pooling has been shown to substantially improve performance over global average pooling. In the graph setting, second-order pooling naturally handles graphs of variable size and is invariant to node permutations, both of which are fundamental requirements for graph-level representation. The empirical results reported in these works demonstrate consistent improvements over first-order baselines across multiple graph classification benchmarks, suggesting that the additional expressiveness of second-order statistics is practically significant and not merely a theoretical curiosity. Taken alongside Function Space Pooling [arxiv_1905.06259], which maps node representations into a function space and integrates over that space to produce a graph-level summary, these methods collectively illustrate the breadth of approaches that have been proposed to enrich the information content of graph-level representations beyond what simple aggregation can provide.

The challenge of scaling GNN architectures to large graphs and deep configurations introduces a further set of concerns that are distinct from expressiveness. Over-smoothing, the phenomenon whereby repeated graph convolution causes node representations to converge to indistinguishable vectors, is a well-recognized obstacle to building deep GNNs. The Multipath Graph Convolutional Neural Network [arxiv_2105.01510] addresses this by constructing multiple parallel convolutional paths of different depths and combining their outputs, thereby preserving both local and global information without forcing all information to pass through the same sequence of aggregation steps. This multipath design is conceptually related to the multi-scale aggregation in MLAP [arxiv_2103.01488] but operates at the level of the convolutional architecture rather than the pooling step. The Break the Ceiling work [arxiv_1906.02174] provides a complementary theoretical analysis, demonstrating that the expressive power of GCNs is constrained not only by the WL hierarchy but also by the choice of activation functions and architectural depth, and proposing stronger multi-scale architectures that overcome these constraints. The DEMO-Net framework [arxiv_1906.02319] addresses a related but distinct limitation, namely the failure of most GNNs to account for node degree heterogeneity in their convolution operations. Because high-degree and low-degree nodes aggregate information from neighborhoods of very different sizes, applying the same convolutional filter to all nodes regardless of degree introduces a systematic bias. DEMO-Net resolves this by learning degree-specific parameters, enabling the model to adapt its aggregation behavior to the local structural context of each node.

Spectral approaches to graph convolution have also continued to evolve in response to the limitations of early polynomial filter designs. The ARMA filter-based GNN [arxiv_1901.01343, ss_476188] replaces polynomial spectral filters with autoregressive moving average filters, which offer a richer frequency response with fewer parameters and are more robust to noise in the graph structure. The recursive formulation of ARMA filters enables efficient distributed computation while capturing long-range spectral dependencies that polynomial filters of the same order cannot represent. This spectral flexibility is particularly valuable in heterophilic graphs, where the optimal filter must suppress low-frequency components rather than amplifying them as standard Laplacian smoothing does. The Geom-GCN framework [arxiv_2002.05287] addresses the heterophily problem from a spatial rather than spectral perspective, embedding nodes in a continuous geometric space and defining neighborhoods based on geometric proximity rather than direct graph adjacency. This allows the aggregation to include structurally similar but topologically distant nodes, overcoming the locality constraint of standard message passing that makes it difficult to capture long-range dependencies. The geometric embedding also preserves the relative positions of nodes within their neighborhoods, addressing the structural information loss that occurs when standard aggregators treat all neighbors as an unordered set.

The practical deployment of GNN research is substantially facilitated by software frameworks that implement the diverse array of architectural components described in the literature. Spektral [arxiv_2006.12138] provides an open-source library built on TensorFlow and Keras that implements a comprehensive set of message-passing and pooling operators, enabling researchers to rapidly prototype and evaluate new architectures without reimplementing standard components from scratch. The availability of such frameworks has lowered the barrier to entry for GNN research and has contributed to the rapid proliferation of new methods. Alongside software infrastructure, survey and review articles play an important role in synthesizing the rapidly expanding literature. The comprehensive survey by Wu et al. [arxiv_1901.00596] provides an early systematic taxonomy of GNN variants, distinguishing recurrent GNNs, convolutional GNNs, graph autoencoders, and spatial-temporal GNNs, and remains a widely cited reference point for situating new contributions within the broader landscape. More recent surveys [arxiv_2307.00865, arxiv_2205.15555] extend this taxonomy to cover graph-level learning methods and the growing literature on graph classification and link prediction, reflecting the maturation of the field and the increasing specialization of research directions. The survey on graph representations [arxiv_2102.02026] additionally covers embedding methods that predate the GNN era, providing useful historical context for understanding the continuities and discontinuities between classical graph representation learning and modern deep learning approaches.

Edge representation and co-embedding of nodes and edges constitute another dimension of GNN design that has received increasing attention as the limitations of node-centric architectures have become apparent. The co-embedding framework [arxiv_2010.13242, ss_477653] argues that edges carry relational information that cannot be adequately captured by node representations alone, and proposes joint learning of node and edge embeddings through a message-passing scheme that explicitly models edge features and updates them in parallel with node features. This is particularly important in applications such as molecular property prediction, where bond types and bond-level features are as informationally relevant as atom-level features. The dual message passing approach of [arxiv_2112.08764] formalizes this intuition by constructing an edge-to-vertex dual graph and performing message passing simultaneously on the primal and dual graphs, enabling the model to count and match subgraph patterns that require explicit edge modeling. The new layer architectures proposed in [arxiv_1811.00052] similarly extend standard GCN layers to incorporate explicit edge feature transformations, demonstrating that the neglect of edge features in early GNN designs represented a significant and correctable limitation. Collectively, these works on edge-aware architectures complement the node-centric perspective that dominates much of the GNN literature and point toward a more symmetric treatment of the relational structure encoded in graphs.
