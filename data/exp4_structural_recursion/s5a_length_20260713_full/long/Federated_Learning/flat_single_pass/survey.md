# Federated Learning (long)

The landscape of federated learning (FL) has evolved rapidly since its initial conceptualization as a privacy-preserving alternative to centralized machine learning, and the body of literature addressing its foundational principles, technical challenges, and practical implementations has grown correspondingly rich and multifaceted. This subsection surveys the principal themes that have emerged in the flat, non-hierarchical organization of FL research, examining how scholars have approached the core architecture of FL systems, the persistent challenge of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data, communication efficiency, privacy-preserving mechanisms, decentralized aggregation strategies, and the integration of differential privacy with federated optimization. Rather than treating these themes as entirely separate silos, the literature reveals a deeply interconnected set of concerns in which advances in one dimension frequently create new tensions or opportunities in another.

The foundational premise of federated learning is that training data need not be centralized in order to train effective machine learning models. Instead, as articulated in several comprehensive surveys, FL enables multiple participants—whether mobile devices, edge nodes, or organizations—to collaboratively train a shared global model by exchanging only locally computed model updates or gradients, never the raw data itself [arxiv_2104.14362] [arxiv_2301.01299]. This architectural choice is motivated by a confluence of practical and regulatory pressures: data protection laws, institutional policies, and the sheer logistical difficulty of aggregating sensitive data from distributed sources all push toward a paradigm in which computation travels to the data rather than the reverse [arxiv_2010.09258]. The survey by Li and colleagues frames this transition explicitly as a progression from distributed machine learning (DML) to federated learning, noting that while FL inherits many of the algorithmic foundations of DML, it introduces a qualitatively different set of constraints arising from the heterogeneity of participating devices, the non-IID nature of locally held data, and the need to protect privacy even against a potentially curious central server [arxiv_2104.14362]. A complementary perspective offered by another survey emphasizes that FL's defining characteristic is not merely the distribution of computation but the deliberate withholding of raw data, which transforms privacy from an afterthought into a first-class design constraint [arxiv_2010.09258].

The standard FL lifecycle, as described across multiple sources, involves iterative rounds in which a central server distributes a global model to a subset of participating clients, each client performs local training using its private data, and the resulting model updates are transmitted back to the server for aggregation into an updated global model [arxiv_2504.17703] [arxiv_2301.01299]. The most widely adopted aggregation strategy is Federated Averaging (FedAvg), which computes a weighted average of client model parameters. While this protocol is elegant in its simplicity, the literature has consistently identified several categories of challenge that complicate its practical deployment. These challenges cluster around three axes: the statistical heterogeneity of client data distributions, the system heterogeneity arising from differences in computational and communication resources across clients, and the privacy and security vulnerabilities that persist even when raw data is never shared [arxiv_2107.06917] [arxiv_2504.17703].

Statistical heterogeneity, and specifically the non-IID problem, has attracted sustained attention as perhaps the most fundamental obstacle to achieving FL performance comparable to centralized training. When clients hold data drawn from different underlying distributions—as is almost universally the case in real-world deployments—the local model updates computed by individual clients may point in divergent directions in parameter space, causing the aggregated global model to converge slowly, oscillate, or settle at a suboptimal solution [arxiv_2106.06843]. The survey by Zhu and colleagues provides a detailed taxonomy of non-IID scenarios, distinguishing between feature distribution skew, label distribution skew, concept shift, and quantity imbalance, and analyzing how each variant affects convergence behavior differently for parametric and non-parametric models in both horizontal and vertical FL settings [arxiv_2106.06843]. Their analysis makes clear that the non-IID problem is not a single phenomenon but a family of related challenges, each requiring somewhat different mitigation strategies. A comparative assessment of federated and centralized machine learning reinforces this point, demonstrating empirically that the performance gap between FL and centralized training widens as the degree of data heterogeneity increases, and that this gap is not fully explained by any single factor but rather by the interaction between non-IID data distributions, the number of local training steps, and the frequency of global aggregation [arxiv_2202.01529].

Several approaches to mitigating the non-IID problem have been proposed in the literature. One class of methods modifies the local training objective to penalize excessive deviation from the global model, thereby reducing client drift. Another class addresses data heterogeneity through data augmentation or sharing strategies, though these approaches must be carefully designed to avoid compromising privacy. A third class employs personalization techniques that allow individual clients to maintain models tailored to their local data distributions rather than forcing convergence to a single global model [arxiv_2106.06843] [arxiv_2301.01299]. The field guide to federated optimization by Kairouz and colleagues provides a rigorous treatment of how these strategies interact with the optimization landscape, arguing that the choice of local solver, the number of local steps, and the aggregation frequency must be jointly tuned to achieve good convergence under data heterogeneity [arxiv_2107.06917]. Their work is notable for its emphasis on the gap between simulation results and real-world performance, cautioning that many proposed solutions to the non-IID problem have been evaluated under idealized conditions that do not reflect the variability and unpredictability of actual federated deployments.

The causal federated learning approach proposed by Tian and colleagues represents a more radical departure from standard FL methodology, arguing that the non-IID problem is fundamentally a symptom of models that learn spurious correlations rather than causal relationships [arxiv_2104.06557]. By incorporating causal inference principles into the local training process, their approach aims to learn representations that generalize across heterogeneous client distributions and are robust to distribution shift. This work also addresses the related problem of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, which becomes particularly acute in FL settings where the test distribution at deployment time may differ substantially from any individual client's training distribution. While the causal approach is theoretically appealing, the authors acknowledge that it introduces additional computational overhead and requires assumptions about the causal structure of the data that may not always be verifiable in practice [arxiv_2104.06557].

Communication efficiency is a second major axis of FL research, driven by the observation that the iterative exchange of model updates between clients and server can impose substantial bandwidth costs, particularly when models are large and the number of participating clients is high [arxiv_2208.01200]. The survey by Shi and colleagues provides a comprehensive taxonomy of communication challenges in FL, organizing them around three dimensions: communication efficiency (how to reduce the volume of data transmitted per round), communication environment (how to handle unreliable or heterogeneous network conditions), and communication resource allocation (how to schedule transmissions and allocate bandwidth across clients) [arxiv_2208.01200]. Their analysis highlights that these three dimensions are not independent: strategies that improve communication efficiency by compressing model updates may interact adversely with the noise tolerance of the underlying network, while resource allocation policies that prioritize clients with better connectivity may exacerbate the non-IID problem by systematically excluding clients with slower connections.

Model compression techniques, including quantization, sparsification, and low-rank approximation, have been widely studied as means of reducing per-round communication costs [arxiv_2208.01200] [arxiv_2101.12240]. Quantization reduces the precision of transmitted model parameters, while sparsification transmits only the most significant gradient components. Both approaches introduce a trade-off between communication savings and model accuracy, and the literature has explored various adaptive schemes that adjust the degree of compression based on the current state of training or the available communication budget. The interaction between communication compression and differential privacy is particularly subtle: adding noise to model updates for privacy purposes can interact with quantization in complex ways, sometimes amplifying the privacy cost or degrading model utility beyond what either technique would cause in isolation [arxiv_2101.12240].

Partial device participation—selecting only a subset of available clients for each training round—is another widely used strategy for managing communication costs. While this reduces the per-round communication burden, it introduces additional variance into the training process, particularly under non-IID data distributions where the selected subset may not be representative of the full client population [arxiv_2101.12240]. Periodic aggregation, in which clients perform multiple local gradient steps between successive rounds of global aggregation, similarly reduces communication frequency at the cost of increased client drift. The interplay among these three communication-reduction techniques—compression, partial participation, and periodic aggregation—and their joint effect on convergence under data heterogeneity and privacy constraints is a recurring theme in the literature, with different works reaching somewhat different conclusions depending on their modeling assumptions and experimental settings [arxiv_2101.12240] [arxiv_2304.05127].

The straggler problem, arising from the heterogeneity of client computational resources and communication rates, poses a related challenge for FL system design. In synchronous FL protocols, the server must wait for all selected clients to complete their local training and transmit their updates before proceeding to the next round, meaning that the overall training speed is limited by the slowest participant. The stochastic coded federated learning (SCFL) framework proposed by Shen and colleagues addresses this problem by having clients generate privacy-preserving coded datasets through random linear combinations of their local data with additive noise, which are then uploaded to the server to compensate for the effects of stragglers [arxiv_2201.10092]. This approach is notable for simultaneously addressing the straggler problem and providing a privacy guarantee through the noise added during coding, though the authors acknowledge that the privacy guarantee is weaker than that provided by formal differential privacy mechanisms.

Privacy preservation is arguably the most intensively studied dimension of FL research, reflecting both the centrality of privacy to FL's original motivation and the growing sophistication of attacks that can extract sensitive information from model updates. The fundamental insight motivating FL—that sharing model updates rather than raw data protects privacy—has been repeatedly challenged by demonstrations that gradient inversion attacks, membership inference attacks, and property inference attacks can recover substantial information about training data from the model updates exchanged during FL training [arxiv_2203.17005] [arxiv_2405.08299]. This has driven a large body of work on privacy-preserving aggregation (PPAgg) protocols that provide formal privacy guarantees beyond the informal protection offered by the basic FL architecture.

The survey on privacy-preserving aggregation by Zhao and colleagues provides a comprehensive review of the PPAgg landscape, organizing existing protocols around two main families: cryptographic approaches, including secure multi-party computation (MPC), homomorphic encryption (HE), and secret sharing, and statistical approaches based on differential privacy (DP) [arxiv_2203.17005]. Cryptographic approaches provide strong, information-theoretic privacy guarantees but typically impose significant computational and communication overhead. Differential privacy provides a mathematically rigorous privacy guarantee by adding carefully calibrated noise to model updates, but introduces a fundamental trade-off between privacy and model utility: stronger privacy guarantees require more noise, which degrades model accuracy [arxiv_2203.17005] [arxiv_2405.08299].

Differential privacy has emerged as the dominant formal privacy framework in FL research, and the literature on differentially private federated learning (DPFL) is now extensive. The systematic review by Hu and colleagues provides a thorough taxonomy of DPFL approaches, distinguishing between central DP (where noise is added by the server after aggregation), local DP (where noise is added by each client before transmission), and shuffled DP (where a trusted shuffler anonymizes client contributions before they reach the server) [arxiv_2405.08299]. Each variant offers a different privacy-utility trade-off: central DP achieves the best utility for a given privacy budget because the noise is added once to the aggregate rather than to each individual update, but it requires trusting the server not to inspect individual updates before aggregation. Local DP eliminates the need to trust the server but requires each client to add substantially more noise to achieve the same privacy guarantee, resulting in significantly worse model utility [arxiv_2405.08299]. The shuffled model occupies an intermediate position, achieving better utility than pure local DP while requiring less trust than central DP, but its practical deployment requires a trusted or cryptographically secure shuffling mechanism.

The holistic review by Yin and colleagues on the integration of FL and DP, framed under the evocative title "Belt and Braces," provides a complementary perspective on this landscape, emphasizing the practical challenges that arise when DP mechanisms are deployed in real FL systems [arxiv_2404.18814]. Their analysis highlights that the choice of privacy accounting method—which determines how the privacy budget is tracked and allocated across training rounds—has a substantial impact on the achievable privacy-utility trade-off. Rényi differential privacy (RDP) and zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP) provide tighter privacy accounting than the classical (ε, δ)-DP framework, enabling higher-utility models for the same nominal privacy budget [arxiv_2404.18814]. The work also discusses the challenge of composition, noting that the privacy cost accumulates across training rounds and that the total privacy budget must be carefully managed to avoid exhausting it before training converges.

The concentrated differentially private federated learning approach developed by Balle and colleagues addresses the privacy-utility trade-off through the lens of zero-concentrated differential privacy, arguing that this framework provides a more accurate characterization of the privacy cost of Gaussian noise mechanisms than the classical DP framework [arxiv_2003.13761]. Their approach achieves privacy guarantees without the severe utility degradation that often accompanies DP mechanisms in FL, by carefully calibrating the noise level to the sensitivity of the model updates and exploiting the amplification-by-subsampling effect that arises when only a subset of clients participates in each round. The authors demonstrate that their approach achieves competitive model utility while providing formal privacy guarantees, and they provide theoretical convergence analysis showing that the privacy mechanism does not fundamentally alter the convergence behavior of the underlying FL algorithm [arxiv_2003.13761].

The interaction between differential privacy and communication efficiency is a recurring theme in the DPFL literature. The work by Agarwal and colleagues on DP under communication constraints examines how the three standard communication-reduction techniques—compression, partial participation, and periodic aggregation—interact with DP mechanisms [arxiv_2101.12240]. Their analysis reveals that these interactions are complex and sometimes counterintuitive: for example, gradient compression can actually improve the privacy-utility trade-off in some settings by reducing the sensitivity of the transmitted updates, while in other settings it can amplify the effective noise level. The paper proposes a unified framework for jointly optimizing communication efficiency and privacy, demonstrating that significant improvements over naive combinations of existing techniques are achievable [arxiv_2101.12240].

The question of how many local optimization steps clients should perform between successive rounds of global aggregation is central to the efficiency of FL, and it has important implications for privacy as well. The work by Koskela and colleagues on secure aggregation with multiple local steps addresses a subtle but important issue: when clients perform multiple local gradient steps, the privacy accounting for DP mechanisms becomes more complex, because the noise added to the final model update must account for the sensitivity of the entire local training trajectory, not just a single gradient step [arxiv_2407.19286]. Their analysis shows that a simple new accounting approach allows clients to perform multiple local steps while still benefiting from the privacy amplification provided by secure aggregation, enabling higher-utility models without sacrificing formal privacy guarantees. This work illustrates the broader principle that advances in privacy accounting can unlock practical improvements in FL system design that would otherwise appear to require a fundamental privacy-utility trade-off [arxiv_2407.19286].

The balance between the number of local steps and communication rounds is also examined from a convergence perspective by Girgis and colleagues, who provide a formal proof of the optimal number of local steps and communication rounds that maximizes convergence performance within a given privacy budget for the DP version of the SCAFFOLD algorithm [arxiv_2304.05127]. Their analysis demonstrates that there exists a non-trivial optimal operating point that depends on the privacy budget, the degree of data heterogeneity, and the number of participating clients, and that naive choices of these hyperparameters can result in substantially suboptimal performance. This work contributes to a growing body of literature that treats FL system design as a joint optimization problem over multiple interacting dimensions rather than a collection of independent design choices [arxiv_2304.05127].

Local differential privacy (LDP) has attracted particular attention as a means of providing privacy guarantees without requiring any trust in the central server. Under LDP, each client adds noise to its model update before transmission, ensuring that the server cannot infer sensitive information about any individual client's data even if it behaves maliciously. The momentum gradient descent federated learning approach proposed by Sun and colleagues integrates LDP with momentum-based gradient descent to improve convergence under the high noise levels required by LDP [arxiv_2209.14086]. Their approach exploits the variance-reduction properties of momentum to partially compensate for the noise introduced by LDP, achieving better convergence than standard LDP-FL approaches while maintaining the same formal privacy guarantee. The authors also demonstrate that their approach reduces communication costs by reducing the number of rounds required to reach a given accuracy level [arxiv_2209.14086].

The CorBin-FL mechanism proposed by Shlezinger and colleagues takes a different approach to achieving LDP, using correlated binary stochastic quantization to simultaneously achieve differential privacy and communication efficiency [arxiv_2409.13133]. The key insight is that by using secure multi-party computation to enable clients to perform correlated quantization of their local model updates, the mechanism can achieve parameter-level local differential privacy (PLDP) while also reducing the communication cost of transmitting quantized updates. The authors provide theoretical analysis showing that CorBin-FL asymptotically optimizes the privacy-utility trade-off between mean squared error and privacy level, and they demonstrate empirically that it achieves competitive performance with significantly lower communication overhead than alternative approaches [arxiv_2409.13133].

Cryptographic approaches to privacy-preserving aggregation provide an alternative to DP-based methods that avoids the utility degradation associated with noise addition. Secure aggregation protocols based on secret sharing allow the server to compute the aggregate of client model updates without learning any individual update, providing information-theoretic privacy guarantees against a curious server [arxiv_2203.17005] [arxiv_2201.07730]. The SCOTCH framework proposed by Choi and colleagues implements a decentralized multi-party secure computation protocol for federated aggregation using secret sharing, providing strong privacy guarantees against curious aggregators or colluding data owners with minimal communication overhead compared to alternative cryptographic approaches [arxiv_2201.07730]. Their protocol is notable for its simplicity and efficiency: by carefully designing the secret sharing scheme to exploit the structure of the aggregation computation, they achieve communication costs that are significantly lower than those of homomorphic encryption-based approaches while providing comparable privacy guarantees.

The TAPFed protocol proposed by Xu and colleagues addresses a limitation of existing secure aggregation schemes, namely their vulnerability to disaggregation attacks in which a malicious aggregator can recover individual client updates by exploiting knowledge of the aggregation protocol [arxiv_2501.05053]. TAPFed uses a threshold functional encryption scheme to enable privacy-preserving federated learning in the presence of multiple decentralized aggregators, some of which may be malicious. The threshold property ensures that privacy is maintained as long as the number of malicious aggregators does not exceed a specified threshold, providing robustness against a realistic adversary model in which some but not all aggregators are compromised [arxiv_2501.05053]. This work represents an important step toward practical privacy-preserving FL in settings where the aggregation infrastructure cannot be fully trusted, which is increasingly relevant as FL is deployed in multi-party settings involving competing organizations.

The privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol developed by Hegedűs and colleagues takes yet another approach, formulating the distributed aggregation problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) and controlling the communication pattern among participants to minimize privacy leakage [arxiv_2012.07183]. Unlike DP-based approaches that add noise to model updates, this protocol achieves privacy by limiting the information that any individual participant can observe about others' updates, exploiting the structure of the ADMM algorithm to ensure that the aggregation can be completed without any participant learning more than the final aggregate. The authors establish formal privacy guarantees against an honest-but-curious adversary and propose an efficient algorithm for constructing the communication pattern, drawing on techniques from combinatorics and graph theory [arxiv_2012.07183].

Decentralized federated learning, in which the central server is eliminated and clients communicate directly with each other in a peer-to-peer fashion, has emerged as an important research direction motivated by concerns about the single point of failure and the concentration of power in centralized FL architectures [arxiv_2306.02570] [arxiv_2210.00325]. The survey by Sun and colleagues on decentralized optimization in FL provides a systematic analysis of the challenges and opportunities that arise when the centralized communication topology is replaced by a decentralized graph [arxiv_2306.02570]. They identify three main dimensions along which decentralized FL differs from its centralized counterpart: the model dimension (how to aggregate models across a graph rather than at a single server), the data dimension (how data heterogeneity interacts with the graph topology), and the communication dimension (how to manage the increased complexity of peer-to-peer communication). Their analysis reveals that decentralized FL introduces new challenges related to the convergence behavior of consensus-based algorithms under data heterogeneity, but also offers new opportunities for privacy protection by eliminating the need to trust a central aggregator [arxiv_2306.02570].

The privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithm proposed by Yan and colleagues addresses the specific challenge of achieving decentralized global model aggregation in environments where the communication graph between learners varies over time [arxiv_2210.00325]. Their approach uses the Metropolis-Hastings method to update the weighted adjacency matrix based on the current communication topology, and integrates Shamir's secret sharing scheme to provide privacy guarantees during the consensus process. The algorithm is notable for its ability to handle high mobility environments in which the network topology changes between successive rounds of aggregation, a scenario that is particularly relevant for FL deployments involving mobile devices or vehicular networks [arxiv_2210.00325]. The authors establish both correctness and privacy properties of the proposed algorithm, demonstrating that it achieves the same global model as a centralized aggregation protocol while providing information-theoretic privacy guarantees against honest-but-curious participants.

The use of decentralized aggregation in combination with differential privacy is explored by Pasquini and colleagues, who deploy an experimental environment for FL with DP using benchmark datasets to investigate the privacy-utility trade-off in peer-to-peer settings [arxiv_2311.16008]. Their results demonstrate that the choice of DP parameters—including the noise scale, the clipping threshold, and the privacy accounting method—has a substantial impact on the achievable utility, and that naive parameter choices can result in models that are either insufficiently private or too noisy to be useful. The authors also find that the interaction between decentralized aggregation and DP is complex: in some settings, the decentralized topology provides additional privacy amplification that allows the noise level to be reduced without sacrificing the formal privacy guarantee, while in other settings it introduces new privacy vulnerabilities that must be carefully managed [arxiv_2311.16008].

The integration of FL with split learning represents another architectural variant that has attracted research attention. The SplitFed framework proposed by Thapa and colleagues combines federated learning and split learning by partitioning the model architecture between clients and a server, with each client training only the portion of the model up to a designated split layer [arxiv_2004.12088]. This approach provides stronger model privacy than standard FL because the server never has access to the full model, and it is better suited to resource-constrained environments because clients need only train a portion of the model. The authors also incorporate differential privacy and PixelDP into the SplitFed framework to provide additional data privacy and model robustness guarantees, demonstrating that the combined approach achieves competitive performance while providing multiple layers of privacy protection [arxiv_2004.12088].

The application of FL to non-standard machine learning models, beyond the neural networks that dominate the FL literature, has also been explored. The FedXGBoost framework proposed by Yamamoto and colleagues adapts the XGBoost gradient boosting algorithm to the federated setting, addressing the challenge that the standard XGBoost training procedure requires access to the full dataset to compute optimal split points [arxiv_2106.10662]. Two variants are proposed: FedXGBoost-SMM, which uses secure matrix multiplication to preserve privacy with lossless accuracy, and FedXGBoost-LDP, which uses local differential privacy for a more lightweight but approximate privacy guarantee. The authors demonstrate that their approach achieves competitive performance with centralized XGBoost while providing formal privacy guarantees, and they argue that the availability of privacy-preserving federated versions of tree-based models is important for applications in which neural networks are not the preferred model class [arxiv_2106.10662].

The chaotic maps-based approach to privacy-preserving federated learning proposed by Fereidooni and colleagues introduces an alternative to differential privacy for protecting the privacy of model updates, using chaotic encryption to obscure the updates transmitted by each client [arxiv_2402.10145]. The authors compare this approach to standard differential privacy on both IID and non-IID datasets, finding that chaotic encryption provides stronger privacy guarantees in some settings while maintaining better model utility than DP-based approaches. The work also addresses the non-IID challenge through a data augmentation strategy that improves the representativeness of each client's local dataset, demonstrating that the combined approach achieves good performance even under severe data heterogeneity [arxiv_2402.10145].

The development of software tools and simulation frameworks for FL has been an important enabler of research progress, allowing researchers to evaluate proposed algorithms under controlled conditions before deploying them in real-world systems. The PrivacyFL simulator introduced by Mugunthan and colleagues provides an extensible, configurable, and scalable simulation environment for privacy-preserving and secure federated learning, supporting a range of privacy mechanisms including differential privacy, secure aggregation, and homomorphic encryption [arxiv_2002.08423]. The simulator is designed to help practitioners assess the feasibility of FL collaboration and verify that it improves model accuracy before committing to a real-world deployment, addressing the practical challenge that setting up a federated learning environment with security and privacy guarantees is a time-consuming process with numerous configuration choices [arxiv_2002.08423].

The Sherpa.ai federated learning framework described by Rodríguez-Barroso and colleagues takes a complementary approach, providing not only a software implementation of FL with differential privacy but also a methodological workflow that guides practitioners through the process of selecting and configuring DP mechanisms for their specific use case [arxiv_2007.00914]. The framework is built on the observation that the matching of FL and DP to the challenges of data privacy protection has driven the release of numerous software tools, but that these tools lack a unified vision and a principled methodology for their use. By providing both the software infrastructure and the methodological guidance, the Sherpa.ai framework aims to lower the barrier to entry for practitioners who wish to deploy privacy-preserving FL in real-world applications [arxiv_2007.00914].

The performance limitations of FL systems have been systematically analyzed in several recent works, which provide a useful synthesis of the challenges identified across the broader literature. The study by Agrawal and colleagues on performance limitations in FL identifies the communication bottleneck and data non-IID-ness as the two most significant obstacles to achieving high-performance FL in practice, and provides a detailed empirical analysis of how these factors interact to affect model accuracy and training speed [arxiv_2501.03477]. Their analysis confirms the findings of earlier theoretical work, demonstrating that the performance gap between FL and centralized training is primarily driven by the combination of data heterogeneity and infrequent global aggregation, and that this gap can be substantially reduced by increasing the frequency of aggregation at the cost of higher communication overhead [arxiv_2501.03477].

The systematic review of machine learning models for FL by Nguyen and colleagues provides a broader perspective on the landscape of FL algorithms, examining the performance and limitations of a wide range of supervised and unsupervised learning approaches in the federated setting [arxiv_2311.10832]. Their review, conducted according to PRISMA guidelines, covers not only the standard neural network-based FL approaches but also federated versions of support vector machines, decision trees, and other classical machine learning models. The authors find that while neural networks dominate the FL literature, there is growing interest in federated versions of classical models for applications where interpretability, computational efficiency, or robustness to data heterogeneity are important considerations [arxiv_2311.10832].

The recent comprehensive survey by Banabilah and colleagues synthesizes the state of FL research across multiple dimensions, providing a structured overview of the FL lifecycle, the key technical challenges, and the principal application domains [arxiv_2504.17703]. Their survey is notable for its emphasis on the practical deployment challenges that arise when FL is applied to real-world problems in healthcare, finance, and IoT, domains in which the privacy and security requirements are particularly stringent and the data heterogeneity is particularly severe. The authors argue that addressing these challenges will require not only algorithmic advances but also progress in system design, standardization, and governance, reflecting the growing recognition that FL is as much a sociotechnical challenge as a technical one [arxiv_2504.17703].

Taken together, the literature on flat federated learning reveals a field that has matured considerably from its origins as a privacy-preserving alternative to centralized machine learning, but that continues to grapple with fundamental tensions between privacy, utility, communication efficiency, and robustness to data heterogeneity. The non-IID problem remains a central challenge, with no single solution having emerged as universally effective across all settings. The privacy-utility trade-off inherent in differential privacy mechanisms continues to motivate research into tighter privacy accounting methods, more efficient noise mechanisms, and hybrid approaches that combine DP with cryptographic techniques. The communication bottleneck drives ongoing work on compression, quantization, and decentralized communication topologies. And the single-point-of-failure vulnerability of centralized FL architectures motivates the growing body of work on decentralized and peer-to-peer FL protocols.

What is perhaps most striking about this literature is the degree to which advances in one dimension create new challenges and opportunities in others. Improving communication efficiency by performing more local gradient steps exacerbates client drift under non-IID data and complicates privacy accounting for DP mechanisms. Strengthening privacy guarantees by adding more noise degrades model utility and slows convergence. Eliminating the central server through decentralization improves robustness and reduces the need to trust a single aggregator, but introduces new challenges for consensus and privacy in peer-to-peer settings. These interdependencies mean that progress in FL requires not only specialized expertise in individual subfields but also a systems-level perspective that can navigate the complex trade-offs among competing objectives. The field guide to federated optimization by Kairouz and colleagues captures this systems perspective particularly well, arguing that effective FL research requires careful attention to the gap between theoretical analysis and practical performance, and that the design of FL algorithms must be informed by realistic models of the deployment environment [arxiv_2107.06917]. This call for greater realism and rigor in FL research is echoed across the literature and reflects the field's growing maturity as it moves from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward real-world deployment at scale.

The intersection of federated learning with emerging computational paradigms represents one of the most active frontiers in the field. The proposal of hybrid classical-quantum federated learning frameworks, such as FEDCOMPASS, illustrates how researchers are beginning to explore whether quantum computing can alleviate some of the computational and communication burdens that have long constrained FL deployments [arxiv_2602.03052]. FEDCOMPASS employs spectral clustering and periodic aggregation strategies to mitigate the performance degradation that hybrid quantum-classical models suffer under non-IID data distributions, a challenge that mirrors the difficulties encountered in purely classical FL settings. The fact that non-IID data heterogeneity emerges as a central obstacle even in quantum-enhanced FL architectures underscores the fundamental nature of this challenge and suggests that algorithmic solutions developed for classical FL may need to be substantially rethought before they can be effectively transferred to quantum settings [arxiv_2602.03052]. While the practical deployment of quantum FL remains a distant prospect for most applications, the theoretical exploration of these hybrid architectures is valuable insofar as it exposes new dimensions of the privacy-utility-communication trade-off that may not be apparent from purely classical analyses.

The security and privacy threat landscape of FL has been comprehensively catalogued in several recent surveys that go beyond the privacy-preserving mechanisms discussed above to examine the full spectrum of adversarial attacks to which FL systems are vulnerable. The survey by Lyu and colleagues on security and privacy in FL covers more than two hundred papers and provides a systematic taxonomy of attacks, including gradient inversion attacks, model poisoning attacks, backdoor attacks, and inference attacks, alongside the corresponding defense mechanisms [arxiv_2508.13730]. A particularly important insight from this survey is that the defenses developed to address one class of attacks often introduce new vulnerabilities to other classes. For example, differential privacy mechanisms that add noise to gradients can reduce the effectiveness of gradient inversion attacks but may simultaneously weaken the server's ability to detect and filter malicious model updates submitted by compromised clients. This adversarial interplay between attack and defense mechanisms means that the security analysis of FL systems must be conducted holistically rather than in isolation, and that the deployment of any single defense mechanism should be accompanied by a careful assessment of its impact on the system's overall security posture [arxiv_2508.13730]. The survey also highlights the growing importance of Byzantine-robust aggregation rules, which are designed to tolerate a fraction of malicious clients without compromising the integrity of the global model, and notes that the theoretical guarantees of these rules are often difficult to maintain in the presence of non-IID data distributions.

The challenge of balancing privacy and performance in FL with differential privacy has been approached from several complementary angles in the recent literature. The work of Girgis and colleagues on balancing privacy and performance for private FL algorithms provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of the trade-off between the privacy budget consumed per communication round and the convergence rate of the global model [arxiv_2304.05127]. Their analysis reveals that the optimal allocation of the privacy budget across communication rounds depends critically on the degree of data heterogeneity among clients, with more heterogeneous settings requiring a more conservative noise schedule to prevent the amplification of client drift by the added noise. This finding has important practical implications, as it suggests that the common practice of using a fixed noise level throughout training may be suboptimal and that adaptive noise scheduling strategies could yield significant improvements in the privacy-utility trade-off [arxiv_2304.05127]. Complementing this theoretical perspective, the empirical benchmark study by Hilprecht and colleagues on FL with differential privacy examines how the number of participating clients and the choice of DP mechanism interact to affect model performance across different data modalities [arxiv_2402.02230]. Their results demonstrate that increasing the number of clients generally improves the privacy-utility trade-off by enabling the use of stronger privacy amplification by subsampling, but that this benefit is contingent on the clients having sufficiently similar data distributions, a condition that is rarely satisfied in practice.

The utility-enhanced approach to FL with differential privacy proposed by Zhang and colleagues addresses the fundamental tension between privacy and utility by developing a novel optimization framework that incorporates the DP noise directly into the local optimization objective rather than adding it as a post-processing step [arxiv_2503.21154]. By treating the noise as an integral part of the optimization process, their approach is able to achieve tighter convergence guarantees than methods that add noise after the local gradient computation, while maintaining the same formal privacy guarantees. The authors demonstrate that their approach yields substantial improvements in model accuracy relative to standard DP-FL baselines across a range of benchmark tasks, particularly in the high-privacy regime where the noise level is large relative to the signal in the gradients [arxiv_2503.21154]. This work is representative of a broader trend in the DP-FL literature toward more principled integration of privacy mechanisms into the optimization process, moving away from the ad hoc noise addition strategies that characterized earlier work.

The systematic review of differentially private FL by Hu and colleagues provides a comprehensive synthesis of the algorithmic landscape, categorizing existing approaches according to the granularity of privacy protection they provide, the type of DP mechanism they employ, and the communication topology they assume [arxiv_2405.08299]. Their review identifies several important gaps in the existing literature, including the relative neglect of local differential privacy in the FL setting, the limited attention paid to the composition of privacy losses across multiple rounds of training, and the lack of standardized evaluation protocols that would enable meaningful comparisons across different DP-FL algorithms. The authors argue that addressing these gaps will require the development of new theoretical tools for privacy accounting in the federated setting, as well as the establishment of community-wide benchmarks that can serve as reference points for future algorithmic development [arxiv_2405.08299]. The review also notes the growing importance of personalized FL approaches, in which different clients are allowed to have different privacy budgets, reflecting the recognition that the privacy requirements of individual clients may vary considerably depending on the sensitivity of their data and their risk tolerance.

The combination of differential privacy with secure aggregation has emerged as a particularly promising approach to achieving strong privacy guarantees in FL without sacrificing model utility. The work of Stevens and colleagues on using secure aggregation in differentially private FL with multiple local steps provides a careful analysis of the interaction between these two privacy-enhancing technologies, demonstrating that their combination can achieve sample-level privacy protection with significantly lower noise levels than DP alone [arxiv_2407.19286]. The key insight is that secure aggregation prevents the server from observing individual client updates, which means that the DP noise only needs to mask the contribution of individual samples within a client's local dataset rather than the contribution of the entire client. This finer granularity of protection allows for a more favorable privacy-utility trade-off, but introduces new challenges for the design of the secure aggregation protocol, which must be compatible with the multiple local gradient steps performed by each client between communication rounds [arxiv_2407.19286]. The TAPFed framework of Xu and colleagues addresses related challenges by proposing a threshold-based secure aggregation protocol that remains robust even when a subset of the participating clients or servers is compromised, providing a more realistic security model than protocols that assume all parties are either fully honest or fully malicious [arxiv_2501.05053].

The CorBin-FL mechanism introduced by Shlezinger and colleagues represents another innovative approach to the joint optimization of privacy and communication efficiency in FL [arxiv_2409.13133]. By using correlated binary stochastic quantization to simultaneously achieve differential privacy and gradient compression, CorBin-FL eliminates the need to add separate noise for privacy and separate quantization for compression, achieving both objectives through a single unified mechanism. The authors demonstrate that the correlation structure of the binary quantization noise can be exploited to achieve tighter privacy guarantees than independent noise addition, while the binary representation of the quantized gradients reduces the communication overhead relative to full-precision gradient transmission [arxiv_2409.13133]. This work exemplifies the growing recognition that the various efficiency and privacy challenges of FL are not independent problems to be solved separately but are deeply interrelated, and that the most effective solutions will be those that address multiple challenges simultaneously through principled joint design.

The two-bit aggregation approach of Sery and colleagues similarly targets the joint optimization of communication efficiency and differential privacy, demonstrating that it is possible to achieve meaningful privacy guarantees with only two bits of precision per gradient coordinate without incurring a significant penalty in model accuracy [arxiv_2110.03017]. Their analysis provides tight bounds on the privacy-utility-communication trade-off achievable with low-precision gradient representations, and demonstrates that the optimal precision level depends on the privacy budget, the number of clients, and the dimensionality of the model. The communication-computation efficient secure aggregation protocol of Park and colleagues addresses the complementary challenge of reducing the overhead of the cryptographic operations required for secure aggregation, proposing a novel protocol that achieves significant reductions in both communication and computation costs relative to existing approaches while maintaining strong security guarantees [arxiv_2012.05433]. Together, these works illustrate the rich interplay between quantization, compression, and cryptographic techniques in the design of efficient and private FL systems.

The momentum gradient descent approach to FL with local differential privacy proposed by Xu and colleagues addresses the challenge of achieving strong privacy guarantees in the local DP setting, where each client must protect its data even from the central server [arxiv_2209.14086]. Local DP is a strictly stronger privacy model than central DP, as it does not require trust in the aggregating server, but it typically requires significantly more noise to achieve the same formal privacy guarantee, leading to a more severe degradation in model utility. The authors demonstrate that incorporating momentum into the local gradient descent process can partially compensate for the increased noise level by smoothing the gradient trajectory and reducing the variance of the noisy gradient estimates. Their empirical results show that the momentum-based approach achieves substantially better convergence than standard local DP-FL baselines, particularly in the early stages of training when the gradient signal is strongest and the noise has the greatest relative impact [arxiv_2209.14086]. This work highlights the importance of algorithm design choices that go beyond the noise mechanism itself in determining the practical utility of local DP-FL systems.

The stochastic coded federated learning framework of Prakash and colleagues addresses the challenge of stragglers in FL, which arises when some clients are significantly slower than others due to heterogeneous computational resources or fluctuating communication rates [arxiv_2201.10092]. Stragglers are a significant practical obstacle to FL deployment, as waiting for the slowest clients to complete their local computations can dramatically increase the wall-clock time per communication round. The coded FL approach addresses this challenge by introducing redundancy into the local computations, allowing the server to reconstruct the global model update from the responses of a subset of the fastest clients without waiting for the stragglers. The authors demonstrate that their approach achieves convergence guarantees comparable to those of standard FL while significantly reducing the expected training time, and that it can be combined with differential privacy mechanisms without incurring additional privacy costs [arxiv_2201.10092]. The integration of coding-theoretic techniques with FL represents a promising direction for addressing the system heterogeneity challenges that are ubiquitous in real-world FL deployments.

The FedPDC framework of Liu and colleagues addresses the non-IID challenge from a different angle, proposing a method for correcting the biased data distribution on individual clients by leveraging a small public dataset available at the server [arxiv_2302.12503]. The key insight is that even a small amount of publicly available data can provide valuable information about the global data distribution that can be used to correct the bias introduced by the non-IID local distributions. The authors demonstrate that their approach achieves significant improvements in model accuracy relative to standard FL baselines on benchmark non-IID tasks, and that the improvements are robust to variations in the size and composition of the public dataset [arxiv_2302.12503]. This work is representative of a broader trend in the non-IID FL literature toward hybrid approaches that combine the privacy benefits of FL with the accuracy benefits of centralized training by leveraging small amounts of publicly available data.

The causal federated learning approach of Vo and colleagues takes a fundamentally different perspective on the non-IID challenge, arguing that the performance degradation of FL under data heterogeneity is a symptom of a deeper problem: the failure of standard FL algorithms to distinguish between spurious correlations and genuine causal relationships in the training data [arxiv_2104.06557]. By incorporating causal inference techniques into the FL training process, their approach aims to learn models that are robust to distribution shifts across clients, not by averaging out the heterogeneity but by identifying the invariant causal mechanisms that underlie the data generating process across all clients. The authors demonstrate that their causal FL approach achieves better generalization to new clients with unseen data distributions than standard FL baselines, suggesting that causal reasoning may provide a principled foundation for addressing the non-IID challenge that goes beyond the heuristic regularization and aggregation strategies that dominate the current literature [arxiv_2104.06557].

The chaotic maps-based privacy-preserving FL approach of Abuadbba and colleagues represents yet another direction in the search for effective privacy mechanisms for FL, proposing the use of chaotic encryption as an alternative to differential privacy for protecting the privacy of local model updates [arxiv_2402.10145]. The authors argue that chaotic encryption can provide stronger privacy guarantees than DP in certain settings, particularly when the data is non-IID and the noise required for DP would be prohibitively large. Their empirical comparison of chaotic encryption and DP across a range of non-IID benchmarks demonstrates that chaotic encryption can achieve comparable or better model accuracy than DP while providing stronger empirical privacy protection, though the authors acknowledge that chaotic encryption lacks the formal mathematical guarantees that make DP attractive for applications with strict regulatory requirements [arxiv_2402.10145]. This work highlights the ongoing tension between the formal guarantees of DP and the practical performance of alternative privacy mechanisms, and suggests that the choice of privacy mechanism should be informed by the specific requirements of the application domain.

The SCOTCH framework of Choi and colleagues addresses the challenge of secure aggregation in FL from a cryptographic perspective, proposing an efficient secure computation framework that reduces the communication overhead of secure aggregation by exploiting the algebraic structure of the aggregation operation [arxiv_2201.07730]. Their approach achieves significant reductions in communication cost relative to existing secure aggregation protocols while maintaining strong security guarantees against a malicious aggregation server. The e-SeaFL protocol of Dong and colleagues similarly targets the efficiency of secure aggregation, proposing a verifiable secure aggregation protocol that requires only a single communication round during the aggregation phase, significantly reducing the latency of the aggregation process relative to multi-round protocols [arxiv_2304.03841]. The efficiency gains achieved by these protocols are particularly important for FL deployments in resource-constrained environments, such as mobile and IoT settings, where the communication and computation overhead of cryptographic operations can be a significant bottleneck.

The comparative assessment of federated and centralized machine learning by Sattler and colleagues provides an important empirical perspective on the practical performance gap between FL and centralized training across a range of benchmark tasks and data distributions [arxiv_2202.01529]. Their results demonstrate that the performance gap is highly variable across tasks and data distributions, ranging from negligible in some settings to substantial in others, and that the gap is most pronounced when the data is highly non-IID and the number of communication rounds is limited. The authors also find that the choice of aggregation algorithm has a significant impact on the performance gap, with more sophisticated aggregation strategies such as FedProx and SCAFFOLD substantially reducing the gap relative to the standard FedAvg baseline. These findings underscore the importance of algorithm selection in practical FL deployments and suggest that the choice of aggregation algorithm should be informed by the specific characteristics of the deployment environment, including the degree of data heterogeneity, the communication budget, and the computational resources available on the clients [arxiv_2202.01529].

The SplitFed framework of Thapa and colleagues, which combines federated learning with split learning, represents an important architectural innovation that addresses the limitations of both approaches [arxiv_2004.12088]. Split learning divides the neural network model between the client and the server, with the client computing the forward pass up to a designated cut layer and sending the intermediate activations to the server for the remainder of the computation. This approach provides stronger model privacy than standard FL, as the client never has access to the full model, but it is slower than FL due to the relay-based training process. SplitFed combines the parallelism of FL with the model privacy of split learning, achieving better performance than either approach alone on resource-constrained devices. The authors demonstrate that SplitFed achieves comparable accuracy to standard FL while providing stronger model privacy guarantees, making it an attractive option for applications where the model architecture itself is considered sensitive intellectual property [arxiv_2004.12088].

The federated XGBoost framework of Yamamoto and colleagues extends the FL paradigm beyond neural networks to the widely used gradient boosting framework, proposing two variants of federated XGBoost that provide privacy guarantees through secure multi-party computation and local differential privacy respectively [arxiv_2106.10662]. The development of federated versions of classical machine learning models such as XGBoost is important for broadening the applicability of FL to domains where tree-based models are preferred over neural networks due to their interpretability, robustness to overfitting, or computational efficiency. The authors demonstrate that their federated XGBoost variants achieve accuracy comparable to centralized XGBoost on benchmark tasks while providing meaningful privacy guarantees, though the communication overhead of the secure multi-party computation variant is significantly higher than that of the local DP variant [arxiv_2106.10662]. This work is representative of the growing body of research on federated versions of non-neural-network machine learning models, which is an important complement to the dominant focus on neural network-based FL in the existing literature.

Synthesizing across the full breadth of the literature reviewed here, several overarching themes emerge that are likely to shape the trajectory of FL research in the coming years. First, the field is moving toward increasingly principled and mathematically rigorous treatments of the privacy-utility-communication trade-off, driven by the recognition that ad hoc approaches to noise addition and gradient compression are insufficient for the stringent requirements of real-world deployments. Second, there is growing interest in hybrid approaches that combine multiple privacy-enhancing technologies, such as differential privacy, secure aggregation, and cryptographic encryption, to achieve stronger privacy guarantees than any single technique can provide alone. Third, the non-IID data challenge continues to motivate a diverse range of algorithmic innovations, from causal inference to public dataset correction to clustered aggregation, reflecting the recognition that no single approach is universally effective across all settings. Fourth, the field is increasingly attentive to the system-level challenges of FL deployment, including straggler mitigation, communication efficiency, and Byzantine robustness, and is developing increasingly sophisticated tools for addressing these challenges in a principled and integrated manner. Finally, the growing body of work on decentralized and peer-to-peer FL architectures reflects the recognition that the centralized FL paradigm, while convenient for analysis and implementation, is not always appropriate for real-world deployments where trust in a central aggregator cannot be assumed. Addressing these challenges in a coherent and integrated manner will require sustained interdisciplinary collaboration across machine learning, cryptography, distributed systems, and the application domains in which FL is deployed, and will likely remain a central preoccupation of the field for years to come [arxiv_2504.17703, arxiv_2508.13730, arxiv_2405.08299].
