# Federated Learning (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm in machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across distributed data sources without requiring the centralization of raw data. This approach addresses a fundamental tension in modern data-driven systems: the need to leverage large, heterogeneous datasets for training powerful models while simultaneously respecting legal constraints, organizational boundaries, and individual privacy rights. The conceptual foundations and architectural principles of federated learning have been extensively documented, and a growing body of survey literature has sought to systematize the field's rapid development across multiple dimensions, including distributed training strategies, communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, and privacy preservation.

The transition from classical distributed machine learning to federated learning represents more than a mere engineering refinement; it reflects a fundamental reconceptualization of where data should reside and how computation should be organized. As [arxiv_2104.14362] observe, data and computing resources are increasingly distributed across end-user devices, geographic regions, and organizational silos, and legal or regulatory frameworks often prohibit the direct sharing of such resources across boundaries. Federated learning emerges in this context as an efficient mechanism for exploiting distributed data and computing resources to collaboratively train machine learning models while obeying applicable laws and ensuring data security and privacy. This framing situates federated learning not merely as a technical solution but as a sociotechnical response to the structural realities of contemporary data governance. The functional architecture proposed in [arxiv_2104.14362] encompasses distributed training protocols, data communication mechanisms, and security considerations, providing a taxonomy that has informed much subsequent research.

Building on this foundational framing, more recent systematic surveys have sought to capture the field's expanding scope. [arxiv_2301.01299] provide a comprehensive account of recent advances in federated learning, emphasizing that while earlier surveys tended to focus on specific perspectives or lacked coverage of the latest developments, the field now demands a multi-faceted treatment that encompasses privacy-preserving methods, communication strategies, aggregation algorithms, and diverse application domains. The survey in [arxiv_2301.01299] is notable for its attention to the collaborative learning dynamic between parties, underscoring that in federated learning only locally trained models or computed gradients are exchanged, rather than raw data, which provides a degree of privacy protection by design. This architectural choice, however, does not eliminate privacy risks entirely, a point that motivates a substantial strand of research discussed below.

One of the most persistently studied challenges in federated learning concerns the statistical heterogeneity of data distributed across participating clients. In standard centralized machine learning, training data is typically assumed to be independently and identically distributed (IID), an assumption that rarely holds when data is generated organically by diverse users or organizations. [arxiv_2106.06843] provide a detailed analysis of the influence of non-IID data on both parametric and non-parametric machine learning models within both horizontal and vertical federated learning settings. Their survey documents that models trained under federated conditions with non-IID data consistently exhibit degraded performance relative to those trained in centralized settings, and they systematically review the range of approaches proposed to mitigate this degradation. These approaches include data-sharing strategies, personalized federated learning methods, and modifications to aggregation algorithms designed to account for distributional divergence among clients. The survey in [arxiv_2106.06843] is particularly valuable for its comparative treatment of the advantages and disadvantages of these approaches, noting that while some methods succeed in improving model accuracy under non-IID conditions, they often do so at the cost of increased communication overhead, reduced privacy guarantees, or assumptions about data distribution that may not hold in practice.

The intersection of non-IID data challenges and privacy preservation is further explored in [arxiv_2402.10145], which proposes a secured federated learning method that simultaneously addresses the non-IID challenge and introduces an additional layer of privacy through chaotic maps-based encryption. The work in [arxiv_2402.10145] is notable for its empirical comparison of differential privacy and chaotic-based encryption as privacy mechanisms, finding that the federated learning process improves average performance metrics of deep neural networks even in non-IID settings. This contribution is significant because it demonstrates that privacy-enhancing mechanisms need not be incompatible with robustness to data heterogeneity, though the specific conditions under which chaotic encryption outperforms differential privacy remain an active area of inquiry. The experimental findings in [arxiv_2402.10145] complement the theoretical analyses in [arxiv_2106.06843] by providing concrete evidence that carefully designed federated systems can maintain utility across both IID and non-IID data regimes.

Communication efficiency represents another central axis of federated learning research, given that the iterative exchange of model updates between clients and a central server can impose substantial bandwidth and latency costs, particularly in settings involving large models or resource-constrained edge devices. [arxiv_2208.01200] offer a contemporary survey of communication challenges in federated learning, organizing their analysis around three perspectives: communication efficiency, communication environment, and communication resource allocation. The survey identifies a range of techniques for reducing communication overhead, including gradient compression, quantization, sparsification, and asynchronous update protocols, and situates these within the broader context of the communication environments in which federated learning is deployed. Importantly, [arxiv_2208.01200] draw an explicit contrast with traditional distributed machine learning, noting that in the latter paradigm, users' private data is transmitted between clients and a central server, resulting in significant privacy risks, whereas federated learning's design of transmitting only model updates rather than raw data represents a deliberate trade-off between privacy and communication cost. This framing aligns with the broader architectural description in [arxiv_2104.14362] and underscores that communication and privacy considerations are deeply intertwined in federated system design.

Privacy preservation in federated learning has attracted substantial research attention, motivated by the recognition that even the exchange of model updates or gradients can leak sensitive information about training data through inference attacks. The dominant approaches to privacy in federated learning include differential privacy, secure multi-party computation, and homomorphic encryption, each offering distinct trade-offs between privacy guarantees, computational cost, and model utility. [arxiv_2003.13761] address the challenge of privacy leakage arising from information exchange between edge devices and a central server, particularly in settings where the server is not fully trusted. Their work develops a federated learning approach based on concentrated differential privacy that aims to provide strong privacy guarantees without substantially degrading model utility or convergence. The authors note that while prior privacy-preserving mechanisms could be applied to federated learning, they typically impose high costs on convergence and utility, and their contribution lies in achieving a more favorable balance between these competing objectives. This work is representative of a broader research program that seeks to make differential privacy practically viable in federated settings, where the cumulative privacy budget across many communication rounds poses a fundamental challenge.

An alternative approach to privacy in federated learning moves away from the centralized server architecture entirely, instead distributing the aggregation process among the participating clients themselves. [arxiv_2012.07183] develop a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that formulates the distributed aggregation problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) and addresses its privacy weaknesses through controlled communication patterns rather than differential privacy or homomorphic encryption. The protocol establishes privacy guarantees against an honest-but-curious adversary by minimizing the information available to any single participant through careful management of the communication graph. This approach contrasts with the server-centric model assumed in much of the federated learning literature, including the frameworks described in [arxiv_2003.13761] and [arxiv_2208.01200], and reflects a growing recognition that the central server itself represents a potential privacy and security vulnerability.

The challenge of achieving privacy-preserving federated learning in fully decentralized, peer-to-peer settings without any coordinator is taken up directly by [arxiv_2210.00325], which proposes the first privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithm for decentralized global model aggregation in environments of high mobility, where the communication graph between learners may vary between successive rounds. The algorithm integrates the Metropolis-Hastings method for updating the weighted adjacency matrix based on current communication topology with Shamir's secret sharing scheme to facilitate privacy in reaching consensus on the global model. The work in [arxiv_2210.00325] extends the decentralized aggregation framework of [arxiv_2012.07183] by explicitly addressing the time-varying nature of communication graphs, a practically important consideration in mobile and dynamic network environments. Together, these two works represent a coherent research direction toward federated learning systems that do not rely on a trusted central aggregator, offering stronger privacy guarantees at the cost of more complex coordination protocols.

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals a field characterized by rapid growth and productive tension among competing objectives. The foundational surveys in [arxiv_2104.14362] and [arxiv_2301.01299] establish the architectural and taxonomic scaffolding within which more specialized contributions can be situated. The challenge of non-IID data, documented systematically in [arxiv_2106.06843] and addressed empirically in [arxiv_2402.10145], remains one of the most practically consequential open problems in federated learning. Communication efficiency, analyzed comprehensively in [arxiv_2208.01200], intersects with privacy considerations in ways that require careful co-design of aggregation protocols and compression strategies. The privacy literature, spanning differential privacy approaches such as [arxiv_2003.13761] and decentralized aggregation protocols such as [arxiv_2012.07183] and [arxiv_2210.00325], reflects a spectrum of trust assumptions and threat models that must be matched to the specific deployment context. The cumulative picture is one of a maturing research field that has moved beyond initial proof-of-concept demonstrations toward a nuanced engagement with the practical constraints and adversarial conditions that characterize real-world federated learning deployments.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Federated learning has emerged as a compelling paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed data sources without requiring the direct exchange of raw data, a property that has made it attractive across a wide range of privacy-sensitive domains [arxiv_2107.06917]. At its core, the framework allows multiple participating clients to collaboratively optimize a shared global model by transmitting only model updates, such as gradients or weights, to a central coordinating server, thereby keeping local datasets resident on the devices that generated them [arxiv_2311.10832]. Despite this structural separation of data from computation, the federated setting introduces a rich and interrelated set of challenges spanning optimization, communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, and, most critically for many deployment contexts, the formal guarantees of privacy that the system can provide [arxiv_2107.06917]. The literature has responded to these challenges along several converging lines of inquiry, and understanding the relationships among these lines is essential for situating recent advances in the field.

The foundational motivation for federated learning rests on the observation that centralizing sensitive data is often legally impermissible, technically infeasible, or socially undesirable [arxiv_2311.10832]. The proliferation of mobile devices and networked endpoints has intensified concerns about the loss of individual control over personal data, and federated learning was in part conceived as an architectural response to these concerns [arxiv_2311.16008]. However, a persistent and important finding in the literature is that the mere act of retaining data locally does not, by itself, constitute a strong privacy guarantee. Model updates transmitted during training can leak information about the underlying training data through a variety of inference attacks, including membership inference attacks that allow an adversary to determine whether a particular data record was used in training [arxiv_2311.16008]. This recognition has driven substantial research into augmenting federated learning with formal privacy mechanisms, most prominently differential privacy, and with cryptographic tools such as secure aggregation.

Differential privacy, when applied in the federated context, provides a mathematically rigorous bound on the amount of information that any single participant's data can contribute to the observable output of the learning process [arxiv_2311.16008]. The combination of differential privacy with federated learning has been extensively studied, and the literature consistently highlights the fundamental tension between privacy and utility: stronger privacy guarantees, achieved through the addition of greater noise to model updates, tend to degrade model accuracy, while weaker noise levels improve utility at the cost of formal privacy protection [arxiv_2311.16008]. The choice of differential privacy parameters, including the privacy budget and the noise mechanism, is therefore central to the practical deployment of privacy-preserving federated learning systems, and empirical investigations have confirmed that these choices have a decisive impact on the resulting trade-off [arxiv_2311.16008]. Systematic reviews of the literature have catalogued the range of machine learning models and training strategies that have been evaluated under federated learning conditions, noting that performance and privacy outcomes vary substantially depending on the model architecture, the dataset, and the specific privacy mechanism employed [arxiv_2311.10832].

A complementary line of work has focused on secure aggregation, a cryptographic technique that allows the server to compute the aggregate of client updates without being able to inspect any individual update. Secure aggregation addresses a distinct threat model from differential privacy: whereas differential privacy limits the information that can be inferred from the aggregate output, secure aggregation prevents the server from accessing individual contributions in the first place [arxiv_2407.19286]. These two techniques are often combined in practice, and their interaction has important implications for the formal privacy guarantees that can be achieved. One of the key insights developed in recent work is that secure aggregation enables a more favorable privacy accounting when clients perform multiple local optimization steps before communicating with the server. Specifically, [arxiv_2407.19286] demonstrates that a new analytical approach allows parties to execute multiple local steps while still fully benefiting from secure aggregation in terms of the resulting differential privacy guarantees, yielding higher-utility models without sacrificing formal privacy properties. This result is significant because multiple local steps are a standard technique for reducing communication frequency in federated optimization, and the ability to combine this efficiency gain with strong privacy accounting represents a meaningful advance over prior methods that required communication at each local step.

The design of secure aggregation protocols has itself been an active area of research, with proposals varying in their cryptographic foundations, communication overhead, and resilience to adversarial behavior. The SCOTCH framework [arxiv_2201.07730] exemplifies one approach, deploying multi-party computation primitives, specifically secret sharing, to construct a decentralized aggregation protocol that provides strict privacy guarantees against curious aggregators or colluding data owners. The authors argue that their protocol achieves these guarantees with minimal communication overhead compared to existing state-of-the-art methods, addressing a practical concern that has limited the adoption of cryptographic privacy techniques in bandwidth-constrained settings. A related but distinct challenge is addressed by TAPFed [arxiv_2501.05053], which targets the specific vulnerability of existing secure aggregation solutions to disaggregation attacks, a class of inference attack that can compromise privacy even when individual updates are nominally protected. TAPFed introduces a threshold functional encryption scheme that accommodates multiple decentralized aggregators, tolerating a certain number of malicious actors while maintaining security guarantees. The contrast between SCOTCH and TAPFed is instructive: while both address the problem of aggregator-side privacy threats, SCOTCH focuses on minimizing communication costs through secret sharing in a semi-honest model, whereas TAPFed explicitly targets the stronger threat model posed by actively malicious aggregators and recently demonstrated inference attacks, reflecting an evolution in the adversarial assumptions that the community considers realistic.

The question of centralization versus decentralization in the aggregation architecture is a recurring theme across the literature and connects the cryptographic and optimization strands of federated learning research. The canonical federated learning setup assumes a central server that coordinates training, but this architecture introduces a single point of failure and concentrates trust in a potentially untrustworthy aggregator [arxiv_2306.02570]. Decentralized federated learning, in which clients communicate directly with one another according to a network topology rather than routing all updates through a central server, has been proposed as a structural remedy for this vulnerability. The survey by [arxiv_2306.02570] systematically examines the challenges and opportunities that arise when decentralized optimization techniques are adapted for the federated setting, organizing the discussion around three axes: the model, the data, and the communication. From the model perspective, decentralized approaches must contend with the fact that different clients may maintain different model states at any given time, complicating convergence analysis. From the data perspective, the statistical heterogeneity that characterizes federated data distributions is exacerbated in decentralized settings because there is no central mechanism for balancing contributions across clients. From the communication perspective, the topology of the client network determines both the efficiency of information propagation and the privacy properties of the system, since updates must traverse multiple hops before reaching all participants.

The use of decentralized aggregation as a privacy-enhancing mechanism has been explored empirically in [arxiv_2311.16008], which deploys a peer-to-peer federated learning environment augmented with differential privacy and evaluates the resulting privacy-utility trade-off on benchmark datasets. The findings reinforce the view that decentralization can reduce the exposure of individual updates to any single aggregating party, but that the formal privacy guarantees depend critically on the specific combination of aggregation topology and differential privacy parameters. This empirical perspective complements the theoretical analysis in [arxiv_2407.19286] and the cryptographic constructions in [arxiv_2201.07730] and [arxiv_2501.05053], collectively suggesting that robust privacy in federated learning requires attention to both the mathematical accounting of information leakage and the architectural choices that determine who can observe what during training.

Beyond the privacy dimension, federated learning research has also grappled with the challenge of accommodating heterogeneous client capabilities. Split learning, in which the model is partitioned between the client and the server so that only intermediate representations rather than raw data or full gradients are transmitted, offers an alternative approach to privacy that is particularly suited to resource-constrained environments [arxiv_2004.12088]. The SplitFed framework [arxiv_2004.12088] combines split learning with federated learning in an attempt to capture the advantages of both: the model privacy benefits of splitting the architecture, the parallelism and communication efficiency of federated aggregation, and the formal privacy guarantees of differential privacy applied to the transmitted representations. Empirical results reported in that work suggest that the hybrid approach can achieve comparable or superior performance to either method alone, while the incorporation of differential privacy and PixelDP further enhances robustness to data reconstruction attacks. This line of work illustrates a broader trend in the literature toward hybrid architectures that draw on multiple privacy-enhancing techniques simultaneously, rather than treating any single mechanism as sufficient.

The optimization perspective on federated learning, articulated comprehensively in [arxiv_2107.06917], provides a unifying framework for understanding many of the design choices that appear across the literature. The authors emphasize that federated optimization problems are distinguished from standard distributed optimization by the combination of communication constraints, statistical heterogeneity across clients, and the need for compatibility with privacy and system requirements. Their recommendations for algorithm design, evaluation methodology, and simulation practice reflect a consensus that has emerged in the field: that progress requires careful attention to the interaction among these constraints rather than optimizing for any single dimension in isolation. This perspective is consistent with the findings reviewed above, in which advances in privacy-preserving federated learning have repeatedly required simultaneous consideration of communication efficiency, aggregation architecture, and formal privacy accounting.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals a field that has matured considerably from its initial formulation, developing a sophisticated understanding of the multiple, interacting dimensions along which federated learning systems must be designed and evaluated. The convergence of cryptographic secure aggregation, differential privacy, decentralized topologies, and hybrid architectures represents the current frontier of the field, with ongoing work seeking to tighten the formal guarantees available under realistic adversarial assumptions while preserving the practical utility that motivates the federated approach in the first place [arxiv_2407.19286][arxiv_2501.05053][arxiv_2306.02570].

### Evidence theme 3

The intersection of federated learning (FL) and privacy preservation has generated a substantial body of research, driven by the recognition that while FL's distributed architecture offers inherent privacy advantages over centralized data collection, it does not in itself constitute a complete privacy solution. This subsection reviews the principal approaches to privacy-preserving federated learning, examining the theoretical foundations, practical mechanisms, and the trade-offs that researchers have identified across a growing literature.

Federated learning was conceived, in part, as a response to the data privacy challenges endemic to traditional distributed machine learning. As [arxiv_2010.09258] explains, FL represents an evolution from conventional distributed machine learning in that it offloads operations typically performed by a central server, transforming that server into a coordinating entity rather than a data-processing hub. In this architecture, sensitive information never leaves the client device; instead, clients train local sub-models and transmit encrypted updates to the central server for aggregation into a global model. This design provides strong baseline privacy guarantees that make FL particularly attractive in contexts where data breaches and information theft are persistent threats. Nevertheless, the literature has consistently demonstrated that these baseline guarantees are insufficient on their own, since model weights and gradients can leak information about the underlying training data, motivating the development of more rigorous privacy-preserving mechanisms layered on top of the basic FL framework [arxiv_2002.08423].

Among the most prominent of these mechanisms is differential privacy (DP), which has emerged as what [arxiv_2405.08299] describes as the de facto standard for privacy protection in federated learning, owing to its rigorous mathematical foundation and its capacity to provide provable, quantifiable privacy guarantees. Differential privacy operates by introducing carefully calibrated noise into the learning process, ensuring that the output of an algorithm is statistically indistinguishable whether or not any single individual's data is included. The systematic review presented in [arxiv_2405.08299] identifies a significant gap in the existing literature: while there is an abundance of algorithmic work incorporating differential privacy into FL, comprehensive taxonomic treatments that categorize and synthesize these contributions have been lacking. To address this, the authors propose a new taxonomy grounded in the definition and guarantee of different differential privacy models, distinguishing among the objects and levels of privacy protection they afford. This taxonomic contribution is important because, as the review makes clear, not all differential privacy formulations offer equivalent protections, and the choice of model has significant downstream consequences for both utility and security.

The integration of differential privacy with federated learning is not without cost, and a central tension in the literature concerns the trade-off between privacy guarantees and model performance. [arxiv_2101.12240] examines this tension in the context of communication constraints, noting that FL systems are simultaneously bottlenecked by communication costs, training variance, and data heterogeneity. The authors observe that the three standard communication-reduction techniques—model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation—each introduce additional variance into training, compounding the heterogeneity-induced variance that arises when devices sample data from different distributions. Incorporating differential privacy into this already complex setting further strains system performance, since noise injection interacts with variance in ways that can significantly degrade convergence. The work in [arxiv_2101.12240] thus frames the problem of differentially private FL under communication constraints as a multi-objective challenge requiring simultaneous management of privacy budgets, communication overhead, and statistical variance, a framing that highlights the interdependencies that simpler analyses tend to overlook.

The Sherpa.ai FL framework, described in [arxiv_2007.00914], represents a practical attempt to operationalize the combination of federated learning and differential privacy within a unified software environment. The authors argue that while several software tools have been released to support FL and DP functionalities individually, they lack the unified vision and methodological workflow necessary for practitioners to deploy them effectively together. The Sherpa.ai framework addresses this by providing a structured pipeline in which differential privacy mechanisms are integrated directly into the federated learning workflow, with explicit attention to measuring and managing privacy loss across the communication rounds between clients and the server. This emphasis on privacy loss quantification reflects the broader consensus in the literature that differential privacy's value lies not merely in the application of noise but in the ability to formally account for cumulative privacy expenditure across multiple interactions.

Simulation environments have also played an important role in advancing privacy-preserving FL research, particularly given the practical difficulties of deploying real-world federated systems. [arxiv_2002.08423] introduces PrivacyFL, a simulator designed to be extensible, configurable, and scalable, enabling researchers and practitioners to test privacy-preserving and secure FL configurations before committing to real-world deployment. The simulator's design acknowledges that setting up a federated learning environment with meaningful security and privacy guarantees is a time-consuming process involving numerous interacting parameters. By providing a controlled environment in which these parameters can be systematically varied, PrivacyFL facilitates the kind of empirical investigation that is otherwise difficult to conduct at scale. The existence of such tools reflects the maturation of the field and the growing recognition that theoretical guarantees must be complemented by practical evaluation infrastructure.

Beyond differential privacy, the literature has explored a range of cryptographic and statistical mechanisms for privacy-preserving aggregation. [arxiv_2203.17005] provides a comprehensive survey of privacy-preserving aggregation (PPAgg) protocols in FL, arguing that aggregation is the key protocol in privacy-preserving federated learning and that it has received substantial research interest precisely because it is the point at which individual model updates are combined into the global model, creating the greatest risk of information leakage. The survey reviews protocols addressing both privacy and security issues, encompassing approaches based on secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption, secret sharing, and differential privacy. By cataloguing these approaches and their respective guarantees, [arxiv_2203.17005] provides a useful map of the design space, revealing that different protocols involve distinct trade-offs between computational overhead, communication cost, and the strength of the privacy guarantee they provide. This comparative perspective is valuable because it makes clear that no single protocol dominates across all dimensions, and the appropriate choice depends heavily on the threat model and the operational constraints of the deployment context.

The application of privacy-preserving FL to non-neural-network models has also received attention, with [arxiv_2106.10662] proposing two variants of federated XGBoost—FedXGBoost-SMM and FedXGBoost-LDP—that extend privacy-preserving FL to gradient-boosted tree models. The authors note that practical adaptation of XGBoost to federated learning has been limited by the high cost incurred by conventional privacy-preserving methods, particularly encryption-based techniques. FedXGBoost-SMM addresses this by deploying an enhanced secure matrix multiplication method that preserves privacy with lossless accuracy and lower overhead than encryption, while FedXGBoost-LDP takes a different approach, using noise perturbation for local differential privacy. The coexistence of these two variants within a single paper is instructive: it illustrates that even within a specific application domain, the choice between cryptographic and statistical privacy mechanisms involves genuine trade-offs, with the secure computation approach offering stronger formal guarantees at higher computational cost and the local differential privacy approach offering greater efficiency at the price of some accuracy degradation. This mirrors the broader pattern identified in [arxiv_2203.17005], where the diversity of PPAgg protocols reflects the diversity of deployment requirements rather than a convergence toward a single best solution.

The straggler problem in federated learning—where slow or unresponsive clients delay global model updates—has been addressed in [arxiv_2201.10092] through a coded federated learning framework called stochastic coded federated learning (SCFL). What is particularly notable about this contribution from a privacy perspective is that the coding mechanism itself serves a dual purpose: each client generates a privacy-preserving coded dataset by adding additive noise to a random linear combination of its local data, and the server collects these coded datasets to construct a composite dataset that compensates for straggling effects. This design integrates privacy preservation directly into the mechanism for handling system heterogeneity, rather than treating them as separate concerns. The convergence and privacy guarantees are analyzed jointly, reflecting an understanding that in practical FL systems, privacy mechanisms cannot be designed in isolation from the system-level constraints they must coexist with. This integrated approach contrasts with frameworks that treat privacy as a post-hoc addition to an otherwise complete FL system, and it aligns with the broader argument in [arxiv_2101.12240] that communication constraints, variance, and privacy must be addressed together rather than sequentially.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals several important themes. First, there is broad consensus that the baseline privacy properties of federated learning, while meaningful, are insufficient to protect against all forms of information leakage, and that additional mechanisms—whether cryptographic, statistical, or both—are necessary [arxiv_2010.09258], [arxiv_2002.08423]. Second, differential privacy has established itself as the dominant formal framework for quantifying and guaranteeing privacy in FL, though its integration with communication constraints, data heterogeneity, and system-level challenges remains an active area of research [arxiv_2405.08299], [arxiv_2101.12240], [arxiv_2007.00914]. Third, the choice among privacy-preserving mechanisms involves genuine trade-offs that depend on the threat model, the computational and communication resources available, and the accuracy requirements of the application, as illustrated by the diversity of PPAgg protocols surveyed in [arxiv_2203.17005] and the competing designs proposed in [arxiv_2106.10662]. Finally, the development of simulation tools [arxiv_2002.08423] and unified frameworks [arxiv_2007.00914] reflects the field's growing emphasis on translating theoretical advances into practical, deployable systems—a transition that will be essential if privacy-preserving federated learning is to fulfill its promise in real-world applications.

### Evidence theme 4

The intersection of federated learning (FL) with privacy-preserving mechanisms represents one of the most active and consequential research frontiers in distributed machine learning. Federated learning, first introduced by Google in 2016, enables collaborative model training across multiple clients—ranging from mobile devices and edge nodes to entire organizations—without requiring raw data to leave the devices on which it resides [arxiv_2501.03477]. The fundamental architecture involves iterative cycles in which a global model is distributed to participating clients, locally trained on device-resident data, and then returned to a central server for aggregation into an updated global model [arxiv_2202.01529]. This decentralized paradigm has attracted substantial research attention precisely because it addresses growing regulatory and ethical concerns around data privacy, security, and compliance, particularly in sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, and smart Internet of Things (IoT) systems [arxiv_2504.17703]. Nevertheless, the apparent privacy guarantees of federated learning are not absolute, and the field has increasingly recognized that the framework must be augmented with formal privacy mechanisms to provide robust, provable protections. The following discussion surveys the principal challenges and solutions that define this research landscape, with particular attention to the interplay between privacy guarantees, communication efficiency, and model utility.

A foundational challenge in federated learning is that, despite the fact that raw data never leaves client devices, the model updates—gradients or weight differentials—transmitted between clients and the server can themselves leak sensitive information. Gradient inversion attacks and membership inference attacks have demonstrated that adversaries with access to model updates can reconstruct private training samples with alarming fidelity [arxiv_2104.06557]. This vulnerability is compounded by the non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) nature of data across clients, which is an inherent characteristic of real-world federated deployments. When data distributions differ substantially across participants, locally trained models diverge significantly from one another, and the aggregated global model may exhibit poor generalization, particularly for out-of-distribution inputs [arxiv_2104.06557]. The non-IID problem thus simultaneously undermines both model performance and, indirectly, privacy, since highly personalized local updates may be more susceptible to inference attacks. Comparative analyses between federated and centralized machine learning have confirmed that non-IID data distributions introduce characteristic training instabilities that have no direct analogue in centralized settings, where the server has full access to a unified dataset [arxiv_2202.01529]. These instabilities manifest as slower convergence, increased variance across communication rounds, and sensitivity to the choice of aggregation algorithm.

Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as the de facto formal standard for quantifying and bounding privacy leakage, and its integration with federated learning has become a major research priority [arxiv_2404.18814]. Differential privacy provides a mathematically rigorous guarantee that the output of a computation—in this context, the aggregated model update—does not change significantly whether or not any single individual's data is included, thereby limiting what an adversary can infer about any particular participant. The standard mechanism for achieving differential privacy in federated learning involves clipping client gradients to bound their sensitivity and then adding calibrated Gaussian or Laplacian noise before transmission to the server [arxiv_2304.05127]. While this approach provides provable privacy guarantees parameterized by a privacy budget ε, it introduces a fundamental tension: stronger privacy guarantees require larger noise magnitudes, which degrade convergence and reduce final model accuracy. This privacy-utility trade-off is not merely a practical inconvenience but a structural challenge that has motivated a substantial body of theoretical and algorithmic work [arxiv_2404.18814].

The distinction between central differential privacy (CDP) and local differential privacy (LDP) is particularly important in the federated context. Under central differential privacy, a trusted server aggregates client updates before noise is applied, which allows for more efficient noise addition and better utility at a given privacy level. However, this model assumes that the server itself is trustworthy, an assumption that is frequently untenable in cross-organizational or cross-device deployments [arxiv_2209.14086]. Local differential privacy, by contrast, requires each client to apply the privacy mechanism to its own update before transmission, ensuring that even a compromised or curious server cannot reconstruct individual contributions. While LDP offers stronger privacy guarantees in adversarial server settings, it typically requires substantially more noise to achieve the same formal privacy level as CDP, leading to more pronounced degradation in model utility [arxiv_2209.14086]. This trade-off has spurred research into intermediate trust models and hybrid mechanisms that seek to capture the benefits of both paradigms.

One notable contribution to this space is the CorBin-FL mechanism, which introduces correlated binary stochastic quantization as a means of simultaneously achieving differential privacy and communication efficiency [arxiv_2409.13133]. The key insight is that by leveraging common randomness shared among clients through secure multi-party computation, it is possible to perform correlated quantization of local model updates such that the aggregate noise introduced satisfies differential privacy requirements while the individual contributions remain protected. Theoretical analysis demonstrates that CorBin-FL achieves parameter-level local differential privacy (PLDP) and asymptotically optimizes the privacy-utility trade-off in terms of mean squared error [arxiv_2409.13133]. This approach is notable because it addresses the communication bottleneck—another critical challenge in federated learning—simultaneously with the privacy problem, rather than treating them as independent concerns. The communication bottleneck arises because transmitting full model updates, particularly for large neural networks, imposes prohibitive bandwidth requirements on resource-constrained edge devices [arxiv_2501.03477]. Quantization and compression techniques that are privacy-aware thus represent a particularly promising direction.

The challenge of balancing privacy and convergence performance has also been approached through careful analysis of the optimization dynamics of differentially private federated learning algorithms. Federated learning involves two nested optimization loops: local gradient steps performed on each client and global communication rounds in which updates are aggregated. The number of local steps and the number of communication rounds are hyperparameters that significantly affect both convergence speed and the effective privacy cost, since each communication round consumes a portion of the privacy budget [arxiv_2304.05127]. Research has established that an optimal balance exists between these two quantities that maximizes convergence performance within a fixed privacy budget. Specifically, formal proofs have been developed for the optimal number of local steps and communication rounds that improve the convergence bounds of differentially private variants of federated optimization algorithms such as SCAFFOLD [arxiv_2304.05127]. These results provide actionable guidance for practitioners who must configure federated learning systems under real-world privacy constraints, and they highlight the importance of treating privacy budget allocation as an integral part of algorithm design rather than an afterthought.

The momentum gradient descent approach offers another angle on this problem. By integrating local differential privacy with momentum-based gradient updates in the federated setting, it is possible to accelerate convergence while maintaining privacy guarantees, thereby partially mitigating the utility cost of noise injection [arxiv_2209.14086]. Momentum methods exploit the history of gradient directions to smooth out the noise introduced by both stochastic sampling and differential privacy mechanisms, and their combination with federated learning has been shown to reduce communication costs by enabling faster convergence in fewer rounds. This is particularly significant given that communication efficiency and privacy are both first-order concerns in practical federated deployments [arxiv_2209.14086].

Beyond differential privacy, the security of federated learning encompasses a broader threat landscape that includes Byzantine attacks, model poisoning, and backdoor injection by malicious clients. These adversarial threats are distinct from privacy leakage but interact with it in complex ways. A malicious client that seeks to degrade the global model or insert a backdoor may exploit the same non-IID data heterogeneity that complicates privacy protection [arxiv_2104.06557]. Causal federated learning has been proposed as a framework for simultaneously addressing robustness and privacy by incorporating causal reasoning into the local training process, thereby improving out-of-distribution generalization and reducing the susceptibility of local updates to both inference attacks and adversarial manipulation [arxiv_2104.06557]. This approach represents a conceptually distinct strategy from noise-based differential privacy, instead seeking to improve the intrinsic properties of the learned representations.

The holistic review of federated learning with differential privacy provided by recent survey work underscores that despite significant progress, the field faces persistent challenges in making the privacy-utility trade-off practically acceptable for real-world deployments [arxiv_2404.18814]. Practitioners frequently lack awareness of the full landscape of available mechanisms and their relative strengths and weaknesses, leading to suboptimal configurations that either sacrifice too much utility or provide weaker privacy guarantees than intended. The survey literature emphasizes the need for standardized benchmarks, clearer communication of privacy semantics to non-expert stakeholders, and continued algorithmic innovation to close the gap between theoretical privacy guarantees and practical model performance [arxiv_2504.17703]. Performance limitations in federated learning remain an active area of investigation, with the communication bottleneck and data non-IID-ness identified as the two most consequential open challenges for the field's continued development [arxiv_2501.03477].

In summary, the literature on privacy-preserving federated learning reveals a rich and evolving research landscape in which formal privacy mechanisms, optimization theory, communication efficiency, and adversarial robustness are deeply intertwined. The integration of differential privacy into federated learning provides provable guarantees but introduces fundamental trade-offs that require careful algorithmic design and hyperparameter selection. Emerging approaches such as correlated quantization, momentum-based optimization, and causal learning offer promising directions for navigating these trade-offs, while survey and comparative work continues to map the boundaries of what is currently achievable and to identify the most pressing open problems.

The preceding synthesis establishes the core tension between privacy guarantees and model utility, yet several additional dimensions of the federated learning landscape merit sustained scholarly attention. One such dimension concerns the architectural diversity of federated systems themselves. While the canonical federated learning setup assumes a star topology in which a central parameter server orchestrates all communication, this centralized architecture introduces a single point of failure and concentrates trust in a potentially adversarial or compromised aggregator [arxiv_2306.02570]. Decentralized federated learning, in which clients communicate directly with peers over a graph topology without any central coordinator, has therefore attracted considerable research interest as an alternative paradigm. The convergence properties of decentralized optimization algorithms in the federated setting differ substantially from their centralized counterparts, particularly when the communication graph is sparse or time-varying, and ensuring privacy in such environments requires fundamentally different cryptographic and statistical tools [arxiv_2210.00325]. The first privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithms for decentralized federated learning over time-varying graphs demonstrate that it is possible to achieve global model aggregation with formal privacy guarantees even when the network topology changes between successive rounds, though the analysis requires careful treatment of the information leakage that can accumulate across multiple hops in the communication graph [arxiv_2210.00325]. The intersection of decentralized optimization theory and federated learning thus represents a frontier where advances in graph theory, distributed control, and cryptography must be synthesized to produce practically deployable systems [arxiv_2306.02570].

The relationship between federated learning and its distributed machine learning antecedents deserves more explicit treatment than it often receives in the literature. Federated learning can be understood as a specialized instantiation of distributed machine learning that imposes additional constraints arising from the statistical heterogeneity of client data, the unreliability and limited bandwidth of client devices, and the requirement that raw data never leave the client [arxiv_2104.14362]. These constraints distinguish federated learning from classical data-parallel distributed training, in which workers are assumed to hold independent and identically distributed data shards and to communicate over high-bandwidth interconnects [arxiv_2010.09258]. The transition from distributed machine learning to federated learning therefore involves not merely a change in deployment context but a qualitative shift in the problem formulation, requiring new algorithmic primitives for handling client drift, partial participation, and asynchronous updates [arxiv_2107.06917]. A field guide to federated optimization formalizes these distinctions and provides a unified framework for analyzing the convergence of a broad class of federated optimization algorithms under realistic assumptions about data heterogeneity and communication constraints [arxiv_2107.06917]. This theoretical scaffolding is essential for understanding why algorithms that perform well in the distributed machine learning setting may degrade significantly when deployed in genuinely federated environments.

The treatment of non-IID data remains one of the most practically consequential open problems in federated learning, and its interaction with privacy mechanisms adds further complexity. When client data distributions are highly heterogeneous, local model updates diverge substantially from the global optimum, a phenomenon known as client drift, which both slows convergence and amplifies the sensitivity of the aggregated model to individual client contributions [arxiv_2106.06843]. From a differential privacy perspective, greater sensitivity implies that more noise must be injected to achieve a given privacy guarantee, which in turn further degrades model utility. This compounding effect means that the privacy-utility trade-off is substantially worse in non-IID settings than in the IID case, and algorithms designed for IID data may provide misleadingly optimistic performance estimates when evaluated on homogeneous benchmarks [arxiv_2402.10145]. Recent work has proposed chaotic map-based encryption as an alternative to additive Gaussian noise for privacy protection in non-IID federated learning, arguing that chaotic encryption can provide strong privacy guarantees with less degradation of model accuracy than differential privacy in highly heterogeneous settings [arxiv_2402.10145]. While such approaches are promising, they require careful cryptographic analysis to establish formal privacy guarantees comparable to those provided by differential privacy, and their practical security properties under adaptive adversaries remain an active area of investigation.

The secure aggregation literature provides a complementary perspective on privacy protection in federated learning, focusing on cryptographic rather than statistical mechanisms. Secure aggregation protocols ensure that the server learns only the aggregate of client updates and not the individual contributions, thereby preventing gradient inversion and membership inference attacks without requiring the injection of noise that degrades model utility [arxiv_2203.17005]. However, existing secure aggregation protocols impose substantial communication and computational overhead, particularly for large model updates, and their scalability to the thousands or millions of clients envisioned in large-scale federated deployments remains a significant engineering challenge [arxiv_2304.03841]. The e-SeaFL protocol addresses this by reducing the aggregation phase to a single communication round while supporting verifiability, allowing the server to prove to clients that the aggregation was performed correctly without revealing individual updates [arxiv_2304.03841]. Threshold-based secure aggregation schemes such as TAPFed further extend this line of work by distributing trust across multiple aggregation servers, so that privacy is preserved as long as fewer than a threshold number of servers collude [arxiv_2501.05053]. These threshold constructions are particularly relevant in cross-silo federated learning settings, where the number of participants is small enough to support more complex cryptographic protocols but the stakes of privacy violation are high due to the sensitivity of institutional data.

The combination of secure aggregation with differential privacy raises subtle technical questions that have only recently begun to receive systematic attention. When secure aggregation is used to protect individual updates, the noise required for differential privacy can in principle be added to the aggregate rather than to individual updates, which allows the noise magnitude to be scaled to the sensitivity of the sum rather than the sensitivity of individual contributions [arxiv_2407.19286]. This aggregate noise addition strategy can substantially improve the privacy-utility trade-off compared to local differential privacy, but it requires that the secure aggregation protocol be carefully integrated with the differential privacy mechanism to avoid information leakage through side channels such as the pattern of client participation or the timing of updates [arxiv_2407.19286]. The interaction between multiple local gradient steps and secure aggregation introduces additional complications, since the sensitivity of multi-step local updates is harder to bound tightly than that of single-step updates, and existing analyses often rely on conservative worst-case bounds that may be far from tight in practice [arxiv_2503.21154]. Developing tighter sensitivity analyses for multi-step local training under realistic data heterogeneity assumptions is therefore an important direction for future work.

Communication efficiency remains a first-order concern in federated learning that interacts with privacy in non-trivial ways. The two-bit aggregation framework demonstrates that extreme quantization of model updates to just two bits per parameter can simultaneously reduce communication costs and provide differential privacy guarantees, since the quantization noise itself can be calibrated to satisfy a privacy budget [arxiv_2110.03017]. This dual-purpose use of quantization noise is conceptually elegant and practically attractive, but it requires careful analysis to ensure that the privacy guarantees are not undermined by the structured nature of quantization artifacts, which may be more informative to an adversary than unstructured Gaussian noise [arxiv_2110.03017]. More broadly, the survey of communication-efficient federated learning identifies gradient compression, sparsification, and quantization as the three primary families of techniques for reducing communication overhead, and notes that each interacts differently with differential privacy mechanisms and secure aggregation protocols [arxiv_2208.01200]. Stochastic coded federated learning offers yet another approach, using coding-theoretic techniques to simultaneously address the straggler problem—in which slow clients delay global aggregation—and the communication bottleneck, while providing convergence guarantees under heterogeneous computational resources [arxiv_2201.10092]. The straggler problem is particularly acute in cross-device federated learning, where client devices may drop out mid-round due to battery depletion, network interruption, or user activity, and its interaction with privacy accounting requires careful treatment since the set of clients that complete a round may be correlated with their data distributions.

Finally, the broader security and privacy threat landscape of federated learning extends well beyond the gradient inversion and membership inference attacks that motivate differential privacy and secure aggregation. A comprehensive survey of attacks and defenses in federated learning catalogues a diverse array of threats including Byzantine fault tolerance, backdoor attacks, model poisoning, and free-riding, and organizes the corresponding defense mechanisms into a unified taxonomy [arxiv_2508.13730]. This survey perspective is valuable for situating the privacy-preserving mechanisms discussed throughout this review within the larger context of federated learning security, and for identifying the gaps and inconsistencies in current defense strategies. Notably, many defenses against Byzantine attacks rely on robust aggregation rules that inspect the distribution of client updates, which may conflict with the privacy requirements of differential privacy and secure aggregation that are designed to prevent the server from learning anything about individual updates [arxiv_2508.13730]. Resolving this tension between robustness and privacy is one of the most challenging open problems in the field, and it underscores the need for holistic system designs that treat security, privacy, and utility as jointly optimized objectives rather than independent desiderata addressed by separate algorithmic components [arxiv_2301.01299].
