# Federated Learning (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning — evidence cluster A

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across multiple participants without requiring the direct sharing of raw data. This approach has attracted substantial scholarly attention, resulting in a growing body of survey literature that examines its foundations, challenges, and evolving methodologies from complementary perspectives [arxiv_2104.14362] [arxiv_2301.01299].

At its core, federated learning arose in response to a fundamental tension between the practical necessity of large-scale data aggregation for effective machine learning and the legal, regulatory, and ethical constraints that increasingly govern data sharing across organizational and jurisdictional boundaries. As Li et al. observe, data and computing resources are now typically distributed across end-user devices, regions, and organizations, and laws or regulations frequently prohibit their direct consolidation [arxiv_2104.14362]. Federated learning addresses this tension by enabling participants to collaboratively train models while keeping their data local, transmitting only model updates or gradients rather than raw information [arxiv_2301.01299]. This architectural choice provides a degree of privacy protection that centralized learning cannot offer, since no party is required to expose its underlying dataset during the training process [arxiv_2301.01299].

The conceptual lineage of federated learning is closely tied to broader developments in distributed machine learning, and several surveys have traced this intellectual genealogy in detail. The survey by Lim et al. proposes a functional architecture for federated learning systems and situates FL within a taxonomy of distributed training techniques, covering aspects such as data communication protocols and system-level security mechanisms [arxiv_2104.14362]. This architectural framing is valuable because it clarifies how federated learning inherits certain properties from classical distributed optimization while introducing novel constraints, particularly around communication overhead and participant heterogeneity. More recent systematic surveys have extended this foundation by cataloguing advanced federated methods and their applications across domains, reflecting the rapid maturation of the field since its initial formalization [arxiv_2301.01299].

One of the most persistently studied challenges in federated learning is the problem of statistical heterogeneity, commonly referred to as the non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) data problem. In standard centralized learning, the assumption that training samples are drawn from a common distribution underpins the convergence guarantees of most optimization algorithms. In federated settings, however, each participating client holds data that reflects its own local context, usage patterns, or population, meaning that the joint distribution of data across clients can be highly non-uniform. The survey by Zhu et al. provides a detailed analysis of how non-IID data degrades model performance in federated learning, examining its effects on both parametric models such as neural networks and non-parametric models, and considering both horizontal federated learning—where clients share the same feature space but different samples—and vertical federated learning—where clients share the same samples but different features [arxiv_2106.06843]. Their review demonstrates that non-IID conditions consistently produce worse outcomes than centralized training, and they systematically evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of proposed mitigation strategies, including data-sharing approaches, regularization techniques, and personalization methods [arxiv_2106.06843]. This analysis underscores that statistical heterogeneity is not merely a technical inconvenience but a structural property of federated environments that demands dedicated algorithmic solutions.

Communication efficiency represents a second major axis of challenge in federated learning research, and it has received increasing attention as deployments have scaled to larger numbers of clients and more complex models. In traditional distributed machine learning, private data is transmitted between clients and a central server, creating privacy risks; federated learning was partly designed to eliminate this exposure, but in doing so it introduces a different set of communication burdens [arxiv_2208.01200]. Because model updates or gradients must be exchanged repeatedly across potentially many rounds of training, the cumulative communication cost can become prohibitive, particularly in settings involving mobile devices or bandwidth-constrained networks. The survey by Shi et al. addresses this problem systematically by examining FL communication research from three perspectives: communication efficiency, the communication environment, and communication resource allocation [arxiv_2208.01200]. This tripartite framework is useful because it separates concerns that are sometimes conflated in the literature—namely, the algorithmic question of how to reduce the volume or frequency of transmitted information, the environmental question of how network topology and reliability affect training dynamics, and the resource management question of how to schedule and prioritize communication among heterogeneous participants. Compared to surveys that treat communication primarily as a secondary concern subordinate to convergence analysis, this work foregrounds communication as a first-class research problem with its own distinct sub-challenges [arxiv_2208.01200].

Taken together, these surveys reveal that federated learning is best understood not as a single algorithm but as a broad framework encompassing a diverse set of design choices and trade-offs. The functional architecture proposed by Lim et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] provides a structural vocabulary for comparing these choices, while the non-IID survey of Zhu et al. [arxiv_2106.06843] and the communication-focused review of Shi et al. [arxiv_2208.01200] illuminate two of the most consequential dimensions along which federated systems can fail or succeed. The more recent systematic survey [arxiv_2301.01299] synthesizes advances across these and other dimensions, noting that earlier surveys tended to focus on specific perspectives and that the field has continued to evolve rapidly. Collectively, this body of literature establishes that privacy preservation, statistical heterogeneity, and communication efficiency form an interconnected set of concerns that any comprehensive treatment of federated learning must address, and it is within this multi-dimensional problem space that subsequent methodological contributions must be situated.

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning — evidence cluster B

Privacy preservation in federated learning has emerged as one of the most actively investigated dimensions of the paradigm, driven by the recognition that the exchange of model updates between clients and a central server—or among peers in decentralized settings—can itself constitute a vector for sensitive information leakage. Even when raw data never leaves a device, gradient information and intermediate model parameters can be exploited by adversaries to reconstruct training samples or infer membership, motivating a rich body of work that seeks to embed formal privacy guarantees directly into the federated training process.

Differential privacy (DP) has been among the most widely adopted frameworks for providing such guarantees, owing to its mathematically rigorous definition of privacy loss and its composability properties. However, a persistent tension exists between the strength of the privacy guarantee and the utility of the resulting model. Naïve applications of DP mechanisms—particularly those based on Gaussian or Laplace noise injection—can severely degrade convergence and final model accuracy, especially when the number of communication rounds is large or the participating client population is heterogeneous. Addressing this tension directly, [arxiv_2003.13761] proposes a federated learning approach grounded in concentrated differential privacy (CDP), a refinement of the standard DP definition that yields tighter privacy accounting under composition. The authors argue that CDP allows the noise magnitude added to gradient updates to be calibrated more precisely across rounds, thereby preserving model utility to a substantially greater degree than mechanisms designed for worst-case DP guarantees. Their empirical results demonstrate that the CDP-based approach achieves competitive model accuracy while maintaining meaningful privacy budgets, suggesting that the choice of privacy accounting framework—not merely the presence of noise—is a critical design variable in privacy-preserving federated learning.

While differential privacy operates by injecting carefully calibrated noise into the learning process, an alternative line of research pursues privacy through cryptographic and protocol-level mechanisms that seek to prevent information leakage without necessarily degrading model quality through noise. Homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation have been explored in this vein, though both carry substantial computational and communication overhead. [arxiv_2012.07183] takes a different approach, developing a privacy-preserving decentralized aggregation protocol that controls the communication topology among participants in each aggregation round rather than relying on DP or homomorphic encryption. The protocol is formulated using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), and the authors analyze its privacy properties against an honest-but-curious adversary—a threat model in which participants follow the protocol faithfully but attempt to infer private information from the messages they receive. By restricting which participants communicate with one another in each round, the protocol limits the information any single participant can accumulate, thereby bounding privacy leakage without introducing the accuracy penalties associated with noise-based mechanisms. The authors also propose an efficient algorithm for constructing the communication pattern, drawing on combinatorial insights to balance privacy and convergence efficiency. This work illustrates that privacy guarantees in federated learning need not be purchased exclusively through statistical noise; structural constraints on information flow can serve as a complementary or alternative mechanism.

The decentralized setting introduces additional complexity that neither centralized DP approaches nor fixed-topology protocols fully address. In fully peer-to-peer federated learning, where no central coordinator exists, achieving consensus on a global model while preserving privacy is substantially more challenging, particularly when the communication graph among learners is dynamic rather than fixed. [arxiv_2210.00325] addresses precisely this scenario, proposing what the authors describe as the first privacy-preserving consensus-based algorithm for decentralized federated learning over time-varying communication graphs. The algorithm integrates the Metropolis-Hastings method to update the weighted adjacency matrix of the communication graph at each aggregation round, accommodating the high mobility environments in which the graph topology may shift between successive rounds. Privacy is achieved through the integration of Shamir's secret sharing scheme, a cryptographic primitive that allows a secret to be distributed among participants such that no subset below a threshold size can reconstruct it. The paper establishes both correctness and privacy properties formally, and evaluates computational efficiency empirically. Compared to [arxiv_2012.07183], which also operates in a decentralized aggregation setting but assumes a more stable communication structure, [arxiv_2210.00325] explicitly models and accommodates topological variability, representing a meaningful extension toward more realistic deployment scenarios such as mobile or vehicular networks.

Beyond the choice of privacy mechanism, the statistical properties of the data distributed across clients introduce further complications. Non-independently and identically distributed (non-IID) data—a condition that arises naturally when clients represent distinct populations or usage patterns—can exacerbate the degradation caused by privacy mechanisms, since the noise or communication constraints imposed for privacy interact with the already-challenging optimization landscape created by data heterogeneity. [arxiv_2402.10145] investigates this intersection explicitly, proposing a federated deep learning method that combines a privacy layer based on chaotic maps-based encryption with strategies for handling non-IID and incomplete datasets. The paper provides a direct empirical comparison between differential privacy and chaotic-based encryption as privacy layers, finding that the federated learning process improves average performance metrics even under non-IID conditions regardless of which privacy mechanism is employed. This comparative perspective is valuable because it situates chaotic encryption—a less commonly studied mechanism in the federated learning literature—alongside the dominant DP paradigm, offering practitioners a broader menu of options when designing systems for sensitive, heterogeneous data environments.

Taken together, these works reveal a landscape in which privacy-preserving federated learning is not a monolithic problem with a single solution, but rather a multidimensional design space. The choice among differential privacy [arxiv_2003.13761] [arxiv_2402.10145], communication-topology control [arxiv_2012.07183], secret sharing [arxiv_2210.00325], and chaotic encryption [arxiv_2402.10145] depends on the threat model, the network topology, the degree of data heterogeneity, and the acceptable trade-off between privacy and model utility. Progress in the field increasingly requires not only the development of individual mechanisms but also principled frameworks for comparing and combining them across these dimensions.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning — evidence cluster A

Federated learning has emerged as a prominent paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed data sources without requiring raw data to be shared with a central server [arxiv_2306.02570]. While this architecture provides a foundational layer of privacy by keeping data local, it does not, in isolation, offer formal privacy guarantees sufficient to withstand sophisticated inference attacks. Consequently, a substantial body of research has focused on augmenting federated learning with complementary privacy-preserving mechanisms, most notably differential privacy and secure aggregation. These techniques, individually and in combination, represent the primary technical strategies for reconciling the competing demands of data privacy and model utility in federated settings.

Differential privacy has become the dominant formal framework for quantifying and bounding the privacy leakage associated with model updates in federated learning. By injecting calibrated noise into the learning process, differential privacy ensures that the participation of any individual data point cannot be reliably inferred from the resulting model. However, as several researchers have observed, the practical deployment of differential privacy in federated learning is far from straightforward. The choice of protection granularity—whether at the level of individual samples, users, or batches—has profound implications for both the formal privacy guarantees and the achievable model utility [arxiv_2407.19286]. Sample-level protection, which is the most fine-grained and therefore most demanding form of differential privacy, is particularly challenging to implement efficiently in federated settings because it typically requires frequent communication between participants and the aggregation server. This communication overhead can become prohibitive in real-world deployments, especially when participants are mobile devices operating under bandwidth and energy constraints.

The tension between communication efficiency and privacy guarantees is a recurring theme in the literature. In the standard federated learning protocol, participants perform multiple local optimization steps between communication rounds in order to reduce the frequency of synchronization with the server. This practice, often referred to as local SGD or FedAvg-style training, is central to the practical scalability of federated learning. However, when combined with sample-level differential privacy and secure aggregation, the existing analytical frameworks have historically required communication at every local optimization step to fully exploit the privacy amplification benefits of secure aggregation [arxiv_2407.19286]. This constraint effectively negates one of the primary efficiency advantages of federated learning. Addressing this limitation, recent work has demonstrated that a refined analysis can permit multiple local optimization steps while preserving the formal privacy guarantees associated with secure aggregation, thereby enabling higher utility models without sacrificing the privacy properties that motivate the use of these techniques in the first place [arxiv_2407.19286].

Secure aggregation addresses a distinct but related vulnerability in federated learning: the risk that a curious or malicious aggregation server may exploit the model updates submitted by individual participants to infer sensitive information about their local training data. Cryptographic approaches to secure aggregation, including protocols based on secret sharing and multi-party computation, have been proposed as a means of ensuring that the server learns only the aggregate of participants' updates and nothing about any individual contribution. The SCOTCH framework exemplifies this line of work, proposing a decentralized multi-party secure computation protocol that deploys secret sharing primitives to protect against both curious aggregators and colluding data owners [arxiv_2201.07730]. A notable feature of SCOTCH is its emphasis on communication efficiency: the authors argue that prior cryptographic approaches to secure aggregation have often incurred large communication overheads or degraded model performance, and they position their framework as achieving strict privacy guarantees with minimal additional cost relative to the state of the art [arxiv_2201.07730]. This concern with practical efficiency is broadly shared across the literature, reflecting the recognition that privacy-preserving mechanisms must be deployable in realistic network and computational environments to be of genuine value.

The combination of differential privacy and secure aggregation is not merely additive in its privacy benefits. When used together, secure aggregation can amplify the privacy guarantees of differential privacy by ensuring that the noise added by individual participants is not observable by the server, which in turn allows for less noise to be added while achieving the same formal privacy level. This synergy is a key motivation for studying the two techniques in conjunction rather than in isolation [arxiv_2407.19286]. Nevertheless, realizing this synergy in practice requires careful co-design of the two mechanisms, and the interaction between them introduces analytical complexities that have not always been fully addressed in prior work.

Beyond the centralized federated learning setting, a growing body of research has examined the use of decentralized communication topologies as an alternative architectural foundation. In centralized federated learning, all participants communicate with a single server, which creates a single point of failure and concentrates trust in the server operator. Decentralized federated learning replaces this hub-and-spoke topology with peer-to-peer communication graphs, distributing both the aggregation function and the associated trust assumptions across the network [arxiv_2306.02570]. This architectural shift introduces new challenges related to convergence analysis, data heterogeneity, and communication efficiency, but also opens new opportunities for privacy preservation by eliminating the need for any single party to observe all model updates [arxiv_2311.16008]. Empirical investigations of decentralized aggregation combined with differential privacy have demonstrated that the selection of differential privacy parameters is critical to the privacy-utility trade-off, and that decentralized topologies can offer meaningful privacy benefits relative to centralized alternatives, particularly in the presence of membership inference attacks [arxiv_2311.16008].

Taken together, these contributions reflect a maturing research agenda that seeks to move beyond the basic federated learning framework toward systems that provide rigorous, formally verifiable privacy guarantees while remaining practical for deployment at scale. The interplay between differential privacy, secure aggregation, and decentralized communication topology constitutes a rich design space, and the literature reviewed here represents significant progress in understanding the trade-offs and opportunities within that space [arxiv_2306.02570][arxiv_2407.19286][arxiv_2201.07730][arxiv_2311.16008].

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning — evidence cluster B

Federated learning has emerged as a foundational paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling multiple participants to collaboratively train models without centralizing raw data. At its core, the framework is motivated by privacy protection and the practical reality that sensitive data is often siloed across organizations, devices, or jurisdictions. As articulated in the field guide by [arxiv_2107.06917], federated learning and analytics represent a distributed approach for collaboratively learning models or statistics from decentralized data, with the distributed learning process formulated as solving federated optimization problems that emphasize communication efficiency, data heterogeneity, and compatibility with privacy and system requirements. These constraints distinguish federated optimization from conventional centralized machine learning settings and have driven a substantial body of research aimed at addressing the unique challenges that arise when training occurs across heterogeneous, non-independently-distributed data sources.

The privacy motivation underlying federated learning is well established in the literature. By keeping data local and sharing only model updates such as gradients or weights, federated learning was initially conceived as a privacy-preserving alternative to centralized data collection. However, as [arxiv_2501.05053] makes clear, traditional federated learning platforms are unable to ensure privacy in practice due to privacy leaks caused by the interchange of gradients. This observation is critical: the mere act of sharing gradient information can expose sensitive details about local training data, undermining the privacy guarantees that federated learning ostensibly provides. Inference attacks, including recently demonstrated disaggregation attacks, have shown that an adversary with access to individual gradient updates can reconstruct private information about participants. This gap between the theoretical privacy promise of federated learning and its practical vulnerabilities has motivated a growing line of research into secure aggregation mechanisms and other cryptographic enhancements.

The broader landscape of federated learning encompasses not only privacy concerns but also questions of model architecture, resource constraints, and system design. A systematic review of the literature [arxiv_2311.10832] situates federated learning as a distributed learning framework enhanced to preserve the privacy of individuals' data, noting its particular relevance in collaborative research contexts where data is sensitive and cannot be pooled. The review highlights that federated learning has significant implications for real-world problems, especially in crisis scenarios requiring real-time decision-making, where multiple entities must work collectively without sharing sensitive data. This distributed approach enables the leveraging of information from multiple sources and the generation of more diverse insights, which is particularly valuable in domains such as healthcare, finance, and emergency response. The systematic treatment offered by [arxiv_2311.10832], conducted according to PRISMA guidelines, underscores the maturity of the field and the breadth of supervised and unsupervised machine learning approaches that have been explored within the federated setting.

An important architectural dimension of federated learning concerns how the model itself is distributed across participants. The conventional federated learning paradigm, in which each client holds a complete copy of the model and trains it locally before sending updates to a central server, is not the only viable configuration. Split learning offers an alternative in which the model architecture is divided between clients and the server, with clients processing data only up to a designated cut layer and transmitting intermediate representations rather than raw data or full gradients. As [arxiv_2004.12088] explains, split learning provides better model privacy than federated learning due to this architectural split, and it is also better suited to resource-constrained environments because clients need not store or compute over the full model. However, split learning suffers from slower training due to its relay-based sequential training across multiple clients, a limitation that federated learning does not share to the same degree. To address this trade-off, [arxiv_2004.12088] proposes SplitFed learning, a hybrid approach that combines federated and split learning to eliminate the inherent drawbacks of each. The empirical results reported in that work demonstrate that the combined approach achieves meaningful improvements, and the incorporation of differential privacy and PixelDP further enhances data privacy and model robustness. This line of work illustrates a broader trend in the field: rather than treating federated learning as a monolithic paradigm, researchers are increasingly exploring hybrid and modular architectures that can be tailored to specific privacy, efficiency, and resource requirements.

Returning to the challenge of privacy vulnerabilities in gradient exchange, [arxiv_2501.05053] proposes TAPFed as a concrete solution for achieving privacy-preserving federated learning in the context of multiple decentralized aggregators, some of which may be malicious. The approach is built on a threshold functional encryption scheme that allows for a certain number of malicious aggregators while maintaining security. This is a meaningful advance over prior secure aggregation solutions, which the authors identify as vulnerable to disaggregation attacks. By distributing trust across multiple aggregators and requiring a threshold of honest parties for correct aggregation, TAPFed addresses a structural weakness in centralized aggregation architectures. The contrast with standard federated learning setups is instructive: whereas [arxiv_2107.06917] frames federated optimization primarily in terms of communication efficiency and data heterogeneity, [arxiv_2501.05053] foregrounds the adversarial threat model and the need for cryptographic guarantees, reflecting the evolution of the field's concerns as practical attacks have become better understood.

Taken together, these works paint a picture of federated learning as a rich and multifaceted research area. The foundational optimization perspective offered by [arxiv_2107.06917] provides the algorithmic scaffolding upon which privacy and architectural innovations are built. The systematic review in [arxiv_2311.10832] documents the breadth of machine learning approaches that have been adapted to the federated setting. The hybrid SplitFed framework in [arxiv_2004.12088] demonstrates that architectural choices can themselves serve as privacy and efficiency mechanisms. And the cryptographic approach of TAPFed [arxiv_2501.05053] addresses the residual privacy vulnerabilities that persist even when gradient sharing is carefully managed. Collectively, this body of work reflects the field's ongoing effort to reconcile the practical demands of distributed learning with robust, verifiable privacy guarantees.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a compelling paradigm for distributed machine learning that fundamentally reorients how sensitive data is handled during model training. Rather than centralizing raw data on a server, FL allows participating clients—ranging from smartphones to IoT sensors—to train local sub-models and transmit only encrypted model updates to a coordinating server, which aggregates these updates into a shared global model [arxiv_2010.09258]. This architectural shift is motivated in large part by the growing prevalence of data breaches and information theft, making FL an attractive alternative to conventional distributed machine learning (DML) frameworks in which the central server plays a far more directive role [arxiv_2010.09258]. Despite this foundational privacy advantage, however, the mere act of keeping raw data local does not fully resolve the privacy and security challenges that arise in practice, and a substantial body of research has consequently focused on strengthening the privacy guarantees embedded within FL systems.

A central mechanism through which such guarantees are pursued is Privacy-Preserving Aggregation (PPAgg), which refers to the suite of cryptographic and statistical protocols applied at the model aggregation step to prevent the server or other participants from inferring sensitive information from the updates they receive. As surveyed by [arxiv_2203.17005], the rapid adoption of FL across both academia and industry has generated a large and diverse literature on PPAgg, yet comprehensive treatments of these protocols had remained scarce prior to that work. The survey identifies PPAgg as the key protocol in Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning (PPFL), noting that practical PPFL systems allow multiple participants to train independently and then aggregate their models in a manner that provides formal privacy guarantees. The breadth of approaches catalogued in [arxiv_2203.17005] underscores that no single technique dominates; instead, researchers have drawn on secure multiparty computation, homomorphic encryption, secret sharing, and differential privacy, each offering distinct trade-offs between computational overhead, communication cost, and the strength of the privacy guarantee provided.

Among these mechanisms, differential privacy (DP) has attracted particular attention because of its strong theoretical foundations and its relative compatibility with the communication constraints that are endemic to FL deployments. Federated learning systems are routinely bottlenecked by communication costs, and this bottleneck is typically addressed through model compression, partial device participation, and periodic aggregation—each of which introduces additional training variance [arxiv_2101.12240]. The situation is further complicated by data heterogeneity: because client devices sample data from potentially different distributions, the variance among device updates during training is elevated beyond what would be observed in homogeneous distributed settings [arxiv_2101.12240]. Variance-reduction algorithms have been proposed to address heterogeneity, but these solutions often demand additional communication resources to transmit control information, creating a tension between statistical efficiency and communication efficiency [arxiv_2101.12240]. When differential privacy mechanisms are layered on top of this already constrained setting, the challenge intensifies, because DP noise injection can amplify the variance that communication-reduction techniques are simultaneously trying to suppress. The work surveyed in [arxiv_2101.12240] thus situates DP within a broader optimization landscape in which privacy, communication, and variance must be jointly managed rather than treated as independent concerns.

The challenge of integrating privacy-preserving mechanisms with practical FL frameworks is not limited to neural network models trained via gradient descent. Tree-based ensemble methods, and XGBoost in particular, represent a widely used class of models whose adaptation to FL has been constrained by the high computational overhead of conventional privacy-preserving techniques [arxiv_2106.10662]. To address this, [arxiv_2106.10662] proposes two variants of federated XGBoost: FedXGBoost-SMM, which employs an enhanced secure matrix multiplication method to achieve lossless accuracy with lower overhead than encryption-based approaches, and FedXGBoost-LDP, which applies local differential privacy through noise perturbation and is evaluated empirically on both real-world and synthetic datasets. The contrast between these two protocols is instructive: FedXGBoost-SMM prioritizes accuracy preservation and relies on cryptographic guarantees, while FedXGBoost-LDP accepts a degree of accuracy degradation in exchange for a lighter-weight, locally enforced privacy mechanism. This trade-off mirrors the broader tension identified in [arxiv_2203.17005] between the robustness of cryptographic PPAgg protocols and the scalability of statistical approaches such as differential privacy.

Taken together, these works illuminate several recurring themes in the privacy-preserving FL literature. First, privacy cannot be treated as an isolated property; it is inextricably linked to communication efficiency, computational cost, and model accuracy, and advances in one dimension frequently impose costs in another [arxiv_2101.12240; arxiv_2106.10662]. Second, the aggregation step is a particularly sensitive point in the FL pipeline, and the design of PPAgg protocols must account for a wide range of threat models and system constraints [arxiv_2203.17005]. Third, while the foundational architecture of FL—in which raw data never leaves the client device—provides a meaningful baseline privacy guarantee [arxiv_2010.09258], this baseline is insufficient on its own, and the field has responded with an increasingly sophisticated toolkit of cryptographic and statistical mechanisms. The diversity of approaches surveyed in [arxiv_2203.17005], the communication-privacy trade-offs analyzed in [arxiv_2101.12240], and the model-specific adaptations proposed in [arxiv_2106.10662] collectively reflect the maturation of privacy-preserving FL from a conceptual aspiration into an active engineering discipline with well-defined sub-problems and a growing repertoire of practical solutions.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The intersection of federated learning and differential privacy has emerged as one of the most active and consequential research domains in privacy-preserving machine learning, attracting sustained scholarly attention across algorithmic design, systems engineering, and formal privacy guarantees. This body of work reflects a shared recognition that federated learning, while architecturally motivated by privacy, does not in itself provide sufficient formal guarantees against information leakage, and that differential privacy offers a mathematically rigorous complement to its distributed training paradigm.

At the foundational level, federated learning enables distributed clients to collaboratively train a shared machine learning model while retaining their training data locally, thereby reducing direct data exposure [arxiv_2002.08423]. However, as multiple studies have emphasized, this architectural separation does not eliminate privacy risk entirely. It remains possible to infer sensitive information about a client's training data from the model weights or gradient updates exchanged during the training process [arxiv_2002.08423]. This vulnerability has motivated the integration of differential privacy into federated learning pipelines, where differential privacy provides a formal, quantifiable bound on the amount of information that can be extracted about any individual data point from the outputs of the learning process [arxiv_2405.08299]. The appeal of differential privacy in this context lies precisely in its rigorous mathematical foundation and its capacity to offer provable guarantees, properties that have elevated it to what one systematic review describes as the de facto standard for privacy protection in federated learning [arxiv_2405.08299].

Despite the conceptual clarity of combining these two paradigms, the practical realization of differentially private federated learning involves navigating a complex landscape of design choices. The level at which differential privacy is applied—whether at the level of individual clients, the server, or the communication channel—has significant implications for both the strength of the privacy guarantee and the utility of the resulting model. Existing taxonomies have not always adequately distinguished between these levels or the different differential privacy models that may be employed, a gap that a recent systematic review explicitly seeks to address by proposing a new taxonomy grounded in the definition and guarantee of privacy protection [arxiv_2405.08299]. This taxonomic effort reflects a broader maturation of the field, in which the accumulation of algorithmic contributions has outpaced the development of organizing frameworks capable of synthesizing and comparing them.

A parallel challenge concerns the software infrastructure required to implement and experiment with differentially private federated learning systems. Two notable contributions to this infrastructure are the Sherpa.ai Federated Learning framework and the PrivacyFL simulator, each of which approaches the problem from a somewhat different angle. The Sherpa.ai framework, described by its authors as built upon a unified vision for federated learning and differential privacy, was motivated by the observation that while several software tools had been released to support these techniques, they lacked the coherent methodological workflow necessary to guide practitioners in their use [arxiv_2007.00914]. The framework thus aims not only to implement the relevant algorithms but also to provide methodological guidelines that help users reason about privacy loss in the communication among federated learning components [arxiv_2007.00914]. PrivacyFL, by contrast, is positioned explicitly as a simulator designed to help clients assess whether collaboration in a federated setting is feasible and whether it is likely to improve model accuracy before committing to a full deployment [arxiv_2002.08423]. Its emphasis on extensibility, configurability, and scalability reflects the practical reality that federated learning environments involve numerous parameters and configurations, and that the cost of setting up such environments—particularly with security and privacy guarantees—is substantial [arxiv_2002.08423]. Together, these two tools illustrate the dual imperatives of the field: the need for principled theoretical frameworks and the need for practical, accessible tooling.

Beyond the privacy dimension, federated learning systems face significant performance challenges that interact with privacy mechanisms in non-trivial ways. One prominent issue is the straggler problem, which arises because clients in a federated system typically possess heterogeneous computational resources and operate under fluctuating communication conditions, leading to slow convergence and unstable training dynamics [arxiv_2201.10092]. The stochastic coded federated learning (SCFL) framework addresses this problem through a coded dataset approach in which each client generates a privacy-preserving coded dataset by adding additive noise to a random linear combination of its local data, with the server subsequently aggregating these coded datasets to compensate for straggling effects [arxiv_2201.10092]. Crucially, this approach is designed to provide both convergence guarantees and privacy guarantees simultaneously, illustrating the broader research imperative of achieving these objectives in concert rather than treating them as independent concerns [arxiv_2201.10092]. The additive noise mechanism employed in SCFL is conceptually related to the noise injection strategies central to differential privacy, though the specific formulation and analytical framework differ.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals several converging themes. First, there is a consistent recognition that the privacy protections afforded by federated learning's distributed architecture are necessary but not sufficient, and that differential privacy provides the formal guarantees needed to close this gap [arxiv_2002.08423; arxiv_2405.08299; arxiv_2007.00914]. Second, the field has produced a growing ecosystem of software tools and frameworks, though these tools have historically lacked unified methodological guidance [arxiv_2007.00914], a limitation that more recent simulator-based approaches have begun to address [arxiv_2002.08423]. Third, the integration of privacy mechanisms with performance optimization remains an active and technically demanding research frontier, as demonstrated by work on coded federated learning that seeks to address stragglers without sacrificing privacy [arxiv_2201.10092]. Finally, the rapid accumulation of algorithmic contributions has created a pressing need for systematic reviews and taxonomies capable of organizing and synthesizing the field's findings [arxiv_2405.08299], underscoring the importance of meta-level scholarly work alongside primary algorithmic research.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in distributed machine learning, enabling collaborative model training across decentralized devices without requiring raw data to leave local storage. Originally introduced by Google in 2016, the framework has since attracted substantial research attention spanning optimization algorithms, privacy mechanisms, communication efficiency, and robustness [arxiv_2501.03477]. At its core, FL operates by distributing an untrained or partially trained global model to individual client devices, where it is refined using locally held data; the resulting model updates are then transmitted back to a central server, which aggregates them to improve the global model iteratively [arxiv_2202.01529]. This architecture is fundamentally motivated by privacy considerations, as sensitive user data never leaves the device, yet it introduces a distinct set of technical challenges that differentiate FL from conventional centralized machine learning.

One of the most extensively studied challenges in FL is the non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) nature of data across participating clients. In centralized machine learning, training data is typically assumed to be drawn from a common distribution, allowing gradient-based optimization to converge reliably. In FL, however, each client's local dataset reflects the idiosyncratic behavior, preferences, or environment of that particular device or user, resulting in heterogeneous data distributions that can substantially impair convergence and degrade global model performance [arxiv_2202.01529]. This non-IID characteristic introduces client drift, wherein locally optimized model updates may diverge from the direction that would benefit the global objective, making aggregation less effective. Empirical and theoretical analyses have confirmed that data heterogeneity is among the primary factors limiting FL performance, alongside communication bottlenecks that arise from the sheer volume of model updates transmitted across potentially bandwidth-constrained edge devices [arxiv_2501.03477]. Together, these two issues—non-IID data distribution and communication overhead—constitute the central engineering challenges that the FL research community continues to address.

Beyond these foundational concerns, the privacy guarantees offered by FL's decentralized architecture are widely recognized as insufficient on their own. While FL prevents raw data from being shared, model updates themselves can leak sensitive information about local training data through gradient inversion attacks and membership inference attacks. This vulnerability has motivated the integration of formal privacy-preserving mechanisms, most notably differential privacy (DP), into FL pipelines [arxiv_2404.18814]. Differential privacy provides mathematically rigorous guarantees by injecting calibrated noise into model updates, ensuring that the presence or absence of any individual data point cannot be inferred from the published model. The combination of FL and DP is therefore considered a natural and powerful pairing for comprehensive privacy preservation, and extensive research efforts have been devoted to making this integration practically viable [arxiv_2404.18814]. Nevertheless, achieving a satisfactory balance between privacy guarantees and model utility remains a persistent challenge, as the noise required for strong DP guarantees can substantially degrade model accuracy, particularly in settings with many clients or highly sensitive data.

Recent work has sought to address this privacy-utility trade-off through novel mechanism design. CorBin-FL, for instance, introduces a privacy mechanism based on correlated binary stochastic quantization, which simultaneously achieves differential privacy and communication efficiency [arxiv_2409.13133]. By leveraging secure multi-party computation techniques, CorBin-FL enables clients to perform correlated quantization of their local model updates in a manner that preserves individual privacy while reducing the communication cost of transmitting high-dimensional gradient vectors. Theoretical analysis demonstrates that CorBin-FL achieves parameter-level local differential privacy (PLDP) and asymptotically optimizes the privacy-utility trade-off as measured by mean square error [arxiv_2409.13133]. This approach is notable because it addresses two of FL's core limitations—privacy and communication efficiency—within a unified framework, rather than treating them as independent concerns. The use of common randomness shared among clients as a coordination mechanism without a trusted server represents a technically sophisticated solution that avoids the centralization of sensitive information while still enabling correlated behavior across participants.

The broader landscape of FL with differential privacy has been surveyed comprehensively, revealing a taxonomy of approaches that differ along several dimensions, including whether noise is added at the client level (local DP) or at the server level (central DP), the granularity at which privacy is enforced, and the specific aggregation protocols employed [arxiv_2404.18814]. Local differential privacy offers stronger privacy guarantees in the sense that even the server cannot observe unperturbed updates, but it typically requires more noise to achieve the same formal privacy budget, leading to greater utility loss. Central DP, by contrast, assumes a trusted aggregator and adds noise only after aggregation, which can preserve utility more effectively but requires trust in the server—a potentially problematic assumption in adversarial or cross-organizational FL deployments. The tension between these two paradigms reflects a broader challenge in FL: the system's threat model and trust assumptions profoundly shape which privacy mechanisms are appropriate, and practitioners must navigate these trade-offs carefully [arxiv_2404.18814].

Comparative analyses between federated and centralized machine learning further illuminate the performance gap that arises from FL's structural constraints. Even when data heterogeneity is controlled for, the inherent differences in how gradients are computed and aggregated in FL—across devices with varying computational capabilities, data volumes, and update frequencies—introduce additional sources of variance that do not exist in centralized training [arxiv_2202.01529]. These differences manifest in slower convergence, sensitivity to the number of local training epochs, and susceptibility to stragglers or dropped clients. Understanding these factors is essential for designing FL systems that are not only privacy-preserving but also practically competitive with centralized alternatives in terms of model quality and training efficiency [arxiv_2501.03477]. Collectively, the literature reviewed here underscores that FL is a multidimensional problem space in which privacy, communication, data heterogeneity, and model performance are deeply intertwined, and that progress on any single dimension must be evaluated in light of its effects on the others.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

The intersection of federated learning (FL) and privacy preservation has attracted sustained scholarly attention, driven by the recognition that distributing model training across clients does not, by itself, guarantee that sensitive information remains protected. The foundational architecture of FL, in which multiple clients—ranging from mobile devices to institutional edge nodes—collaboratively train a shared global model without centralizing raw data, was designed precisely to address data privacy concerns [arxiv_2504.17703]. Yet as the literature has matured, researchers have come to appreciate that this decentralized design offers only a partial privacy guarantee, and that additional mechanisms are required to defend against a range of inference and adversarial threats.

A recurring theme across the literature is the tension between privacy guarantees and model utility. Differential privacy (DP) has emerged as the dominant formal mechanism for strengthening privacy in FL, operating by injecting calibrated noise into client model updates before they are transmitted to the aggregating server [arxiv_2304.05127]. While DP provides mathematically rigorous privacy bounds, its application inevitably degrades convergence performance, because the noise introduced to obscure individual contributions also distorts the gradient signal that drives learning. This trade-off is not merely a practical inconvenience but a fundamental constraint that shapes the design space of private FL algorithms. Addressing it requires careful reasoning about how training hyperparameters interact with the privacy budget. Specifically, the number of local gradient steps performed on each client and the number of global communication rounds together determine how much useful information is extracted from the data before noise is applied, and optimizing these quantities jointly can substantially improve convergence bounds without relaxing the privacy guarantee [arxiv_2304.05127]. This insight—that the balance between local computation and global communication is itself a privacy-relevant design choice—represents a meaningful advance over earlier work that treated these hyperparameters as independent concerns.

A complementary line of inquiry focuses on the distinction between centralized and local differential privacy. Centralized DP, as typically applied in FL, relies on the assumption that the server aggregating model updates is trustworthy; it is the server that applies or verifies the noise mechanism, and clients must accept that their updates are handled faithfully [arxiv_2209.14086]. This assumption is frequently untenable in practice, particularly in cross-organizational or cross-jurisdictional deployments where no single party commands universal trust. Local differential privacy (LDP) addresses this limitation by requiring each client to privatize its own update before transmission, so that even a compromised or curious server cannot recover sensitive information from the received data [arxiv_2209.14086]. The privacy guarantee under LDP is therefore stronger in the threat model it accommodates, though it typically comes at the cost of greater noise magnitude and, consequently, slower or less accurate convergence. The work integrating LDP with momentum gradient descent in FL represents an effort to recover some of this lost efficiency by exploiting the variance-reduction properties of momentum-based optimization, thereby partially compensating for the additional noise introduced by local privatization [arxiv_2209.14086].

Beyond the formal privacy mechanisms, the literature identifies a broader set of vulnerabilities that persist even when DP is applied. FL remains susceptible to backdoor attacks, model poisoning, and membership inference attacks, in which malicious or compromised clients manipulate the aggregated model or attempt to reconstruct information about other participants' data from shared updates [arxiv_2104.06557]. These attack surfaces are distinct from the privacy leakage addressed by DP, and they motivate research into complementary defenses. One notable direction proposes incorporating causal reasoning into the FL framework as a means of simultaneously improving robustness and privacy [arxiv_2104.06557]. The causal approach seeks to identify and exploit stable, invariant relationships in the data rather than spurious correlations that may be specific to a particular client's distribution. By grounding the learned model in causal structure, this method aims to improve out-of-distribution generalization—a persistent challenge in FL due to the non-IID nature of client data—while also reducing the model's sensitivity to the idiosyncratic features of any individual client's dataset, thereby limiting the information that can be inferred about that client from the global model [arxiv_2104.06557].

The non-IID data problem deserves emphasis as a cross-cutting challenge that interacts with privacy concerns in non-trivial ways. When client data distributions are heterogeneous, the local models trained on individual clients diverge substantially from one another and from the global optimum, a phenomenon sometimes called client drift [arxiv_2504.17703]. This divergence complicates the application of DP because the noise required to mask individual contributions must be calibrated relative to the sensitivity of the updates, and high inter-client variance inflates this sensitivity. Strategies that reduce client drift—such as variance-reduction methods or control variates—thus have implications not only for convergence speed but also for the effective privacy cost of training [arxiv_2304.05127].

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals a field grappling with multiple, interacting dimensions of the privacy-utility trade-off. Formal mechanisms such as centralized and local DP provide rigorous guarantees but impose convergence costs that must be managed through careful hyperparameter optimization [arxiv_2304.05127] and algorithmic innovation [arxiv_2209.14086]. Structural approaches such as causal FL address complementary vulnerabilities—robustness to adversarial manipulation and poor out-of-distribution generalization—that DP alone cannot resolve [arxiv_2104.06557]. And overarching surveys situate these contributions within the broader FL lifecycle, highlighting the systemic nature of the challenges and the need for solutions that address privacy, security, and data heterogeneity in an integrated fashion [arxiv_2504.17703]. The convergence of these threads suggests that future progress will depend on frameworks capable of reasoning jointly about privacy budgets, optimization dynamics, and adversarial threat models rather than treating each concern in isolation.
