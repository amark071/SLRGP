# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference — evidence cluster A

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) built on the Transformer architecture has generated substantial scholarly interest in the challenge of efficient inference. While these models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of natural language processing tasks, their deployment in practical, resource-constrained environments remains a formidable obstacle. The core tension is well-articulated across the surveyed literature: LLMs achieve state-of-the-art performance at the cost of enormous memory footprints and computational demands that strain both hardware and economic resources. As [arxiv_2404.14294] identifies, the primary sources of inefficiency can be traced to three interrelated factors—the sheer scale of model parameters, the quadratic complexity of the attention mechanism with respect to sequence length, and the sequential nature of auto-regressive decoding. This diagnostic framing provides a useful foundation for understanding why inference optimization has emerged as one of the most active subfields in contemporary machine learning research.

Several comprehensive surveys have sought to map this rapidly evolving landscape. [arxiv_2402.09748] approaches the problem from an algorithmic perspective, drawing an explicit parallel between compression techniques developed for smaller neural networks and those now being adapted for LLMs. The authors organize the field into five canonical categories: quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, compact architecture design, and dynamic networks. Crucially, however, they emphasize that LLMs introduce qualitatively distinct challenges that complicate the straightforward application of these established methods. In particular, the prohibitive cost of fine-tuning or retraining LLMs after compression means that many classical compression pipelines, which assume a post-compression adjustment phase, are not directly transferable. This observation is echoed and extended by [arxiv_2408.03130], which similarly surveys quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, and architectural optimizations, but places particular emphasis on the practical challenges and real-world deployment considerations that accompany each technique. Together, these two works underscore that the field is not merely applying known solutions to a larger problem but is instead confronting a set of constraints that demand genuinely novel methodological thinking.

The taxonomic frameworks proposed across the surveyed literature reveal both consensus and meaningful divergence in how researchers conceptualize the optimization space. [arxiv_2404.14294] introduces a three-level taxonomy—data-level, model-level, and system-level optimization—that is notably broader in scope than the algorithm-centric categorizations offered by [arxiv_2402.09748] and [arxiv_2408.03130]. By incorporating data-level strategies, such as prompt compression and input preprocessing, alongside model-level techniques and system-level scheduling, [arxiv_2404.14294] captures a wider range of interventions that affect inference efficiency without necessarily modifying model weights. This inclusive framing reflects a growing recognition that efficiency gains can be achieved at multiple points in the inference pipeline, not solely through changes to the model itself. [arxiv_2401.02038] adopts yet another organizational lens, situating inference optimization within the broader lifecycle of LLM development that spans data preprocessing, training architecture, and deployment. On the inference side specifically, [arxiv_2401.02038] highlights model compression, parallel computation, memory scheduling, and structural optimization as the principal areas of concern, a decomposition that aligns closely with the system-level considerations foregrounded by [arxiv_2404.14294].

A recurring theme across all four works is the centrality of quantization as a compression strategy. Quantization reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, thereby decreasing memory requirements and potentially accelerating arithmetic operations. [arxiv_2402.09748] and [arxiv_2408.03130] both treat quantization as a primary technique, with the latter providing detailed discussion of the practical trade-offs between post-training quantization and quantization-aware training in the context of very large models. The challenge, as both surveys note, is that LLMs exhibit sensitivity to aggressive quantization in ways that smaller models do not, partly because of the presence of outlier activation values that disrupt uniform quantization schemes. Pruning, which removes redundant weights or structures from a model, is similarly treated as a foundational technique, though [arxiv_2402.09748] notes that unstructured pruning, while effective at reducing parameter counts, does not always translate into practical inference speedups on modern hardware without specialized support.

Knowledge distillation, wherein a smaller student model is trained to replicate the behavior of a larger teacher model, occupies a somewhat different position in the LLM context. [arxiv_2402.09748] observes that the scale of LLMs makes the distillation process itself computationally expensive, and that the emergent capabilities of very large models are not always faithfully transferred to smaller surrogates. [arxiv_2408.03130] reinforces this point, noting that distillation for LLMs often requires careful selection of intermediate representations and loss functions to preserve task-relevant behaviors. Both works suggest that distillation is most effective when combined with other compression strategies rather than applied in isolation.

Beyond compression, the surveyed literature also addresses system-level and architectural optimizations that can yield substantial efficiency improvements without altering model weights. [arxiv_2401.02038] discusses parallel computation strategies, including tensor parallelism and pipeline parallelism, as essential tools for distributing inference workloads across multiple devices. Memory scheduling techniques, such as key-value cache management, are highlighted as particularly important given the memory-intensive nature of auto-regressive generation. [arxiv_2404.14294] similarly addresses attention mechanism optimizations, including sparse attention and linear attention variants, as responses to the quadratic complexity bottleneck identified in its problem analysis.

Taken together, these four surveys paint a coherent picture of a field grappling with the tension between model capability and deployment feasibility. The consensus view is that no single technique is sufficient to address the full scope of inference inefficiency; rather, practical deployment of LLMs typically requires a combination of model-level compression, architectural modification, and system-level engineering. The diversity of taxonomic frameworks across [arxiv_2402.09748], [arxiv_2404.14294], [arxiv_2401.02038], and [arxiv_2408.03130] reflects the multidimensional nature of the problem and suggests that the field is still in the process of consolidating its conceptual vocabulary. What is clear, however, is that efficient inference has become a first-order concern in LLM research, driven by the practical imperative to make these powerful models accessible beyond the confines of large-scale data center infrastructure.

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference — evidence cluster B

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the central challenges in contemporary artificial intelligence research, driven by the tension between the remarkable capabilities of these models and the substantial computational resources they demand. A broad family of compression and optimization techniques has been developed to address this tension, spanning weight pruning, quantization, knowledge distillation, architectural search, and key-value cache management. Collectively, these approaches target two interrelated objectives: reducing the memory footprint required to store and serve LLMs, and accelerating inference throughput to meet the latency constraints imposed by real-world production environments.

Weight pruning and quantization represent perhaps the most widely adopted strategies for compressing neural language models. The intuition underlying both approaches is that modern LLMs are substantially over-parameterized, containing considerable redundancy that can be exploited without catastrophic loss of capability [arxiv_2409.17372]. Quantization reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, thereby shrinking memory consumption and enabling faster arithmetic operations on hardware that supports low-precision computation. Pruning, by contrast, zeroes out or removes individual weights, attention heads, or entire layers, producing sparse representations that can be executed more efficiently when paired with appropriate runtime support. The practical value of combining these two techniques is illustrated by work on fast transformer inference on CPUs, where hardware-aware pruning is applied in conjunction with quantization and knowledge distillation to produce a compressed DistilBERT variant that achieves high throughput while preserving accuracy [arxiv_2211.07715]. Crucially, that work also demonstrates that realizing the theoretical gains of sparsity and quantization requires co-designed inference runtime engines with optimized kernels for sparse and quantized operators, underscoring that algorithmic compression alone is insufficient without corresponding systems-level support [arxiv_2211.07715].

Knowledge distillation complements pruning and quantization by transferring the behavioral knowledge encoded in a large teacher model into a smaller student model, often yielding better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs than compression of the teacher in isolation. When distillation is integrated into a unified pipeline alongside pruning and quantization, as demonstrated in the fast DistilBERT framework, the resulting models can satisfy stringent industrial latency requirements that would otherwise preclude the use of transformer-based models in production [arxiv_2211.07715]. This pipeline perspective, in which multiple compression techniques are applied sequentially and synergistically rather than in isolation, has become increasingly influential in the field.

A complementary line of inquiry questions whether weight-level optimization is sufficient on its own, arguing that the architecture of the compressed model deserves equal attention. Most conventional compression methods concentrate on optimizing the values of existing weights while leaving the underlying network topology fixed, thereby potentially missing more efficient structural configurations [arxiv_2409.17372]. Neural Architecture Search (NAS) offers a principled mechanism for exploring the space of possible architectures, but traditional NAS methods were designed for models with far fewer parameters than contemporary LLMs, and their computational complexity scales poorly to the billion-parameter regime [arxiv_2409.17372]. To address this limitation, training-free architecture search frameworks have been proposed that identify optimal subnets inheriting weights directly from a pre-trained LLM, preserving the fundamental capabilities of the original model while achieving inference acceleration without the prohibitive cost of retraining from scratch [arxiv_2409.17372]. This training-free paradigm represents a significant departure from earlier NAS approaches and reflects a broader trend toward leveraging the already-learned representations in large pre-trained models rather than discarding them during compression.

The integration of low-rank representations with NAS techniques offers yet another avenue for simultaneously compressing and fine-tuning LLMs. Low-rank adapters, originally developed for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, have been shown to combine naturally with weight-sharing super-networks, a NAS strategy in which a single over-complete network encodes a large population of candidate architectures that share weights during search [arxiv_2501.16372]. By jointly optimizing the low-rank structure of adapters and the architectural configuration of the resulting subnet, this combined methodology yields models with reduced memory footprints and faster inference times while remaining amenable to task-specific adaptation [arxiv_2501.16372]. The authors argue that such synergistic approaches have the potential to democratize access to LLMs by making deployment viable in resource-constrained environments where neither full fine-tuning nor uncompressed inference is feasible [arxiv_2501.16372]. Compared to the training-free subnet search of [arxiv_2409.17372], the low-rank NAS approach of [arxiv_2501.16372] accepts some additional training cost in exchange for greater flexibility in adapting the compressed model to downstream tasks, reflecting a fundamental trade-off between adaptation quality and search efficiency.

Beyond weight-level and architecture-level compression, the management of the key-value (KV) cache during autoregressive inference has emerged as a distinct and increasingly critical bottleneck. As context lengths grow, the memory required to store KV caches grows exponentially, imposing severe constraints on batch size, sequence length, and overall inference scalability [arxiv_2508.06297]. A taxonomy of KV cache optimization strategies has been systematically examined in recent survey work, encompassing selective token retention strategies that discard less important cached entries, quantization of cached key and value tensors to reduce their memory footprint, and attention compression techniques that restructure the attention mechanism itself to reduce the volume of information that must be cached [arxiv_2508.06297]. Each of these strategies involves distinct trade-offs between memory savings, inference speed, and model accuracy, and their applicability varies across deployment scenarios and model architectures [arxiv_2508.06297]. Importantly, compatibility issues with different model families and the challenge of maintaining accuracy under aggressive cache compression remain open problems that limit the practical adoption of these methods [arxiv_2508.06297].

Taken together, the literature reveals a multi-dimensional landscape of efficiency techniques that operate at different levels of the inference stack, from individual weight values and numerical precision, through architectural topology and adapter structure, to the dynamic management of intermediate activations during generation. The most effective practical systems tend to combine insights from several of these dimensions simultaneously, as exemplified by the integrated pruning, distillation, and quantization pipeline of [arxiv_2211.07715], while emerging research continues to push the frontier by rethinking the architecture search problem [arxiv_2409.17372] and by developing principled approaches to KV cache compression [arxiv_2508.06297] and low-rank architectural optimization [arxiv_2501.16372].

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation — evidence cluster A

The efficient inference of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary natural language processing research. As these models scale to billions of parameters, the computational and memory demands associated with both training and deployment have grown substantially, motivating a diverse body of work aimed at reducing overhead without sacrificing model quality. This subsection reviews the foundational considerations and principal technical strategies that define the current landscape of efficient LLM inference, drawing on recent contributions spanning model size trade-offs, quantization, key-value cache optimization, and broader algorithmic efficiency frameworks.

A natural starting point for understanding efficient inference is the relationship between model size and computational cost during training, which has direct implications for the quality of models ultimately deployed. The counterintuitive finding reported by [arxiv_2002.11794] challenges the assumption that smaller models are inherently more efficient. Their empirical investigation of Transformer models across self-supervised pretraining and high-resource machine translation tasks demonstrates that, while smaller models execute faster per iteration, wider and deeper models converge in significantly fewer steps. Crucially, this acceleration in convergence typically outpaces the additional per-iteration cost of larger models, meaning that the most compute-efficient training strategy is to train extremely large models but halt after relatively few iterations. This insight reframes the efficiency problem: the goal is not merely to minimize per-step cost but to minimize total compute required to reach a target performance level. The implication for inference is significant, as it justifies the deployment of large, high-quality models that have been trained efficiently, provided that inference-time compression and optimization techniques are subsequently applied. This "train large, then compress" paradigm establishes a conceptual bridge between training efficiency and inference efficiency, situating downstream compression methods as necessary complements to large-scale pretraining [arxiv_2002.11794].

Among the most widely adopted inference optimization strategies is post-training quantization (PTQ), which reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations to decrease memory footprint and accelerate computation. [arxiv_2408.08554] addresses two persistent challenges in this domain: the performance degradation associated with low-bit quantization, and the difficulty of efficiently executing quantized matrix operations across varying precisions on GPU hardware, which typically supports only a limited set of integer computing unit types. Their proposed framework, ABQ-LLM, introduces an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm that achieves strong performance across diverse quantization configurations while enabling efficient arbitrary-precision inference on GPU architectures. This contribution is notable because it decouples quantization granularity from hardware constraints, allowing practitioners to select precision levels that balance accuracy and speed without being restricted to the integer formats natively supported by existing hardware. The work thus advances the practical deployability of quantized LLMs, addressing a gap between algorithmic flexibility and hardware-level execution that has historically limited the real-world gains of low-bit quantization schemes [arxiv_2408.08554].

Complementing quantization-based approaches, memory management during the autoregressive generation process represents another critical bottleneck for LLM inference. During generation, Transformer models maintain a key-value (KV) cache that stores intermediate attention states for all previously processed tokens, enabling efficient computation of attention over long contexts. However, the memory consumed by this cache grows linearly with sequence length, rapidly becoming a primary constraint on throughput and batch size. [arxiv_2404.15949] addresses this problem through CORM, a KV cache eviction policy that dynamically retains only the most essential key-value pairs during inference. The method is grounded in two empirical observations about Transformer attention behavior: adjacent tokens tend to exhibit high similarity in their query vectors, and the attention computation for a given query can rely on information from only a small fraction of preceding queries. By exploiting these structural regularities, CORM substantially reduces the memory footprint of the KV cache without requiring model retraining or architectural modification. This training-free, plug-in nature distinguishes CORM from approaches that require changes to the model itself, making it broadly applicable across deployed LLMs [arxiv_2404.15949]. Compared to quantization methods such as ABQ-LLM [arxiv_2408.08554], which target weight and activation precision, KV cache eviction strategies like CORM operate on the dynamic memory generated during inference, suggesting that the two approaches are largely orthogonal and potentially complementary in a unified inference optimization pipeline.

A more comprehensive treatment of efficiency across both training and inference phases is offered by [arxiv_2405.11704], which systematically analyzes the internal structure of large-scale language models and identifies efficiency bottlenecks at multiple levels of the computational stack. The work examines how Transformer architectures and their derivatives constrain computing efficiency while capturing long-range dependencies, and evaluates the contributions of adaptive optimization algorithms such as AdamW, massively parallel computing techniques, and mixed-precision training strategies to accelerating convergence and reducing memory usage during training. On the inference side, the paper reviews advances in model compression, including quantization, pruning, and related strategies, providing a unified analytical lens through which the trade-offs among these techniques can be assessed [arxiv_2405.11704]. This breadth of coverage situates individual techniques within a larger efficiency ecosystem, reinforcing the view advanced by [arxiv_2002.11794] that training and inference efficiency must be considered jointly rather than in isolation.

Taken together, these works illuminate the multi-dimensional nature of the efficient inference problem. The decision to train large models and subsequently compress them [arxiv_2002.11794] motivates the need for robust post-training optimization methods, among which arbitrary-bit quantization [arxiv_2408.08554] and dynamic KV cache management [arxiv_2404.15949] represent complementary and practically significant advances. The broader analytical framework provided by [arxiv_2405.11704] underscores that no single technique is sufficient on its own; rather, efficient LLM inference requires coordinated optimization across model size selection, numerical precision, memory management, and algorithmic design.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation — evidence cluster B

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) has emerged as one of the most pressing challenges in contemporary artificial intelligence research. As model parameter counts reach into the hundreds of billions, the computational and memory costs associated with inference have become prohibitive in real-world scenarios, motivating a diverse body of work aimed at reducing latency, memory footprint, and overall serving costs without sacrificing output quality [arxiv_2407.09111]. This subsection reviews the principal families of techniques that have been proposed to address these challenges, examining their underlying mechanisms, relative strengths, and the ways in which they interact or complement one another.

A foundational observation motivating much of this literature is that the standard autoregressive decoding process in transformer-based LLMs is inherently sequential: each new token can only be generated after all preceding tokens have been produced, creating a bottleneck that underutilizes the parallel processing capabilities of modern AI accelerators. Speculative decoding has emerged as a compelling strategy to circumvent this limitation. The core idea is to employ a smaller, faster speculative model to draft a sequence of candidate tokens, which are then verified in parallel by the larger target model, yielding a net speedup when the acceptance rate of drafted tokens is sufficiently high [arxiv_2503.15921]. While this paradigm has demonstrated meaningful acceleration in controlled settings, early implementations relied on a single, homogeneous small speculative model (SSM) applied uniformly across all inference requests. This design choice is suboptimal because requests vary considerably in their linguistic complexity and domain characteristics, meaning that a single SSM may speculate well on some inputs while performing poorly on others. SPIN addresses this limitation by introducing a heterogeneous pool of SSMs, coupled with a learning-based selection algorithm that dynamically assigns the most appropriate speculative model to each request without requiring prior knowledge of the request distribution [arxiv_2503.15921]. Beyond SSM selection, SPIN also provides robust support for batched inference and holistic optimization of both the speculation and verification phases, recognizing that prior work had tended to optimize these stages in isolation [arxiv_2503.15921]. This systems-level perspective—treating inference as an end-to-end pipeline rather than a collection of independent components—represents an important methodological advance.

Memory management constitutes a second major axis of research in efficient LLM inference. Transformer architectures cache the key and value representations computed during the attention mechanism in order to avoid redundant computation across decoding steps, but this key-value (KV) cache grows linearly with both sequence length and batch size, rapidly exhausting the high-bandwidth memory available on GPU accelerators when generating long sequences [arxiv_2406.19707]. InfiniGen addresses this challenge through a dynamic KV cache management framework specifically designed for long-text generation scenarios [arxiv_2406.19707]. The central insight exploited by InfiniGen is that only a small subset of previously computed tokens exerts a dominant influence on each subsequent attention computation. By performing a lightweight rehearsal using the inputs of the current transformer layer together with a portion of the query weights and key cache of the next layer, InfiniGen can speculatively identify which tokens are likely to be important and prefetch only their KV entries from slower off-chip memory into on-chip memory ahead of time [arxiv_2406.19707]. This offloading-aware design allows the system to serve inference requests with sequence lengths that would otherwise exceed GPU memory capacity, effectively decoupling the practical context length from the physical memory constraints of the accelerator. The approach is notable for its synergy with existing offloading-based inference systems, suggesting that it can be integrated into production serving stacks with relatively modest engineering effort.

Quantization represents a third major strategy for reducing the resource demands of LLM inference. By representing model weights and activations in lower-precision numerical formats—most commonly 8-bit or 4-bit integers rather than 32-bit or 16-bit floating point—quantization can substantially reduce both the memory footprint of model parameters and the computational cost of matrix multiplications, which dominate the arithmetic workload of transformer inference [arxiv_2407.09111]. However, a persistent tension in the quantization literature concerns the relationship between the deployment phase and the fine-tuning phase. Most quantization techniques are designed with deployment in mind and assume that the model has already been fully trained; applying further fine-tuning to a quantized model is non-trivial because the discrete, low-precision weight representation is not straightforwardly compatible with gradient-based optimization [arxiv_2305.14152]. The Parameter-Efficient and Quantization-aware Adaptation (PEQA) method directly confronts this tension by combining parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with sub-4-bit integer quantization [arxiv_2305.14152]. Rather than updating the quantized weight values themselves, PEQA updates only the quantization scales associated with each weight tensor, a design choice that keeps the number of trainable parameters extremely small while still enabling meaningful task-specific adaptation. This approach is complementary to existing PEFT methods such as LoRA in that it operates directly on quantized representations, making it applicable in memory-constrained environments where even storing full-precision adapter weights would be burdensome [arxiv_2305.14152].

Taken together, these lines of research reflect a broader recognition that efficient LLM inference is a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated advances at the algorithmic, systems, and hardware levels [arxiv_2407.09111]. Speculative decoding techniques such as SPIN target the latency bottleneck introduced by sequential token generation [arxiv_2503.15921]; KV cache management frameworks such as InfiniGen address the memory bandwidth and capacity constraints that limit throughput for long-context workloads [arxiv_2406.19707]; and quantization-aware adaptation methods such as PEQA reduce the parameter storage costs that make fine-tuning and deployment of large models expensive [arxiv_2305.14152]. Importantly, these approaches are not mutually exclusive: a production inference system might simultaneously employ quantized weights to reduce memory pressure, dynamic KV cache offloading to support long sequences, and speculative decoding to improve token generation throughput. Understanding how these techniques interact—and where their combined benefits may be subadditive or even counterproductive—remains an important open question for the field.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

The efficient management of key-value (KV) caches has emerged as one of the most consequential research directions in large language model inference, given that the memory overhead associated with storing attention keys and values across transformer layers constitutes a primary bottleneck in high-throughput deployment scenarios. As models grow deeper and sequence lengths increase, the cumulative memory consumed by KV caches can rival or exceed that of the model parameters themselves, motivating a diverse array of strategies that seek to reduce this footprint without substantially degrading model quality.

One prominent line of work addresses the problem by reconsidering which layers actually require full KV cache storage. The Layer-Condensed KV Cache approach [arxiv_2405.10637] proceeds from the observation that deep language models maintain KV caches across every layer, yet not all layers contribute equally to representational fidelity. By computing and caching key-value pairs for only a small subset of layers and reusing or approximating the remainder, this method achieves memory savings that translate directly into throughput gains. Empirical results reported in that work demonstrate up to 26× higher throughput relative to standard transformer inference, a figure that is particularly striking because it is achieved while maintaining competitive performance on language modeling benchmarks and downstream tasks. Crucially, the authors note that their approach is orthogonal to other memory-saving techniques, meaning it can be composed with complementary methods to yield further improvements [arxiv_2405.10637]. This composability is an important practical property, since real deployment pipelines often combine multiple optimization strategies simultaneously.

A related but distinct challenge arises from the dynamic growth of the KV cache during autoregressive generation over long sequences. ALISA addresses this by exploiting the sparsity structure inherent in attention computations [arxiv_2403.17312]. The core insight is that KV caching already converts the quadratic-complexity attention computation into linear-complexity memory accesses during the decode phase, but this benefit is undermined when the cache grows large enough to introduce I/O bottlenecks or trigger out-of-memory errors, particularly on resource-constrained hardware such as a single commodity GPU. By selectively retaining only the KV entries that are likely to receive significant attention weight—leveraging the empirical observation that attention distributions are often sparse—ALISA reduces the effective cache size without discarding information that materially influences output quality [arxiv_2403.17312]. Comparing ALISA with the Layer-Condensed KV Cache [arxiv_2405.10637], both methods reduce the total volume of stored KV data, but they do so along orthogonal dimensions: the former prunes along the token sequence axis by exploiting attention sparsity, while the latter prunes along the layer axis by condensing which layers maintain caches at all. This distinction suggests that the two approaches could in principle be combined, consistent with the composability claim made in [arxiv_2405.10637].

Beyond cache management, another major axis of inference optimization concerns the precision at which model weights and activations are represented. Quantization reduces the bit-width of stored values, thereby decreasing both memory footprint and the bandwidth required to move data between memory and compute units. This is especially relevant for CPU-based deployment, where memory bandwidth is often the binding constraint. The work on efficient LLM inference on CPUs [arxiv_2311.00502] proposes an INT4 weight-only quantization flow coupled with a highly optimized runtime featuring custom kernels tuned for CPU architectures. By quantizing weights to four-bit integers while retaining higher precision for activations during computation, the method achieves substantial reductions in memory capacity requirements and bandwidth pressure. Experiments spanning popular models including LLaMA, LLaMA 2, and GPT-NeoX demonstrate that this approach enables practical inference on CPU hardware, which is significant because CPUs are far more widely available than high-end GPUs in many deployment contexts [arxiv_2311.00502]. The public release of the implementation further lowers the barrier to adoption.

A more integrated perspective on model compression for inference is offered by the framework combining GPTQ-based quantization, low-rank adaptation, and knowledge distillation [arxiv_2601.09865]. This pipeline targets edge deployment scenarios where computational, memory, and energy budgets are severely constrained. The approach addresses three interrelated challenges: obtaining task-specific training data through a data distillation process, fine-tuning the compressed model using LoRA to recover task performance, and applying GPTQ quantization to reduce model size. Knowledge distillation via Kullback-Leibler divergence is used to align the compressed model's output distribution with that of the original, while Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and the Muon optimizer are employed to improve the efficiency of the training process itself. The reported outcome is up to 2× memory compression—for instance, reducing a 6 GB model to 3 GB—while preserving or enhancing task-specific performance [arxiv_2601.09865]. Comparing this with the CPU inference work [arxiv_2311.00502], both rely on quantization as a core mechanism, but the former embeds quantization within a broader distillation and fine-tuning pipeline oriented toward task-specific edge deployment, whereas the latter focuses on general-purpose inference efficiency through runtime and kernel optimization. The distinction reflects a broader tension in the field between methods that treat compression as a post-hoc engineering step and those that integrate it with task-aware training.

Taken together, these works illustrate that efficient inference for large language models is not a single problem but a constellation of interrelated challenges spanning memory capacity, memory bandwidth, computational complexity, and hardware heterogeneity. KV cache reduction strategies such as layer condensation [arxiv_2405.10637] and sparsity-aware caching [arxiv_2403.17312] address the memory growth that accompanies long-context generation, while quantization-based approaches [arxiv_2311.00502, arxiv_2601.09865] target the static memory footprint of model parameters and the bandwidth costs of weight loading. The convergence of these strategies, and their noted orthogonality and composability, suggests that the most capable deployment systems will likely integrate multiple techniques drawn from each of these directions simultaneously.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

The memory and computational demands of large language model (LLM) inference have motivated a rich body of research targeting multiple layers of the system stack, from architectural redesign and draft-model training to hardware-level memory management. A central challenge is that the autoregressive decoding process is inherently memory-bound: each forward pass generates a single token while repeatedly loading billions of parameters and an ever-growing key-value (KV) cache from off-chip memory, resulting in low hardware utilization and poor throughput [arxiv_2403.00858]. Addressing this bottleneck requires coordinated innovations across model design, caching strategies, and accelerator architecture, and the literature reviewed here reflects precisely that breadth of concern.

One of the most prominent algorithmic responses to the memory-bound nature of autoregressive generation is speculative decoding, in which a small, fast draft model proposes multiple candidate tokens that a larger target model then verifies in a single parallel forward pass. Because verification is parallelizable, the scheme can yield substantial wall-clock speedups without altering the output distribution of the target model. A practical obstacle, however, is that suitable draft models are frequently absent from modern open-source LLM families. To close this gap, researchers have proposed dedicated training frameworks that align a compact draft model directly to a chat-fine-tuned target. One such framework trains Llama 2 Chat Drafter 115M—a model representing only 1.64% of the parameter count of Llama 2 Chat 7B—through a pipeline of pretraining, distillation-based dataset generation, and supervised fine-tuning [arxiv_2403.00858]. The explicit alignment to a chat-capable target is significant: because instruction-tuned models exhibit token distributions that diverge from those of base models, a draft trained on generic text corpora may propose tokens that the target frequently rejects, negating the latency benefits of speculation. By generating the distillation dataset from the target model itself, the framework ensures distributional compatibility and thereby sustains high acceptance rates during inference [arxiv_2403.00858]. This work illustrates a broader principle: inference acceleration techniques are most effective when the components involved—draft and target, or cache and model—are co-designed rather than assembled post hoc.

While speculative decoding addresses the per-step compute and memory-access cost, a complementary line of research targets the KV cache itself, which grows linearly with sequence length and can dominate memory consumption during long-context inference. A comprehensive survey of KV cache management strategies organizes the literature into token-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations [arxiv_2412.19442]. Token-level strategies encompass KV cache selection, budget allocation, merging, quantization, and low-rank decomposition, each of which seeks to reduce the number or precision of stored key-value vectors without substantially degrading attention quality. Model-level strategies involve architectural modifications—such as grouped-query attention or changes to the number of attention heads—that reduce the cache footprint by design. System-level strategies address scheduling, paging, and offloading policies that govern how cache entries are allocated and evicted across the memory hierarchy [arxiv_2412.19442]. The taxonomy is useful because it reveals that no single technique is universally dominant; rather, the optimal combination depends on the deployment context, including sequence length distribution, batch size, and available hardware resources.

Architectural approaches to KV cache management offer a particularly clean solution by bounding cache growth at the model level rather than relying on post-hoc eviction heuristics. The Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) exemplifies this direction by introducing a predefined KV cache capacity constraint directly into the transformer's decode-only architecture [arxiv_2411.15785]. Rather than accumulating key-value vectors indefinitely, the BCT dynamically updates the stored sequences so that the cache length never exceeds a fixed budget. Experimental results demonstrate that this bounded design significantly reduces memory usage while preserving inference quality and system throughput [arxiv_2411.15785]. Compared with token-level eviction strategies surveyed in [arxiv_2412.19442], the BCT approach has the advantage of providing hard memory guarantees that are predictable at deployment time, which is valuable for latency-sensitive production systems. The trade-off is that the capacity constraint must be chosen at training time, potentially limiting adaptability to workloads with highly variable context lengths.

Hardware-level innovations represent a further frontier, targeting the memory controller itself rather than the model or its cache management policy. One recent proposal augments the on-chip memory controller of AI accelerators with two complementary capabilities: lossless block compression of model weights and KV cache entries using algorithms such as LZ4 and ZSTD, and context-dependent dynamic quantization whose energy and bandwidth consumption scale with the actual information content of each block [arxiv_2503.18869]. The lossless compression component is particularly noteworthy because it reduces effective memory capacity and bandwidth demands without any degradation in inference quality—a property that distinguishes it from lossy techniques such as weight pruning or fixed-precision quantization, which trade accuracy for efficiency [arxiv_2503.18869]. By situating compression logic in the memory controller rather than in the model itself, this design is orthogonal to software-level optimizations and can therefore be combined with techniques such as speculative decoding [arxiv_2403.00858] or bounded-cache architectures [arxiv_2411.15785] for compounding gains. The dynamic quantization feature further allows the system to exploit the empirical observation that many weight and activation tensors are compressible to lower precision in practice, even when a fixed low-precision format would be too aggressive [arxiv_2503.18869].

Taken together, these works reveal a multi-level optimization landscape for efficient LLM inference. Speculative decoding with aligned draft models [arxiv_2403.00858] reduces the effective number of full-model forward passes; bounded-cache architectures [arxiv_2411.15785] and the broader family of KV cache management strategies surveyed in [arxiv_2412.19442] constrain the memory footprint of the attention mechanism; and compression-aware memory controller designs [arxiv_2503.18869] reduce the bandwidth and energy cost of every memory transaction. A recurring theme across all four perspectives is that efficiency gains are most robust when the optimization is tightly coupled to the specific characteristics of the target workload and hardware platform, whether through distributional alignment of draft and target models, capacity-aware training of bounded-cache transformers, or context-sensitive compression policies embedded in accelerator silicon.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The intersection of model compression and parameter-efficient fine-tuning has emerged as one of the most productive areas of research in making large language models (LLMs) practical for real-world deployment. As LLMs continue to scale in parameter count, the costs associated with both training and inference have grown correspondingly, motivating a rich body of work that seeks to reduce these costs without sacrificing model quality. The literature in this space broadly addresses two interrelated challenges: how to compress pre-trained models so that inference becomes computationally tractable, and how to adapt those compressed models to downstream tasks without incurring prohibitive training overhead.

Quantization stands out as one of the most widely adopted compression strategies, reducing the numerical precision of model weights and activations to lower memory consumption and accelerate arithmetic operations. Early approaches to quantization for LLMs largely relied on post-training quantization (PTQ), which applies precision reduction to an already-trained model without any gradient-based updates. While PTQ is attractive for its simplicity and low computational cost, it introduces accuracy degradation that becomes increasingly difficult to recover as bit-widths decrease. This limitation has been a central motivation for subsequent research. AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858] represents an early and influential attempt to address this gap by combining PTQ with parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Specifically, AlphaTuning employs binary-coding quantization, which factorizes full-precision parameters into binary parameters and a separate set of scaling factors. During the adaptation phase, the binary values are frozen while only the scaling factors are updated, thereby enabling task-specific recovery of accuracy at a fraction of the cost of full fine-tuning. This design reflects a broader insight that not all quantized parameters contribute equally to downstream performance, and that selective updating of a small subset can meaningfully restore model quality.

Building on this line of thinking, more recent work has sought to move beyond PTQ entirely by integrating quantization directly into the training loop. L4Q [arxiv_2402.04902] proposes combining Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), a widely used parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that introduces trainable low-rank matrices into the model's weight updates. The motivation for this combination is straightforward: PTQ-based approaches, including AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858], face inherent limitations in recovering accuracy loss because the quantization error is not accounted for during the optimization process. By contrast, QAT exposes the model to quantization noise during training, allowing gradients to flow through a more realistic approximation of the inference-time computation. L4Q further introduces a memory-optimized layer design that makes this joint training feasible without exceeding the memory budgets typical of academic or resource-constrained settings. Taken together, AlphaTuning and L4Q illustrate a clear progression in the field: from post-hoc correction of quantization errors through selective fine-tuning, toward proactive integration of quantization constraints into the adaptation process itself.

While quantization addresses the precision of individual parameters, a complementary line of research examines the structural redundancy of transformer architectures and asks whether entire components can be removed or bypassed without meaningful loss of capability. The empirical investigation reported in [arxiv_2404.05741] explores this question directly, demonstrating that skipping latter attention sublayers in transformer-based LLMs constitutes an effective form of model compression. The rationale is that attention layers in the later portions of the network tend to be computationally expensive yet functionally redundant, contributing little to the model's representational capacity. By simply omitting these layers during inference, the authors report a 21% speed increase in single-token generation for Llama 2 7B, while simultaneously observing improvements on several common benchmarks. This counterintuitive finding—that removing layers can improve benchmark performance—suggests that some attention sublayers may introduce noise or overfitting artifacts rather than useful computation. Compared to quantization-based approaches, layer skipping is architecturally simpler and requires no changes to numerical precision or weight representation, but it operates at a coarser granularity and may be less amenable to fine-grained accuracy-efficiency trade-offs.

A more systems-oriented perspective on compression is offered by KV Pareto [arxiv_2512.01953], which focuses specifically on the memory bottlenecks that arise during long-context inference. As sequence lengths grow, the key-value (KV) cache—which stores intermediate attention states to avoid redundant computation—grows linearly, quickly becoming a dominant source of memory pressure. KV Pareto addresses this by framing the optimization problem as a multi-dimensional trade-off, jointly considering KV cache quantization, chunked prefill, and model weight quantization via AWQ. Rather than optimizing each technique in isolation, the framework systematically maps Pareto-optimal configurations across multiple LLM architectures including Qwen, Llama, and Mistral, evaluating combinations of integer quantization schemes at varying bit-widths and granularities. This systems-level framing is notably distinct from the parameter-efficient fine-tuning perspective of AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858] and L4Q [arxiv_2402.04902], which are primarily concerned with training-time efficiency and accuracy recovery. KV Pareto instead treats compression as a deployment-time engineering problem, seeking configurations that minimize total memory consumption subject to task accuracy constraints. The identification of model-specific Pareto frontiers is particularly valuable for edge deployment scenarios, where hardware constraints are fixed and the practitioner must select the best achievable configuration rather than design a new training procedure.

Across these works, a coherent picture emerges of the multi-faceted nature of efficient inference for LLMs. Quantization-aware fine-tuning methods such as AlphaTuning [arxiv_2210.03858] and L4Q [arxiv_2402.04902] address the tension between compression and accuracy at the level of individual parameters and training dynamics. Structural pruning approaches like layer skipping [arxiv_2404.05741] offer complementary gains by exploiting architectural redundancy. And systems-level frameworks like KV Pareto [arxiv_2512.01953] situate these techniques within the broader context of memory management and deployment constraints. Together, they underscore that no single technique is sufficient, and that the most effective strategies for efficient inference will likely require coordinated optimization across multiple dimensions simultaneously.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) at scale has generated a rich body of research addressing the computational and memory bottlenecks that arise during inference. As LLMs become embedded in an increasingly diverse range of applications, the community has responded with a multifaceted set of optimization strategies spanning hardware-software co-design, memory compression, and systematic evaluation frameworks. Understanding how these contributions relate to one another is essential for situating ongoing work within the broader landscape of efficient LLM inference.

A foundational challenge in LLM inference is that the autoregressive decoding process requires storing intermediate key and value (KV) activations for every token in the context, a structure known as the KV cache. While this mechanism avoids redundant computation, it introduces severe memory pressure, particularly as batch sizes, context lengths, and model sizes grow. As noted in the literature, the KV cache can quickly become the dominant contributor to GPU memory usage and the primary bottleneck of inference latency [arxiv_2405.03917]. This observation has motivated a substantial line of work on KV cache compression, with quantization emerging as one of the most prominent techniques.

Quantization-based approaches to KV cache compression differ significantly in their assumptions about the structure of the activations they compress. A naive uniform quantization strategy applies the same bit-width reduction to all entries, but this approach tends to incur high approximation errors, particularly for entries with outlier magnitudes. GEAR addresses this limitation by proposing a hybrid compression framework that first applies quantization to the majority of entries sharing similar magnitudes, pushing them to ultra-low precision, and then employs a complementary mechanism to handle the residual approximation error introduced by this aggressive compression [arxiv_2403.05527]. The key insight motivating GEAR is that the autoregressive decoding process compounds errors across steps, meaning that even small per-step approximation errors can accumulate into critical deviations in model generation quality. By targeting near-lossless compression rather than simply maximizing compression ratio, GEAR attempts to preserve generation fidelity while still achieving substantial memory savings.

A different perspective on the structure of KV activations is taken by Coupled Quantization (CQ), which observes that distinct channels within a key or value activation embedding are not independent but are instead highly inter-dependent [arxiv_2405.03917]. Specifically, the joint entropy of multiple channels grows at a slower rate than the sum of their marginal entropies, implying that treating channels independently wastes representational capacity. CQ exploits this inter-channel dependency by coupling multiple channels together during quantization, encoding the activations in a more information-efficient manner. This approach is particularly motivated by the failure of existing methods at very low bit widths, where the assumption of channel independence becomes especially costly. Comparing GEAR and CQ, both works are motivated by the inadequacy of uniform quantization, but they diagnose the problem differently: GEAR focuses on the heterogeneity of entry magnitudes within a single channel and the error accumulation dynamics of autoregressive decoding, while CQ focuses on the statistical dependencies across channels. Together, these works suggest that effective KV cache compression requires attending to the internal structure of activations along multiple dimensions simultaneously.

Beyond compression techniques, the broader challenge of evaluating and comparing inference optimization methods has received growing attention. The diversity of optimization algorithms, hardware configurations, and code libraries available to practitioners makes it difficult to assess which approaches are most effective for a given deployment scenario. Addressing this gap, one line of work proposes a coarse-to-fine evaluation framework for LLM inference efficiency that examines both overall system performance across practical usage scenarios and fine-grained performance characteristics at the level of individual Transformer modules [arxiv_2404.11502]. By combining theoretical analysis with empirical measurement, this framework aims to help users understand not only which methods perform better in aggregate but also why, by identifying the specific computational bottlenecks that different optimizations address. This kind of structured evaluation is particularly valuable given that inference efficiency is not a single-dimensional quantity but depends on the interplay between model architecture, hardware capabilities, batching strategies, and the nature of the workload.

Complementing evaluation frameworks, simulation-based tools offer a different mode of exploration that is especially useful during the design phase, before hardware or software systems are fully deployed. TokenSim is introduced as a comprehensive hardware and software exploration system designed specifically for LLM inference, supporting extensible system optimizations including scheduling and memory management [arxiv_2503.08415]. By simulating LLM inference workloads and validating results against real-world datasets with an error rate of less than one percent, TokenSim enables researchers and system designers to explore the performance implications of different hardware configurations and software strategies without the cost and time required for full system deployment. The ability to simulate scheduling and memory management policies in particular is relevant to the KV cache compression literature, since the effectiveness of compression strategies is tightly coupled to how memory is allocated and managed at the system level.

Taken together, these works reflect a convergence of concerns around memory efficiency, evaluation rigor, and system-level design in LLM inference. Quantization-based compression methods such as GEAR [arxiv_2403.05527] and CQ [arxiv_2405.03917] address the memory bottleneck from an algorithmic perspective, while evaluation frameworks [arxiv_2404.11502] and simulation tools [arxiv_2503.08415] provide the infrastructure needed to assess and explore these optimizations in realistic deployment contexts. The interplay between these layers of the inference stack underscores the importance of treating efficient LLM inference as a systems problem that requires coordinated advances across algorithms, evaluation methodology, and hardware-software co-design.
