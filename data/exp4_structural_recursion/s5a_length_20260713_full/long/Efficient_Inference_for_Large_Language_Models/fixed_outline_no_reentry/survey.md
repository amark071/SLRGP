# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (long)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has fundamentally transformed the landscape of natural language processing, enabling remarkable performance across a diverse array of tasks ranging from question answering and code generation to complex reasoning and dialogue. Yet this success has come at a steep computational price. As these models have scaled to billions and even hundreds of billions of parameters, the memory and computational demands associated with their inference have grown to a degree that poses serious challenges for practical deployment, particularly in resource-constrained environments such as edge devices, mobile platforms, and latency-sensitive production systems. The academic community has responded with a substantial and rapidly expanding body of research aimed at making LLM inference more efficient, and several comprehensive surveys have emerged to organize and synthesize this literature [arxiv_2402.09748; arxiv_2404.14294; arxiv_2401.02038; arxiv_2408.03130].

A useful starting point for understanding the scope of the problem is to identify the fundamental sources of inefficiency in LLM inference. Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] provide a particularly systematic analysis, attributing the core challenges to three interrelated factors: the sheer size of modern language models, the quadratic complexity of the attention operation with respect to sequence length, and the sequential, auto-regressive nature of the decoding process. Each of these factors imposes distinct bottlenecks. Large model size translates directly into high memory consumption and slow weight loading, while quadratic attention complexity becomes prohibitive as context windows extend to tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens. Auto-regressive decoding, meanwhile, requires generating one token at a time, making it inherently difficult to exploit the parallelism that modern hardware accelerators are designed to provide. This tripartite diagnosis is broadly consistent with the framing offered by Wan et al. [arxiv_2402.09748], who similarly note that the inference costs of large models are qualitatively different from those of smaller predecessors, not merely in degree but in kind, because the sheer scale renders many standard post-training optimization strategies either prohibitively expensive or insufficiently effective.

Against this backdrop, the literature has converged on several major families of techniques for improving inference efficiency. The most widely studied are quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, and architectural optimization, and these categories appear consistently across the major surveys [arxiv_2402.09748; arxiv_2404.14294; arxiv_2408.03130; arxiv_2401.02038]. Quantization reduces the numerical precision of model weights and activations, typically from 32-bit or 16-bit floating point to 8-bit integers or lower, thereby reducing both memory footprint and the cost of arithmetic operations. Pruning removes redundant weights or structural components from the network, exploiting the empirical observation that large models tend to be significantly over-parameterized. Knowledge distillation transfers the capabilities of a large teacher model into a smaller, more efficient student model. Architectural optimization encompasses a broader set of strategies, including the design of more efficient attention mechanisms, the replacement of dense layers with sparse or low-rank alternatives, and the search for compact network topologies that achieve favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-offs.

While these categories are shared with the broader model compression literature, Wan et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] are careful to highlight two characteristics that distinguish the LLM setting from earlier work on compressing smaller models. First, the cost of fine-tuning or retraining after compression is dramatically higher for LLMs, which means that methods requiring extensive post-compression adaptation are often impractical. This has spurred considerable interest in training-free or data-free compression approaches that can be applied directly to pre-trained weights without any gradient-based optimization. Second, LLMs are typically deployed as general-purpose systems expected to handle a wide variety of tasks, which means that compression must preserve broad generalization rather than merely maintaining performance on a narrow benchmark. These constraints have important implications for the design and evaluation of compression algorithms.

The survey by Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] adopts a three-level taxonomy that organizes the literature into data-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations, a framing that usefully captures the full stack of interventions available to practitioners. Data-level optimizations include techniques such as prompt compression and input filtering that reduce the effective computational load without modifying the model itself. Model-level optimizations correspond to the classical compression techniques described above. System-level optimizations address the hardware and software infrastructure through which inference is executed, including memory scheduling, batching strategies, and kernel-level implementations. This multi-level perspective is complementary to the more algorithm-centric taxonomy of Wan et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] and together they suggest that achieving efficient inference in practice requires coordinated effort across all three levels.

The survey by Blalock and colleagues, as represented in the review by Kaddour et al. [arxiv_2408.03130], similarly emphasizes the importance of understanding the practical challenges and trade-offs associated with each optimization technique, not merely their theoretical properties. Quantization, for example, can yield substantial speedups on hardware with native support for low-precision arithmetic, but the benefits are highly dependent on the specific hardware platform and software stack. Pruning can reduce the number of floating-point operations, but unstructured sparsity is notoriously difficult to accelerate on modern GPUs without specialized kernels, a point also illustrated by the work on fast transformer inference on CPUs [arxiv_2211.07715]. That work demonstrates that realizing the theoretical benefits of pruning and quantization in practice requires not only the compression algorithms themselves but also a carefully engineered inference runtime with optimized sparse and quantized operators. The resulting pipeline, applied to DistilBERT, achieves significant throughput improvements with minimal accuracy degradation, illustrating the importance of co-designing algorithmic and systems-level components.

One area that has received growing attention as context lengths have increased is the optimization of the key-value (KV) cache, which stores intermediate attention states to avoid redundant computation during auto-regressive decoding. As Dong et al. [arxiv_2508.06297] document in a dedicated review, the memory demands of the KV cache grow linearly with sequence length and batch size, creating a significant bottleneck that can dominate inference costs for long-context applications. The review systematically examines strategies for compressing the KV cache, including selective token retention, quantization of cached values, and attention compression techniques that reduce the number of key-value pairs that must be stored and retrieved. These methods involve non-trivial trade-offs between memory savings, computational overhead, and the potential for accuracy degradation, and the review identifies compatibility with diverse model architectures and deployment scenarios as a key open challenge. The KV cache problem is in some respects distinct from classical model compression in that it is a dynamic, runtime phenomenon rather than a static property of the model weights, and it therefore requires optimization strategies that operate at inference time rather than during a pre-deployment compression phase.

Architectural search and design represent another important dimension of the efficiency landscape. While much of the compression literature focuses on modifying existing pre-trained models, a complementary line of work asks whether more efficient architectures can be identified from the outset or discovered through automated search. Ma et al. [arxiv_2409.17372] argue that most compression techniques concentrate on weight optimization while neglecting the exploration of optimal architectures, and propose a training-free architecture search framework that identifies efficient subnets within a pre-trained LLM. By inheriting weights from the original model, these subnets can achieve inference acceleration without the need for retraining, addressing the practical constraint identified by Wan et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] regarding the high cost of post-compression fine-tuning. This approach is conceptually related to the broader neural architecture search (NAS) literature, but the scale of LLMs has historically made NAS prohibitively expensive. The work of Munoz et al. [arxiv_2501.16372] addresses this challenge by combining low-rank adapter techniques with weight-sharing super-networks, a strategy that enables efficient architecture search and fine-tuning simultaneously. Low-rank adapters, originally developed for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, are shown to be compatible with NAS-based compression, and the resulting models exhibit reduced memory footprints and faster inference times. This synthesis of PEFT and NAS represents an emerging direction that seeks to democratize LLM deployment by making high-quality compressed models accessible without requiring massive computational resources.

The comprehensive overview provided by Zhao et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] situates these inference optimization techniques within the broader context of LLM development, tracing the evolution from training through deployment. Their treatment of the inference side covers model compression, parallel computation, memory scheduling, and structural optimization, and they emphasize that low-cost deployment is not merely a current engineering concern but a defining trend that will shape the future trajectory of LLM research. This perspective reinforces the view that efficient inference is not a peripheral concern but a central challenge whose resolution is necessary for the continued practical impact of large language models.

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals a field that is both technically diverse and strategically coherent. The diversity is evident in the range of techniques under active investigation, from low-level quantization and pruning to high-level architectural search and KV cache management. The coherence lies in the shared recognition that the computational and memory costs of LLM inference are a fundamental barrier to broad deployment, and that overcoming this barrier requires innovations at multiple levels of the system stack. The following sections of this review examine each major category of optimization in greater depth, analyzing the mechanisms, trade-offs, and empirical results that characterize the current state of the art.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently has become one of the defining problems of contemporary natural language processing research. As model sizes have grown from hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, the gap between raw model capability and practical deployability has widened considerably. Researchers have responded by developing a rich ecosystem of techniques targeting different stages of the model lifecycle—from training strategies and compression methods to runtime memory management and decoding acceleration. This subsection surveys the foundational ideas and recent advances in this space, tracing the intellectual lineage of efficient inference research and situating key contributions relative to one another.

A natural starting point is the question of how model size interacts with training efficiency, since the economics of training directly shape what models are available for inference. The counterintuitive finding reported by [arxiv_2002.11794] is instructive here: although smaller Transformer models execute faster per iteration, wider and deeper models converge in substantially fewer steps, and this acceleration in convergence typically outpaces the additional computational overhead of using larger models. The implication is that the most compute-efficient training strategy is to train extremely large models but stop after a comparatively small number of iterations—a regime the authors describe as "train large, then compress." This observation reframes the efficiency problem: rather than constraining model size at training time, one should train at scale and subsequently apply compression to recover inference-time efficiency. This perspective has proven influential, as it motivates the large body of post-training compression work that followed and implicitly justifies the two-stage paradigm—large-scale pretraining followed by quantization, pruning, or distillation—that now dominates the field [arxiv_2405.11704].

Within this two-stage paradigm, quantization has emerged as one of the most practically impactful compression strategies. The core idea is to represent model weights and, in some formulations, activations using lower-precision numerical formats, thereby reducing both memory footprint and the cost of arithmetic operations. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is particularly attractive because it requires no retraining, making it applicable to already-deployed models. However, as [arxiv_2408.08554] observe, PTQ deployment faces two interrelated challenges: low-bit quantization degrades model performance, and the limited variety of integer computing units available on contemporary GPUs means that quantized matrix operations at non-standard precisions cannot be straightforwardly accelerated. Their proposed framework, ABQ-LLM, addresses both issues by introducing an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm paired with an inference framework capable of executing arbitrary-precision quantized operations efficiently on GPU hardware. This contribution is notable because it decouples the choice of quantization precision from the constraints of hardware integer unit types, opening the design space to finer-grained precision assignments across layers or even individual weight groups.

The tension between compression aggressiveness and model quality is also central to [arxiv_2305.14152], which focuses on the intersection of quantization and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT). The authors note that while PEFT methods such as LoRA reduce optimizer state memory during fine-tuning, the sheer size of pre-trained LLM weights remains a bottleneck, and most quantization techniques are designed for the deployment phase rather than for continued adaptation. Their method, PEQA (Parameter-Efficient and Quantization-aware Adaptation), bridges this gap by updating only the quantization scales of an already-quantized model, enabling task-specific adaptation without dequantizing the weights. Compared to ABQ-LLM [arxiv_2408.08554], which focuses on inference acceleration for fixed models, PEQA targets the fine-tuning phase and is therefore complementary: together, these works suggest a workflow in which a model is quantized aggressively, fine-tuned via scale updates, and then deployed with hardware-aware quantized kernels. The broader survey by [arxiv_2405.11704] situates both approaches within a taxonomy of model compression techniques—quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation—and notes that adaptive optimization algorithms such as AdamW and mixed-precision training strategies contribute to efficiency gains not only at inference time but also during the training phase that precedes compression.

Beyond weight compression, a second major axis of inference optimization concerns the management of the key-value (KV) cache, which arises from the autoregressive nature of Transformer-based generation. During decoding, the keys and values computed at each attention layer for all previously generated tokens must be retained so that subsequent tokens can attend to them without recomputation. The memory cost of this cache grows linearly with both sequence length and batch size, making it a primary bottleneck for serving long-context or high-throughput workloads [arxiv_2404.15949]. Two complementary strategies have been developed to address this: eviction-based approaches that selectively discard KV entries deemed unlikely to be needed, and offloading-based approaches that move KV data to slower but larger memory tiers and prefetch it on demand.

CORM [arxiv_2404.15949] exemplifies the eviction approach. The authors identify two empirical regularities in Transformer attention: adjacent tokens tend to have highly similar query vectors, and the attention computation for the current query can rely on the attention information of only a small fraction of preceding queries. Exploiting these observations, CORM implements a dynamic eviction policy that retains only the KV pairs most relevant to recent queries, substantially reducing the memory footprint of the cache without requiring modifications to model weights or architecture. The policy is training-free and can be applied at inference time, making it broadly applicable across model families.

InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] takes a different approach, targeting long-text generation scenarios where the full KV cache cannot fit in GPU memory even with aggressive eviction. Rather than discarding tokens permanently, InfiniGen offloads the KV cache to CPU memory and uses a speculative prefetching mechanism to retrieve only the entries that will be needed for the next attention computation. The key insight is that the important tokens for a given attention layer can be predicted by performing a lightweight rehearsal using the inputs of the current layer together with a subset of the query weights and key cache of the subsequent layer. This allows the system to prefetch only the essential KV entries before they are needed, hiding the latency of CPU-to-GPU data transfer. Comparing CORM and InfiniGen, one observes a complementary division of labor: CORM is most effective when the working set of relevant KV entries is small relative to GPU memory capacity, while InfiniGen is designed for regimes where even a compressed cache exceeds on-device memory. In practice, these approaches could be combined—evicting the least important entries and offloading the remainder—though the literature has not yet fully explored such hybrid designs.

A third major strategy for accelerating LLM inference is speculative decoding, which addresses the sequential bottleneck inherent in autoregressive generation. Because each token must be generated one at a time and each generation step requires a full forward pass through the large model, the process is inherently latency-bound. Speculative decoding circumvents this by using a small, fast draft model to propose multiple candidate tokens in parallel, which are then verified in a single batched forward pass through the large model. If the large model accepts the proposed tokens, multiple tokens are effectively generated per forward pass, reducing end-to-end latency. SPIN [arxiv_2503.15921] extends this paradigm in several important directions. First, it observes that existing speculative decoding systems use a single, homogeneous small speculative model (SSM) for all requests, which is suboptimal because different requests vary in difficulty and therefore benefit from different draft models. SPIN addresses this by maintaining a pool of heterogeneous SSMs and using a learning-based algorithm to select the most appropriate SSM for each request without requiring prior knowledge of request difficulty. Second, SPIN introduces robust support for batch processing, which is essential for high-throughput serving but has been underexplored in the speculative decoding literature. Third, the system optimizes both the speculation and verification phases holistically, rather than treating them independently. These innovations position SPIN as a systems-level contribution that builds on the theoretical foundations of speculative decoding while addressing practical deployment concerns.

The relationship between speculative decoding and KV cache management is worth noting. Speculative decoding introduces additional complexity for KV cache handling because the draft tokens may be rejected, requiring the cache to be rolled back to the last accepted token. This interaction means that KV cache optimization strategies such as those proposed by CORM [arxiv_2404.15949] and InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] must be carefully integrated with speculative decoding systems. SPIN [arxiv_2503.15921] acknowledges this challenge as part of its holistic optimization framework, though the precise mechanisms for cache management under speculative decoding remain an active area of investigation.

Stepping back, the tutorial by [arxiv_2407.09111] provides a useful integrative perspective, surveying the full landscape of inference optimization techniques for foundation models on AI accelerators. The authors organize the field around complementary axes: model compression (quantization, pruning, distillation), memory management (KV cache optimization, offloading), and decoding acceleration (speculative decoding, parallel decoding). Their framing emphasizes that these techniques are not mutually exclusive but rather form a layered optimization stack, with each layer addressing a different bottleneck. This systems view is consistent with the trajectory of the literature reviewed here: early work focused on individual techniques in isolation, while more recent contributions such as SPIN [arxiv_2503.15921] and InfiniGen [arxiv_2406.19707] increasingly integrate multiple strategies within unified frameworks.

Taken together, the works reviewed in this subsection reveal a field that has matured from isolated algorithmic contributions toward integrated systems thinking. The foundational insight of [arxiv_2002.11794]—that large models are worth training because they can be compressed—has been operationalized through quantization frameworks [arxiv_2408.08554, arxiv_2305.14152], KV cache management systems [arxiv_2404.15949, arxiv_2406.19707], and speculative decoding architectures [arxiv_2503.15921], all surveyed within the broader context of hardware-aware deployment [arxiv_2407.09111, arxiv_2405.11704]. The central tension running through this literature is between model quality and resource consumption, and the most promising recent directions are those that treat this tension not as a fixed trade-off but as an optimization problem amenable to learned, adaptive, and hardware-co-designed solutions.

### Evidence theme 3

The memory and computational demands of large language model (LLM) inference have catalyzed a rich body of research focused on reducing bottlenecks without sacrificing model quality. Among the most consequential of these bottlenecks is the key-value (KV) cache, a data structure that stores intermediate attention computations to avoid redundant recalculation during autoregressive generation. While the KV cache enables the substitution of quadratic-complexity attention recomputation with linear-complexity memory accesses [arxiv_2403.17312], it introduces its own severe memory pressure, particularly as sequence lengths grow and model depth increases. The literature has responded with a diverse array of strategies spanning hardware-aware design, architectural modification, sparsity exploitation, and compression, each targeting different facets of the inference efficiency problem.

A foundational observation motivating much of this work is that the KV cache scales with both the number of transformer layers and the sequence length, making deep models especially susceptible to memory exhaustion. The survey by [arxiv_2412.19442] provides a comprehensive taxonomy of KV cache management strategies, organizing them into token-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations. Token-level strategies include cache selection, budget allocation, merging, quantization, and low-rank decomposition, while model-level and system-level approaches address architectural choices and deployment infrastructure respectively. This hierarchical framing is useful for situating more specialized contributions, as it reveals that no single technique dominates across all deployment scenarios; rather, the field has converged on a recognition that complementary methods must often be combined.

At the model-level, one of the more structurally innovative proposals is the Layer-Condensed KV Cache [arxiv_2405.10637], which challenges the conventional assumption that every transformer layer must maintain its own KV cache. By computing and caching KVs for only a small subset of layers and reusing them across the remaining layers, this approach achieves up to 26× higher throughput than standard transformers while remaining competitive on language modeling and downstream task benchmarks. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that their method is orthogonal to other memory-saving techniques, meaning it can be composed with quantization, pruning, or other compression strategies without architectural conflict. This composability is a significant practical advantage, as it allows practitioners to stack multiple optimizations without redesigning the inference pipeline from scratch.

A complementary architectural approach is the Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) introduced in [arxiv_2411.15785], which addresses KV cache memory consumption by imposing a predefined capacity constraint at the model design stage rather than as a post-hoc optimization. The BCT dynamically updates key-value vector sequences within a fixed-length cache, enabling efficient inference under strict memory budgets while preserving model performance and system throughput. Compared to the Layer-Condensed KV Cache, which reduces memory by limiting the number of layers that contribute to the cache, the BCT operates by bounding the temporal extent of cached tokens. These two approaches are thus complementary: one compresses across the layer dimension, the other across the sequence dimension. Together, they illustrate that the KV cache can be attacked from multiple structural angles, and the choice between them may depend on whether the primary constraint is model depth or context length.

Sparsity-aware approaches represent another important thread in the literature. ALISA [arxiv_2403.17312] exploits the observation that attention patterns in transformer models are often sparse, meaning that not all cached key-value pairs are equally relevant for a given query. By selectively retaining only the most informative KV entries based on sparsity patterns, ALISA reduces the I/O bottleneck that arises when large caches must be loaded from memory during each decoding step. This is particularly relevant for resource-constrained deployments on commodity GPUs, where out-of-memory errors and reduced throughput due to I/O saturation are practical concerns. The sparsity-aware framing connects ALISA to the broader token-level selection strategies catalogued in [arxiv_2412.19442], but ALISA's contribution lies in its system-level integration of sparsity detection with cache management, rather than treating them as independent concerns.

Beyond KV cache management, the inference efficiency literature also grapples with the memory bandwidth demands imposed by model weights themselves. This is especially acute for autoregressive generation, where each decoding step requires loading the full set of model parameters from memory to compute a single output token, making the process heavily memory-bound rather than compute-bound [arxiv_2403.00858]. Weight quantization has emerged as a primary tool for addressing this bottleneck. The work on efficient LLM inference on CPUs [arxiv_2311.00502] proposes an automatic INT4 weight-only quantization flow paired with highly optimized runtime kernels, demonstrating that aggressive quantization to four-bit integer representations can enable practical LLM deployment on CPU hardware. This is a notable departure from the prevailing assumption that LLM inference requires high-end GPU accelerators, and the results on models including Llama2, Llama, and GPT-NeoX suggest that the approach generalizes across model families. The public release of the associated codebase further enhances the practical impact of this contribution.

The integration of quantization with other compression techniques is explored in [arxiv_2601.09865], which proposes a unified framework combining GPTQ-based quantization, low-rank adaptation (LoRA), and knowledge distillation. By leveraging Kullback-Leibler divergence for distillation, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, and the Muon optimizer, this pipeline achieves up to 2× memory compression—for instance, reducing a 6GB model to 3GB—while preserving or enhancing task-specific performance. The explicit combination of quantization and distillation in a single pipeline reflects a broader trend in the field toward integrated optimization frameworks, as opposed to applying individual techniques in isolation. This is consistent with the composability argument made in [arxiv_2405.10637], and with the multi-level taxonomy of [arxiv_2412.19442], both of which emphasize that the greatest efficiency gains often arise from combining orthogonal methods.

Hardware-level innovations offer yet another dimension of the inference efficiency problem. The compression-aware memory controller design proposed in [arxiv_2503.18869] takes a fundamentally different approach by modifying the on-chip memory controller in AI accelerators to apply lossless block compression algorithms such as LZ4 and ZSTD to both model weights and KV cache data. Unlike lossy compression techniques such as quantization, lossless compression preserves inference quality exactly while still reducing memory capacity and bandwidth consumption. Additionally, the design enables memory bandwidth and energy consumption to scale proportionally with context-dependent dynamic quantization, providing a hardware substrate that can adapt to varying inference workloads. This contribution is notable for operating at a layer of the stack—the memory controller—that is largely invisible to software-level optimization efforts, suggesting that hardware-software co-design may be essential for achieving the full potential of inference efficiency improvements. Compared to purely algorithmic approaches such as ALISA [arxiv_2403.17312] or the Layer-Condensed KV Cache [arxiv_2405.10637], the memory controller approach of [arxiv_2503.18869] is transparent to the model and runtime, making it broadly applicable without requiring changes to model architecture or inference code.

Speculative decoding represents a distinct but related strategy for accelerating LLM inference, targeting the latency rather than the memory footprint of generation. The core idea is to use a small, fast draft model to propose candidate token sequences, which are then verified in parallel by the larger target model, thereby amortizing the cost of the target model's memory-bound decoding steps across multiple tokens [arxiv_2403.00858]. A key challenge for this approach is the availability of suitable draft models, particularly for instruction-tuned or chat-fine-tuned variants of popular model families. The work in [arxiv_2403.00858] addresses this by proposing a training framework for direct alignment of draft models to chat-capable target models, training a 115M-parameter drafter for Llama 2 Chat 7B using only pretraining, distillation dataset generation, and fine-tuning. The resulting drafter represents only 1.64% of the original model's parameter count, making it extremely lightweight. This approach connects to the distillation-based compression strategies in [arxiv_2601.09865], though the objectives differ: [arxiv_2403.00858] uses distillation to align the draft model's output distribution with the target model for the purpose of speculative decoding, while [arxiv_2601.09865] uses distillation to compress the target model itself for direct deployment.

Taken together, the literature surveyed here reveals a field characterized by methodological diversity and increasing sophistication in the combination of techniques. Memory bottlenecks in LLM inference arise from multiple sources—model weight size, KV cache growth, memory bandwidth limitations, and hardware architecture constraints—and the research community has responded with solutions operating at each of these levels. The convergence toward integrated frameworks that combine quantization, architectural modification, sparsity exploitation, and hardware co-design reflects a maturing understanding that no single technique is sufficient for the full range of deployment scenarios encountered in practice.

### Evidence theme 4

The efficient deployment of large language models (LLMs) at scale has become one of the central engineering and research challenges of the current era. As model sizes continue to grow, the costs associated with inference—measured in memory consumption, latency, and energy expenditure—have grown correspondingly, motivating a rich body of work on compression, caching, and system-level optimization. This subsection surveys the principal strategies that have emerged for making LLM inference tractable, organizing the discussion around three interrelated themes: model compression through quantization and structural pruning, key-value (KV) cache optimization, and systems-level frameworks for holistic inference management.

Model compression occupies a foundational role in the efficient inference literature. The core intuition is that the full-precision parameters of a large pre-trained model carry more representational capacity than is strictly necessary for many downstream tasks, and that reducing numerical precision or eliminating redundant components can yield substantial savings with minimal accuracy degradation. Quantization, which maps floating-point weights and activations to lower-bit integer representations, has attracted particular attention because it directly reduces both memory footprint and the cost of arithmetic operations. Early work in this space typically separated the quantization step from any task-specific adaptation: a pre-trained model would first be subjected to post-training quantization (PTQ), and then parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) would be applied to recover accuracy lost during compression [arxiv_2210.03858]. AlphaTuning exemplifies this paradigm by employing binary-coding quantization, which factorizes full-precision parameters into binary values and a set of learnable scaling factors; during adaptation, the binary values are frozen while only the scaling factors are updated, achieving a compact representation that supports efficient inference [arxiv_2210.03858]. This separation of concerns—compress first, then adapt—is computationally convenient but carries an inherent limitation: the quantization error introduced in the PTQ stage is not directly visible to the fine-tuning objective, so the recovery of accuracy is necessarily incomplete.

More recent work has sought to overcome this limitation by integrating quantization-aware training (QAT) directly with PEFT. L4Q proposes precisely such an integration, combining QAT with LoRA through a memory-optimized layer design that allows the model to learn quantization-robust representations during the adaptation phase itself [arxiv_2402.04902]. By making the quantization process differentiable and jointly optimizing it with the low-rank adaptation parameters, L4Q addresses the accuracy gap that afflicts PTQ-then-PEFT pipelines. The authors report that this approach significantly reduces memory overhead during both training and inference while maintaining competitive task performance [arxiv_2402.04902]. Comparing AlphaTuning and L4Q thus reveals a clear methodological progression: where AlphaTuning treats quantization and adaptation as sequential operations, L4Q treats them as a unified optimization problem, reflecting a broader trend in the field toward end-to-end co-design of compression and fine-tuning.

Beyond weight quantization, structural approaches to model compression have also demonstrated promise. One particularly striking finding is that not all components of a Transformer architecture contribute equally to output quality. Empirical investigation of attention sublayers in Llama 2 7B reveals that latter attention sublayers are largely redundant: skipping them entirely yields a 21% speed increase in single-token generation while, counterintuitively, improving performance on several standard benchmarks [arxiv_2404.05741]. This result challenges the assumption that every architectural component of a large model is necessary for high-quality inference and suggests that principled layer-dropping strategies can serve as a lightweight and effective form of model compression. The observation also has implications for the design of future architectures, pointing toward the possibility of building models with fewer attention layers in later stages without sacrificing capability [arxiv_2404.05741]. Taken together, the quantization and structural pruning literature converges on the view that LLMs as typically trained contain substantial redundancy, and that exploiting this redundancy is a viable path to efficient inference.

A second major axis of research concerns the KV cache, which has become a dominant source of memory pressure in autoregressive LLM inference. During generation, the keys and values computed for all previous tokens must be stored and reused at each decoding step; as sequence length, batch size, or model size increases, the KV cache can quickly become the primary bottleneck for both memory and latency [arxiv_2405.03917]. The problem is particularly acute for long-context models, where the cache grows linearly with sequence length and can exhaust GPU memory before the generation task is complete [arxiv_2512.01953]. Addressing this bottleneck has motivated a range of techniques, including token eviction, sparse attention, and, most relevantly here, quantization of the cached activations themselves.

Quantizing the KV cache introduces a distinct set of challenges compared to weight quantization, because the keys and values are dynamic activations rather than static parameters, and their statistical properties can vary substantially across layers, heads, and token positions. Naive uniform quantization to very low bit widths tends to introduce large approximation errors that compound across decoding steps, leading to significant degradation in generation quality [arxiv_2403.05527]. GEAR addresses this problem through a hybrid compression strategy: the majority of KV entries, which exhibit similar magnitudes, are quantized to ultra-low precision, while a small residual component is handled separately to absorb the approximation error [arxiv_2403.05527]. By explicitly modeling and correcting the error introduced by aggressive quantization, GEAR achieves near-lossless compression at high compression ratios, making it possible to serve long-context requests within tight memory budgets.

A complementary perspective on KV cache quantization is offered by Coupled Quantization (CQ), which observes that distinct channels within a key or value activation embedding are not statistically independent: their joint entropy grows more slowly than the sum of their marginal entropies, indicating substantial inter-channel redundancy [arxiv_2405.03917]. By coupling multiple channels together during quantization and exploiting this inter-dependency, CQ encodes activations in a more information-efficient manner than channel-wise methods, achieving effective compression rates as low as one bit per channel without the catastrophic accuracy loss that typically accompanies such extreme quantization [arxiv_2405.03917]. The contrast between GEAR and CQ is instructive: GEAR focuses on the heterogeneity of entry magnitudes within a single channel and uses a residual correction mechanism, while CQ focuses on the statistical dependencies across channels and uses joint encoding. Both approaches represent sophisticated departures from uniform quantization, and both demonstrate that careful attention to the statistical structure of KV activations is essential for achieving high compression without quality loss.

The interaction between KV cache compression and other inference optimizations has begun to receive systematic attention. KV Pareto introduces a systems-level framework that jointly evaluates KV cache quantization, chunked prefill, and model weight quantization, mapping the Pareto frontier between total memory consumption and task accuracy across multiple LLM architectures including Qwen, Llama, and Mistral [arxiv_2512.01953]. By exploring combinations of integer quantization schemes at varying granularities—per-token, per-tensor, and per-block—alongside four-bit weight quantization via AWQ, KV Pareto identifies model-specific configurations that achieve optimal memory-accuracy trade-offs [arxiv_2512.01953]. This work makes an important methodological contribution by demonstrating that the optimal configuration is not universal but depends on the specific model architecture and deployment context, and that individual optimization techniques evaluated in isolation may not combine additively when deployed together. The framework thus provides practitioners with a principled tool for navigating the complex configuration space of inference optimization.

The complexity of this configuration space underscores the need for simulation and profiling tools that can evaluate inference systems without requiring exhaustive physical experimentation. TokenSim addresses this need by providing a comprehensive hardware and software exploration framework designed specifically for LLM inference [arxiv_2503.08415]. The system supports extensible optimizations including scheduling and memory management, and validates its predictions against real-world workloads with an error rate of less than one percent [arxiv_2503.08415]. By enabling rapid exploration of the performance implications of different system configurations, TokenSim complements the algorithmic contributions of works like KV Pareto and GEAR, providing the infrastructure needed to translate theoretical compression gains into practical deployment decisions.

Evaluation methodology itself has emerged as a non-trivial concern in the efficient inference literature. The diversity of optimization techniques, code libraries, and deployment scenarios makes it difficult for practitioners to compare methods on a common basis or to understand which optimizations are responsible for observed performance gains. A coarse-to-fine evaluation framework proposed in [arxiv_2404.11502] addresses this challenge by examining inference performance across four usage scenarios within two practical applications, and by providing both theoretical and empirical fine-grained analyses of individual Transformer modules. This multi-granularity approach reveals that the relative effectiveness of different optimization strategies depends strongly on the specific usage scenario, and that module-level analysis is necessary to understand the mechanisms underlying system-level performance differences [arxiv_2404.11502]. The framework thus serves as a diagnostic tool that can guide the selection and combination of optimization techniques for specific deployment contexts.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here reveals a field that has moved well beyond simple, isolated compression techniques toward integrated, systems-aware approaches to efficient inference. The progression from PTQ-then-PEFT pipelines to joint QAT-PEFT methods like L4Q reflects a growing recognition that compression and adaptation must be co-designed to minimize accuracy loss [arxiv_2402.04902]. The development of sophisticated KV cache compression methods like GEAR and CQ reflects the emergence of the cache as a primary bottleneck and the need for techniques that respect the statistical structure of dynamic activations [arxiv_2403.05527][arxiv_2405.03917]. The systems-level perspective embodied in KV Pareto and TokenSim reflects the understanding that individual optimizations must be evaluated and configured in the context of the full inference pipeline [arxiv_2512.01953][arxiv_2503.08415]. And the structural finding that latter attention layers can be skipped without accuracy loss [arxiv_2404.05741] opens new directions for architectural simplification that complement quantization-based approaches. The convergence of these threads suggests that the most impactful future advances in efficient LLM inference will likely arise from principled co-optimization across the algorithmic, architectural, and systems dimensions simultaneously.

The trajectory traced by the literature points toward several underexplored frontiers that deserve closer examination. One such frontier concerns the hardware–software co-design perspective, wherein the memory controller itself—rather than solely the model weights or activation representations—becomes a target for optimization. The compression-aware memory controller design introduced in [arxiv_2503.18869] exemplifies this direction by enhancing the on-chip memory controller in AI accelerators to transparently handle compressed data formats, thereby alleviating bandwidth and capacity bottlenecks without requiring changes to the model or the serving stack. This approach is conceptually distinct from algorithmic compression methods in that it operates at the microarchitectural level, treating compression as a first-class concern in hardware design rather than as a post-hoc software transformation. The implication is that the gains achievable through algorithmic compression—whether via quantization, pruning, or KV cache eviction—can be further amplified when the underlying hardware is designed to natively process compressed representations, suggesting that future efficiency gains will increasingly require co-optimization across the full hardware–software stack rather than advances in any single layer alone.

Speculative decoding represents another major axis of inference acceleration that operates orthogonally to compression, targeting the sequential bottleneck inherent in autoregressive generation rather than the memory footprint of model parameters or activations. The core insight is that a small, fast draft model can propose multiple candidate tokens in parallel, which the larger target model then verifies in a single forward pass, recovering the exact output distribution of the target while amortizing its per-token cost over multiple accepted tokens [arxiv_2403.00858]. However, the practical effectiveness of speculative decoding depends critically on the alignment between the draft and target model distributions, and this alignment is not guaranteed when the target model has been fine-tuned for instruction following or chat applications. The direct alignment approach of [arxiv_2403.00858] addresses this by training the draft model to match the output distribution of a chat-fine-tuned target, demonstrating that distribution mismatch is a significant source of inefficiency in naive speculative decoding pipelines and that targeted fine-tuning of the draft model can substantially improve acceptance rates. This finding has practical implications for the deployment of speculative decoding in production systems, where target models are almost universally fine-tuned rather than base pretrained models.

The heterogeneity of real-world inference workloads introduces further complications that homogeneous speculative decoding systems are ill-equipped to handle. Requests vary substantially in difficulty, prompt length, and expected output length, and a single draft model calibrated for average-case performance will be suboptimal for requests at the tails of the difficulty distribution. SPIN addresses this limitation by employing heterogeneous speculative models that adapt the speculation strategy to the estimated difficulty of each request, while also providing robust support for batched inference [arxiv_2503.15921]. The ability to handle batched requests is particularly important in high-throughput serving scenarios, where the efficiency gains of speculative decoding must be realized across diverse concurrent requests rather than for a single isolated query. Complementing this, the ALISE framework approaches the scheduling dimension of speculative decoding by introducing speculative scheduling policies that mitigate head-of-line blocking and reduce tail latency in multi-request serving systems [arxiv_2410.23537]. The distinction between SPIN and ALISE is instructive: SPIN focuses on the model-level question of which draft model to use for a given request, while ALISE focuses on the system-level question of how to schedule requests to minimize overall latency. Both dimensions are necessary for realizing the full potential of speculative decoding in production deployments, and their joint consideration points toward a richer design space than either perspective alone would suggest.

Aggregated speculative execution, as explored in the Minions framework, extends the speculative decoding paradigm further by aggregating predictions from multiple small models rather than relying on a single draft model [arxiv_2402.15678]. This aggregation strategy improves token acceptance rates by diversifying the proposal distribution, and it does so without requiring fine-tuning of either the draft or target models, making it applicable in settings where model modification is impractical. The contrast with the direct alignment approach of [arxiv_2403.00858] highlights a fundamental trade-off in speculative decoding system design: alignment-based methods achieve high acceptance rates through targeted training but require access to the target model's training pipeline, while aggregation-based methods achieve robustness through diversity but may introduce coordination overhead. Understanding when each approach is preferable requires careful empirical characterization across diverse model families and deployment scenarios, and this remains an active area of investigation.

The cross-layer sharing of key-value representations offers yet another architectural lever for reducing the memory footprint of the KV cache, distinct from both eviction-based and quantization-based approaches. The Layer-Condensed KV Cache method proposes computing and caching KV pairs for only a small subset of layers, with the remaining layers reusing cached representations from the selected layers [arxiv_2405.10637]. This approach exploits the empirical observation that KV representations in adjacent or nearby layers are often highly similar, so that reuse introduces only modest approximation error while yielding substantial memory savings proportional to the fraction of layers whose KV computation is eliminated. The YOCO++ framework extends this cross-layer sharing paradigm by incorporating weighted residual connections that allow upper layers to refine the shared KV representations from the middle layer, thereby recovering much of the accuracy lost by naive sharing [arxiv_2604.13556]. The residual connection mechanism is conceptually analogous to the error-correction approach of GEAR [arxiv_2403.05527], but applied at the architectural level of layer-wise KV sharing rather than at the quantization level of entry-wise approximation. This convergence of error-correction thinking across different levels of the inference stack suggests a general design principle: aggressive compression or sharing can be made near-lossless by pairing it with a lightweight residual or correction mechanism that absorbs the approximation error.

The Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) takes a more radical architectural stance by proposing that models be designed from the outset with a predefined KV cache capacity, rather than retrofitting cache compression onto models trained with unbounded cache assumptions [arxiv_2411.15785]. By dynamically updating the key-value vector sequences within a fixed-size cache during training, BCT learns to make efficient use of bounded memory as an intrinsic capability rather than as an externally imposed constraint. This approach is philosophically aligned with the "train large, then compress" paradigm critiqued in [arxiv_2002.11794], but inverts the logic: rather than training a large model and then compressing it, BCT trains a model that is inherently efficient by design. The tension between these two philosophies—post-hoc compression versus efficiency-by-design—runs throughout the efficient inference literature and reflects a deeper question about whether the redundancy present in large models is a necessary byproduct of effective training or an avoidable inefficiency that better architectures could eliminate.

The Dynamic Memory Compression (DMC) method occupies an interesting middle ground in this design space by learning compression policies online during inference rather than imposing fixed compression ratios at training time or post-hoc [arxiv_2403.09636]. DMC trains the model to decide, for each attention head and each token, whether to merge the current KV pair with a previous one or to store it independently, effectively learning a content-adaptive compression policy that varies across heads, layers, and input contexts. The learned policy naturally allocates more cache capacity to heads and layers that require fine-grained temporal resolution and compresses more aggressively where coarser representations suffice, achieving better accuracy-memory trade-offs than fixed-ratio methods. The ALISA framework similarly exploits the sparsity structure of attention patterns to guide KV cache eviction decisions, observing that attention weights are highly sparse and that the important tokens for a given query can be identified with low overhead [arxiv_2403.17312]. The convergence of DMC and ALISA on the principle of content-adaptive, sparsity-aware cache management—despite arriving from different methodological directions—provides strong evidence that static, content-agnostic compression policies leave substantial efficiency gains on the table.

The InfiniGen framework addresses the specific challenge of long-text generation, where the KV cache grows to sizes that exceed GPU memory capacity even with aggressive compression [arxiv_2406.19707]. InfiniGen introduces a dynamic KV cache management strategy that prefetches and evicts cache entries based on predicted future access patterns, synergistically combining partial KV generation with CPU offloading to extend the effective context length beyond what GPU memory alone could support. The CORM method similarly targets the memory footprint of long-context inference by observing that recent messages in the KV cache are disproportionately important for predicting the next token, and that older entries can be compressed more aggressively without significant accuracy loss [arxiv_2404.15949]. The complementarity between InfiniGen and CORM is notable: InfiniGen manages the logistics of moving cache entries between GPU and CPU memory, while CORM reduces the total volume of data that must be managed by exploiting the recency bias of attention. Combining these two approaches—recency-aware compression with offload-aware prefetching—represents a natural direction for future work on long-context inference efficiency.

Finally, the LoRC framework introduces low-rank compression of KV cache matrices as an alternative to quantization-based approaches, decomposing key and value matrices into low-rank factors that can be stored and computed more efficiently [arxiv_2410.03111]. The progressive compression strategy of LoRC allows the compression ratio to increase gradually as the sequence length grows, matching the compression aggressiveness to the available redundancy in the accumulated cache. This progressive approach contrasts with the fixed-ratio methods reviewed earlier and reflects a broader trend toward adaptive, context-sensitive efficiency techniques that respond dynamically to the characteristics of the input rather than applying uniform transformations. Taken together, the diverse approaches surveyed in this section—from hardware-level memory controller design [arxiv_2503.18869] to architectural innovations like BCT [arxiv_2411.15785] and YOCO++ [arxiv_2604.13556], from speculative decoding variants [arxiv_2503.15921, arxiv_2410.23537] to adaptive cache management strategies [arxiv_2403.09636, arxiv_2403.17312, arxiv_2406.19707]—collectively illustrate that efficient LLM inference is a genuinely multi-dimensional problem whose solution will require sustained co-innovation across algorithms, architectures, and systems.
