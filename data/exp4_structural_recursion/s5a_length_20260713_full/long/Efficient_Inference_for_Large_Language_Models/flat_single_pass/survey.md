# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (long)

The challenge of deploying large language models (LLMs) efficiently has emerged as one of the most consequential research problems in contemporary artificial intelligence. As transformer-based architectures have scaled to hundreds of billions of parameters, the computational and memory costs incurred during inference have grown correspondingly, creating a substantial gap between the capabilities demonstrated in research settings and what can be practically sustained in production environments or on resource-constrained hardware. This subsection surveys the landscape of efficient inference for LLMs, examining the primary sources of inefficiency, the major families of optimization techniques that have been proposed to address them, and the systems-level considerations that govern how these techniques interact in practice. The discussion draws on a broad body of recent literature to characterize both the state of the art and the open challenges that remain.

To understand why efficient inference is so difficult, it is necessary first to appreciate the structural sources of inefficiency in modern LLMs. Three root causes recur throughout the literature. The first is sheer model size: contemporary LLMs contain parameter counts that can reach into the hundreds of billions, requiring memory capacities that far exceed what is available on a single consumer-grade accelerator. The second is the quadratic complexity of the self-attention mechanism with respect to sequence length, which imposes severe computational costs when processing long contexts. The third is the autoregressive decoding paradigm, in which tokens are generated one at a time, each requiring a full forward pass through the model, making the process inherently sequential and memory-bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound [arxiv_2404.14294]. These three factors interact in ways that compound their individual effects: a large model with a long context window, serving many concurrent requests, can exhaust both memory capacity and memory bandwidth simultaneously, leading to severe throughput degradation and latency spikes.

A useful framing for the overall optimization landscape is the taxonomy proposed by several survey papers, which organize techniques into data-level, model-level, and system-level categories [arxiv_2404.14294]. Model-level approaches seek to reduce the intrinsic cost of the model itself, either by compressing existing weights or by designing architectures that are cheaper to execute. System-level approaches accept the model as given and instead optimize how it is scheduled, batched, and executed on hardware. Data-level approaches manipulate the inputs or intermediate representations to reduce the work that must be done. In practice, the most effective deployments combine techniques from all three levels, and understanding how they interact is as important as understanding each technique in isolation [arxiv_2408.03130]. A complementary perspective offered by other surveys organizes the same material around the specific bottleneck being addressed—memory capacity, memory bandwidth, or arithmetic throughput—which can be more useful when diagnosing a particular deployment scenario [arxiv_2402.09748].

Before turning to specific techniques, it is worth noting a foundational empirical observation that motivates much of the compression literature. Work on transformer models for NLP established that training very large models for a relatively small number of steps is often more compute-efficient than training smaller models to convergence, because larger models converge in far fewer iterations and this acceleration typically outpaces the additional per-iteration cost [arxiv_2002.11794]. This "train large, then compress" paradigm has become a dominant strategy: one trains the largest model that is feasible given training compute budgets, then applies post-training compression to obtain a smaller model suitable for inference. The implication is that compression is not merely a remediation for models that are too large, but an integral part of a rational training and deployment pipeline.

Quantization is arguably the most widely studied and practically deployed compression technique for LLM inference. The core idea is to represent model weights and, in some formulations, activations using lower-precision numerical formats, thereby reducing both memory footprint and the cost of arithmetic operations. For LLMs specifically, quantization presents challenges that are less severe in smaller models: the distribution of weights and activations in very large transformers can be highly non-uniform, with outlier values that are disproportionately important for model quality but difficult to represent accurately at low bit widths [arxiv_2402.09748]. Post-training quantization (PTQ) methods, which apply quantization after training without requiring retraining, are particularly attractive because the cost of retraining LLMs is prohibitive. However, PTQ at very low bit widths—below four bits—tends to incur significant accuracy degradation, and much recent work has focused on closing this gap.

One important line of work addresses the challenge of arbitrary-bit quantization, recognizing that the standard GPU integer computing units support only a limited set of precisions and that this mismatch between the desired quantization level and the available hardware primitives creates inefficiency. ABQ-LLM introduces an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm and inference framework that achieves efficient arbitrary-precision quantized inference on GPU hardware, addressing both the accuracy degradation associated with low-bit quantization and the hardware utilization problem caused by mismatched precision [arxiv_2408.08554]. This work illustrates a broader tension in the quantization literature: the most aggressive compression ratios are often not achievable without co-designing the quantization scheme with the hardware execution engine.

A related challenge concerns the quantization of the key-value (KV) cache rather than the model weights themselves. As context lengths increase, the KV cache can become the dominant consumer of GPU memory, and quantizing it to lower precision is an attractive way to reduce this footprint. The observation that distinct channels of key and value activation embeddings are highly inter-dependent motivates Coupled Quantization (CQ), which couples multiple channels together to exploit their statistical dependencies and encode activations more information-efficiently [arxiv_2405.03917]. The authors demonstrate that this approach enables effective compression to very low bit widths—approaching one bit per channel—where naive per-channel quantization fails. This work highlights the importance of exploiting the statistical structure of activations, not just weights, when designing quantization schemes for inference.

The interaction between quantization and fine-tuning has received considerable attention, because many deployment scenarios require adapting a pre-trained LLM to a specific task or domain. Standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) reduce the number of trainable parameters, but they do not by themselves reduce the memory required to store the base model weights during fine-tuning. Combining quantization with PEFT addresses both the training-time and inference-time memory problems simultaneously. PEQA (Parameter-Efficient and Quantization-aware Adaptation) achieves this by updating only the quantization scales of a pre-quantized model, leaving the quantized weights themselves frozen [arxiv_2305.14152]. This approach is notable because it operates directly on quantized representations rather than requiring a full-precision copy of the weights during fine-tuning. A complementary approach, AlphaTuning, employs binary-coding quantization to factorize full-precision parameters into binary parameters and scaling factors, then fine-tunes only the scaling factors for a target task [arxiv_2210.03858]. Both methods represent attempts to make the "train large, then compress" paradigm compatible with task-specific adaptation, though they differ in the granularity at which they allow the model to be updated.

A more recent contribution, L4Q, integrates Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) with LoRA through a memory-optimized layer design, arguing that the sequential approach of applying PTQ followed by PEFT is suboptimal because the accuracy loss from quantization may be too large for PEFT to recover [arxiv_2402.04902]. By training the quantization parameters jointly with the low-rank adaptation parameters, L4Q aims to find quantized representations that are better suited to the target task from the outset. This work reflects a broader trend toward tighter integration of compression and adaptation, moving away from treating them as independent post-processing steps.

An integrated framework combining GPTQ-based quantization, LoRA, and a specialized data distillation process has been proposed to achieve up to 2x memory compression while preserving task-specific performance [arxiv_2601.09865]. This work employs Bayesian hyperparameter optimization and the Muon optimizer to navigate the complex trade-off space between compression ratio and accuracy, illustrating how the combination of multiple techniques can yield results that none achieves individually. The use of knowledge distillation via Kullback-Leibler divergence as part of the compression pipeline connects the quantization literature to the broader distillation literature, which is discussed further below.

Pruning represents another major family of model-level compression techniques. The basic idea is to identify and remove weights, neurons, attention heads, or entire layers that contribute little to model quality, thereby reducing both the parameter count and the computational cost of inference. For LLMs, structured pruning—which removes entire components such as attention heads or feed-forward neurons—is generally more practically useful than unstructured pruning, because structured sparsity maps directly to reductions in the dimensions of matrix multiplications and thus to actual speedups on standard hardware without requiring specialized sparse kernels [arxiv_2402.09748]. Unstructured pruning, which zeros out individual weights, requires hardware support for sparse computation to realize its theoretical benefits, and this support is not uniformly available across deployment targets.

An empirical investigation of layer-level pruning in transformer LLMs found that skipping the latter attention sublayers is an effective compression strategy, because these layers tend to be redundant while also being computationally expensive [arxiv_2404.05741]. The authors report a 21% speed increase in single-token generation for Llama 2 7B while surprisingly improving performance on several benchmarks, suggesting that the final attention layers in large models may actually be harmful in some settings. This finding connects to a broader body of work on the uneven distribution of information across layers in transformer models, which motivates layer-wise compression strategies that treat different parts of the network differently.

Hardware-aware pruning, which designs the sparsity pattern to match the capabilities of the target hardware, has been demonstrated in the context of CPU deployment. A pipeline for creating fast transformer models on CPUs combines hardware-aware pruning with knowledge distillation, quantization, and an optimized inference runtime with specialized kernels for sparse and quantized operators [arxiv_2211.07715]. This work is notable for its end-to-end perspective: rather than optimizing each component independently, it co-designs the pruning strategy, the distillation procedure, and the runtime to achieve the best overall throughput under latency constraints. The resulting Fast DistilBERT model demonstrates that significant efficiency gains are achievable on CPU hardware, which is relevant for deployment scenarios where GPU resources are unavailable or too expensive.

Knowledge distillation offers a complementary approach to compression that does not directly modify the weights of the target model but instead trains a smaller student model to mimic the behavior of a larger teacher. For LLMs, distillation is complicated by the very high cost of the teacher model and the difficulty of generating the large amounts of training data needed to train a capable student. Nevertheless, distillation remains an important tool, particularly when combined with other techniques. The draft model training framework for speculative decoding described in [arxiv_2403.00858] uses distillation as part of the pipeline for training a small draft model that can generate candidate tokens for verification by a larger target model. The resulting Llama 2 Chat Drafter 115M, with only 1.64% of the parameters of the 7B target model, is trained through a combination of pretraining, distillation dataset generation, and fine-tuning with alignment, demonstrating that distillation can produce highly capable small models when the training pipeline is carefully designed.

Architecture search and compact architecture design represent a less explored but increasingly important direction for efficient LLM inference. Rather than compressing an existing large model, these approaches seek to identify architectures that are intrinsically more efficient while preserving the capabilities of larger models. Traditional neural architecture search (NAS) methods face challenges when applied to LLMs because the search space is enormous and the cost of evaluating candidate architectures is very high. A training-free architecture search framework addresses this challenge by identifying optimal subnets that inherit weights from a pre-trained LLM without requiring any additional training [arxiv_2409.17372]. By treating the large model as a supernet and searching for efficient subnetworks within it, this approach can discover architectures that achieve inference acceleration while preserving the fundamental strengths of the original model.

The combination of low-rank adapters with NAS techniques has been explored as a way to simultaneously compress and adapt LLMs. Low-rank representations, as used in LoRA and related methods, reduce the effective dimensionality of the weight matrices, while NAS can identify which components of the architecture are most important to retain [arxiv_2501.16372]. Weight-sharing super-networks, in which a single large network contains all candidate architectures as subnetworks, enable efficient search by amortizing the cost of training across all candidates. The resulting compressed models exhibit reduced memory footprints and faster inference times while maintaining competitive performance, and the approach is argued to democratize access to LLMs by making them deployable in resource-constrained environments.

The KV cache has emerged as a central focus of the efficient inference literature, particularly as context lengths have grown. During autoregressive generation, the keys and values computed for all previous tokens must be stored and reused at each subsequent step, and the memory required for this cache grows linearly with sequence length and batch size. For long contexts or large batches, the KV cache can consume more memory than the model weights themselves, making it the primary bottleneck for throughput [arxiv_2508.06297]. A comprehensive survey of KV cache optimization techniques organizes them into selective token strategies, quantization, and attention compression, evaluating their effectiveness, trade-offs, and application scenarios [arxiv_2508.06297]. A complementary survey categorizes KV cache management strategies into token-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations, covering KV cache selection, budget allocation, merging, quantization, and low-rank decomposition at the token level, and offloading and hardware-specific optimizations at the system level [arxiv_2412.19442].

Token-level KV cache compression methods seek to identify and retain only the most important key-value pairs, discarding those that contribute little to the attention computation. CORM (Cache Optimization with Recent Message) is motivated by two empirical observations: that adjacent tokens' query vectors are highly similar, and that the attention computation for the current query can rely on the attention information of a small fraction of preceding queries [arxiv_2404.15949]. Based on these observations, CORM implements a KV cache eviction policy that dynamically retains essential key-value pairs for inference, significantly reducing the memory footprint without requiring any modification to the model weights. The reliance on query similarity as a proxy for importance is an interesting design choice that distinguishes CORM from methods that use attention scores directly.

ALISA (Accelerating Large Language Model Inference via Sparsity-Aware KV Caching) addresses the same problem from the perspective of attention sparsity, observing that the attention mechanism in transformer models is naturally sparse—most tokens attend strongly to only a small subset of other tokens [arxiv_2403.17312]. By exploiting this sparsity, ALISA reduces the memory required for the KV cache and alleviates the I/O bottlenecks that arise when the cache must be read from memory at each decoding step. The system is designed for deployment on resource-constrained hardware such as a single commodity GPU, where memory capacity is particularly limited.

GEAR (Generative Inference of LLM with Efficient KV Cache Compression) takes a different approach, arguing that existing methods that either drop tokens or quantize all entries uniformly incur high approximation errors that compound over the autoregressive decoding process [arxiv_2403.05527]. GEAR applies quantization to the majority of entries with similar magnitudes, then uses a low-rank matrix to approximate the residual error, achieving near-lossless high-ratio compression. The combination of quantization and low-rank approximation is a recurring theme in the compression literature, reflecting the complementary strengths of the two approaches: quantization is effective for entries with small magnitudes, while low-rank approximation captures the structured variation in the residual.

InfiniGen addresses the challenge of serving long-text generation by proposing a KV cache management framework that synergistically works with offloading-based inference systems [arxiv_2406.19707]. The key insight is that the important tokens for computing the attention in the next layer can be speculated by performing a minimal rehearsal with the inputs of the current layer and part of the query weight and key cache of the subsequent layer. This allows the system to prefetch only the essential KV entries from CPU memory to GPU memory before they are needed, hiding the latency of the memory transfer behind the computation of the current layer. The speculative prefetching approach is conceptually related to speculative decoding, in that it uses a cheap computation to predict what will be needed in a more expensive subsequent computation.

Layer-Condensed KV Cache proposes a more radical approach: rather than compressing the KV cache after it has been computed, it modifies the model architecture to compute and cache the KVs of only a small number of layers, with other layers reusing the KVs from those layers [arxiv_2405.10637]. This approach achieves up to 26x higher throughput than standard transformers while maintaining competitive performance in language modeling and downstream tasks. The method is orthogonal to existing memory-saving techniques and can be combined with them, and the authors note that it is particularly effective for deep models where the number of layers is large. The Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) takes a related approach, building LLMs from the ground up with a predefined KV cache capacity by implementing a bounded-length KV cache that dynamically updates the key-value vector sequences [arxiv_2411.15785]. Unlike post-hoc compression methods, BCT is designed into the model architecture, which allows it to achieve efficient inference within limited cache capacity without the accuracy degradation that can result from compressing a model that was not designed with bounded cache in mind.

The joint optimization of KV cache compression and model weight compression has been explored in the KV Pareto framework, which systematically maps the trade-off frontier between total memory consumption and task accuracy across KV cache quantization, chunked prefill, and model weight quantization [arxiv_2512.01953]. By evaluating multiple LLM architectures with varying quantization schemes and granularities, the framework identifies model-specific Pareto-optimal configurations that minimize memory usage for a given accuracy target. This systems-level perspective is important because the optimal configuration depends on the specific model, hardware, and accuracy requirements of the deployment scenario, and a framework that can efficiently search this space is more practically useful than one that optimizes a single technique in isolation.

The memory controller design has been proposed as a hardware-level complement to software-level compression techniques. A compression-aware memory controller design enhances the on-chip memory controller in AI accelerators to achieve lossless block compression of model weights and KV cache using standard algorithms such as LZ4 and ZSTD, and to enable memory bandwidth and energy consumption to scale proportionally with context-dependent dynamic quantization [arxiv_2503.18869]. This approach is notable because it achieves memory savings without any degradation in inference quality, unlike lossy compression methods, and because it operates transparently below the software stack, requiring no changes to the model or the inference framework.

Speculative decoding has emerged as a powerful technique for accelerating autoregressive generation without modifying the model or its outputs. The basic idea is to use a small, fast draft model to generate a sequence of candidate tokens, which are then verified in parallel by the large target model [arxiv_2403.00858]. Because the verification step processes multiple tokens simultaneously, it is much more efficient than generating each token independently, and the overall speedup depends on how often the draft model's predictions are accepted by the target model. The key challenge is training a draft model that is both fast and accurate enough to achieve high acceptance rates.

SPIN (Speculative decoding with Heterogeneous Speculative Models) addresses several limitations of existing speculative decoding approaches, including the use of homogeneous draft models that cannot adapt to requests of varying difficulty, the lack of robust support for batch processing, and insufficient optimization of both the speculation and verification phases [arxiv_2503.15921]. SPIN uses multiple heterogeneous draft models and a learning-based algorithm for selecting which draft model to use for each request, enabling the system to adapt to the varying difficulty of different inputs. The system also includes optimizations for the verification phase that improve throughput when processing batches of requests with different speculation lengths. This work illustrates the maturation of speculative decoding from a conceptually simple idea to a complex systems engineering challenge.

CPU-based inference has received attention as a deployment target for scenarios where GPU resources are unavailable or too expensive. Efficient LLM inference on CPUs has been demonstrated through an automatic INT4 weight-only quantization flow combined with a specialized LLM runtime with highly-optimized kernels for LLM inference [arxiv_2311.00502]. The approach is demonstrated on popular LLMs including Llama 2, Llama, and GPT-NeoX, showing that practical inference speeds are achievable on CPU hardware with appropriate quantization and runtime optimization. This work complements the CPU-focused pipeline described in [arxiv_2211.07715], which combines pruning, distillation, quantization, and a custom runtime for transformer models. Together, these works demonstrate that CPU deployment is a viable option for many applications, particularly when the model has been appropriately compressed.

The evaluation of inference efficiency is itself a non-trivial challenge, because the performance of different optimization techniques depends heavily on the hardware, the workload, and the specific metrics being optimized. A coarse-to-fine analysis of the inference performance of various code libraries examines four usage scenarios within two practical applications, providing both theoretical and empirical analyses of each module in the transformer architecture [arxiv_2404.11502]. This work highlights the importance of understanding the underlying mechanisms of inference optimization, not just the headline numbers, because the same technique can have very different effects depending on the context. The TokenSim framework addresses the need for systematic hardware and software exploration by providing a simulation environment for LLM inference systems that supports extensible optimizations including scheduling and memory management [arxiv_2503.08415]. By validating simulation results against real-world datasets with an error rate of less than 1%, TokenSim enables researchers and practitioners to explore the performance implications of different design choices without the expense of running experiments on actual hardware.

The efficiency of inference on AI accelerators more broadly has been surveyed in [arxiv_2407.09111], which provides a comprehensive discussion of complementary inference optimization techniques including quantization, pruning, distillation, and architectural innovations. This tutorial-style treatment is notable for its attention to the practical aspects of deploying optimized models on real hardware, including the importance of matching the optimization strategy to the capabilities of the target accelerator. The survey in [arxiv_2401.02038] takes a broader view, covering both training and inference optimization, and situates inference efficiency within the larger context of the LLM development lifecycle. The authors argue that low-cost training and deployment represent the future development trend for LLMs, and that the techniques discussed in the inference optimization literature are not merely engineering conveniences but are essential for the continued democratization of LLM capabilities.

A recurring theme across the literature is the tension between the generality of optimization techniques and their effectiveness in specific deployment scenarios. Techniques that work well for one model architecture, hardware platform, or workload pattern may be less effective or even counterproductive in other settings. This observation motivates the development of adaptive and context-aware optimization strategies that can adjust their behavior based on the characteristics of the current workload. The SPIN system's use of heterogeneous draft models with learned selection policies is one example of this trend [arxiv_2503.15921]. The KV Pareto framework's systematic mapping of the trade-off frontier is another [arxiv_2512.01953]. The compression-aware memory controller's context-dependent dynamic quantization is a third [arxiv_2503.18869]. These works collectively suggest that the future of efficient LLM inference lies not in finding a single universally optimal configuration but in developing systems that can navigate the optimization space intelligently in response to changing conditions.

The efficiency optimization of large-scale language models has also been analyzed from the perspective of the mathematical principles underlying the key algorithmic components [arxiv_2405.11704]. This analysis examines how the transformer architecture restricts computing efficiency while capturing long-term dependencies, and evaluates the contribution of adaptive optimization algorithms, massively parallel computing techniques, and mixed precision training strategies to training efficiency. On the inference side, the analysis covers model compression techniques including quantization, pruning, and knowledge distillation, providing a unified theoretical framework for understanding their effects. This kind of principled analysis is important for guiding the development of new techniques and for understanding why existing techniques work when they do.

The survey in [arxiv_2408.03130] provides a particularly detailed treatment of the practical challenges associated with each optimization technique, including the engineering difficulties of deploying quantized or pruned models at scale, the compatibility issues between different optimization methods, and the trade-offs between compression ratio, accuracy, and inference speed. The authors organize the discussed methods into a taxonomy that presents an overview of the optimization landscape and helps navigate it to understand the research trajectory. This practical orientation complements the more theoretically focused analyses in other surveys and is valuable for practitioners who need to make concrete deployment decisions.

An important dimension of the efficient inference problem that has received increasing attention is the interaction between optimization techniques and the specific characteristics of LLM workloads in production. Production LLM serving systems must handle requests with widely varying lengths, complexity, and latency requirements, often from many concurrent users. The batch processing capabilities of different optimization techniques vary considerably: some techniques, such as standard quantization, are straightforwardly compatible with batching, while others, such as speculative decoding, require more careful engineering to work efficiently with variable-length batches [arxiv_2503.15921]. The scheduling and memory management strategies used by the serving system can have a large impact on throughput and latency, independent of the model-level optimizations applied [arxiv_2503.08415].

The relationship between model compression and the emergent capabilities of LLMs is another important consideration. Large language models exhibit capabilities that emerge at scale and that are not present in smaller models, and there is concern that aggressive compression may disproportionately degrade these capabilities even when overall benchmark performance is maintained. The literature on this question is still developing, but several works note that the accuracy of compressed models on standard benchmarks may not fully capture their performance on tasks that require reasoning or in-context learning [arxiv_2402.09748]. This observation motivates the development of more comprehensive evaluation protocols for compressed LLMs and suggests that the choice of evaluation benchmark can significantly affect conclusions about the effectiveness of different compression techniques.

The question of how to efficiently fine-tune compressed LLMs for specific tasks is closely related to the broader question of how to make LLMs more accessible for deployment in resource-constrained environments. The combination of low-rank adapters with NAS techniques [arxiv_2501.16372] and the integration of quantization-aware training with LoRA [arxiv_2402.04902] both address this question from different angles. The former focuses on finding efficient architectures, while the latter focuses on finding efficient representations of the weights within a fixed architecture. Both approaches recognize that the cost of fine-tuning is a significant barrier to the widespread adoption of LLMs and that reducing this cost is as important as reducing the cost of inference.

The efficiency optimization landscape for LLMs is thus characterized by a rich interplay between model-level, data-level, and system-level techniques, each addressing different aspects of the inference bottleneck. Quantization reduces memory footprint and arithmetic cost; pruning removes redundant computation; distillation transfers knowledge to smaller models; architecture search identifies intrinsically efficient designs; KV cache optimization addresses the memory bottleneck specific to autoregressive generation; speculative decoding accelerates the sequential generation process; and system-level optimizations improve hardware utilization and scheduling efficiency. The most effective deployments combine multiple techniques from these categories, and the development of frameworks for systematically exploring the resulting trade-off space is an active and important area of research [arxiv_2512.01953, arxiv_2503.08415].

Looking across the surveyed literature, several broad conclusions emerge. First, no single optimization technique is universally dominant; the best approach depends on the specific model, hardware, workload, and accuracy requirements of the deployment scenario. Second, the interaction between different optimization techniques is complex and not always additive; combining techniques can yield synergistic benefits but can also introduce compatibility issues or unexpected accuracy degradation. Third, the evaluation of inference efficiency requires careful attention to the metrics being optimized and the conditions under which they are measured; headline numbers from individual papers are often not directly comparable because they are measured under different conditions. Fourth, the field is moving rapidly, with new techniques being proposed at a high rate, and the relative merits of different approaches are likely to shift as hardware capabilities evolve and as the scale and diversity of LLM applications continue to grow. Fifth, the democratization of LLM capabilities—making powerful models accessible to users and organizations without access to large-scale GPU infrastructure—is an important motivating goal for much of the efficient inference literature, and progress toward this goal requires advances on multiple fronts simultaneously [arxiv_2501.16372, arxiv_2402.09748, arxiv_2401.02038].

The preceding discussion has established the broad contours of the efficient inference landscape, but several important threads merit deeper examination. One such thread concerns the hardware-software co-design perspective, which has grown increasingly prominent as researchers recognize that algorithmic innovations alone cannot fully close the gap between the computational demands of large language models and the capabilities of available hardware. The work presented in [arxiv_2503.18869] exemplifies this perspective by proposing modifications to the on-chip memory controller of AI accelerators to better accommodate the access patterns induced by compressed LLM weights. Rather than treating the memory subsystem as a fixed constraint to be worked around by algorithmic means, this approach treats it as a design variable that can be co-optimized with the compression strategy. The resulting system achieves reductions in effective memory bandwidth demand that complement those obtained through quantization and pruning, suggesting that the full benefits of compression can only be realized when the hardware is designed to exploit the structure that compression introduces. This insight has broader implications for the design of next-generation AI accelerators, where the memory hierarchy, interconnect topology, and compute units may all need to be co-designed with the compression and serving strategies that will be used in deployment.

The simulation-based approach to hardware-software co-exploration is further developed in [arxiv_2503.08415], which introduces TokenSim as a framework for systematically evaluating the interaction between hardware configurations and software-level serving strategies for LLM inference. By enabling rapid simulation of diverse hardware and software configurations without requiring physical deployment, TokenSim allows researchers and system designers to identify bottlenecks and evaluate the potential impact of proposed optimizations before committing to costly hardware or software changes. This kind of tooling is particularly valuable given the diversity of deployment scenarios for LLMs, ranging from cloud-scale data centers with thousands of high-end GPUs to edge devices with severely constrained memory and compute budgets. The ability to simulate and compare configurations across this range is essential for making principled design decisions and for understanding how the relative merits of different optimization techniques depend on the underlying hardware.

Closely related to hardware-software co-design is the question of how to evaluate inference efficiency in a rigorous and reproducible way. The work in [arxiv_2404.11502] addresses this question directly, proposing a coarse-to-fine evaluation framework that decomposes inference efficiency into multiple levels of granularity, from high-level metrics such as end-to-end latency and throughput to fine-grained metrics such as per-layer computation time and memory access patterns. This hierarchical approach to evaluation is important because it allows researchers to identify the specific bottlenecks that limit efficiency in a given deployment scenario and to assess whether a proposed optimization actually addresses those bottlenecks or merely shifts them elsewhere. The authors demonstrate that headline metrics such as tokens per second can be misleading when compared across different hardware platforms or batch sizes, and that a more nuanced evaluation is necessary to draw valid conclusions about the relative merits of different optimization techniques. This observation reinforces the broader point made in the preceding discussion about the difficulty of comparing results across papers and highlights the need for standardized evaluation protocols in the efficient inference literature.

The challenge of deploying LLMs on CPU-based infrastructure, rather than GPU clusters, represents a particularly important practical dimension of the efficient inference problem. While GPUs dominate large-scale LLM training and serving, many deployment scenarios—including on-premises enterprise deployments, edge computing applications, and cost-sensitive production systems—require efficient inference on CPUs. The work in [arxiv_2311.00502] addresses this challenge by proposing an INT4 weight-only quantization flow combined with a specialized LLM runtime optimized for CPU execution. The key insight is that the memory bandwidth constraints of CPUs make weight-only quantization particularly attractive, since the bottleneck in CPU-based LLM inference is typically the cost of loading model weights from memory rather than the cost of arithmetic operations. By reducing the precision of weights to four bits while retaining higher precision for activations, the proposed approach achieves substantial speedups on CPU hardware with minimal accuracy degradation. This work complements the earlier study of CPU-based inference for smaller transformer models in [arxiv_2211.07715], which demonstrated that the combination of quantization and pruning can enable production-grade throughput on CPU hardware for models such as DistilBERT, and suggests that similar techniques can be extended to much larger models with appropriate engineering effort.

The question of how to handle the diversity of quantization bit widths and hardware support for different integer formats is addressed in [arxiv_2408.08554], which proposes a framework for arbitrary-bit quantized inference acceleration. Standard hardware typically provides efficient support for specific integer formats such as INT8 and INT4, but research has shown that intermediate bit widths such as INT3 or INT2 can offer better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs for certain models and tasks. The ABQ-LLM framework addresses the challenge of deploying models quantized to non-standard bit widths by providing a flexible software abstraction that maps arbitrary-bit quantized operations to efficient implementations on available hardware. This approach decouples the choice of quantization bit width from the constraints of hardware support, enabling researchers and practitioners to explore a wider range of accuracy-efficiency trade-offs without being limited to the bit widths natively supported by their hardware. The framework also addresses the challenge of performance degradation at very low bit widths by incorporating techniques for mitigating quantization error, including mixed-precision quantization and learned quantization parameters.

The interaction between quantization and fine-tuning has been explored from multiple angles in the literature. The work in [arxiv_2305.14152] focuses specifically on the challenge of fine-tuning models that have been compressed to sub-4-bit precision, demonstrating that standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA can be applied to heavily quantized models with appropriate modifications. The key challenge is that the gradient flow through quantized weights is discontinuous, making standard backpropagation difficult to apply directly. The proposed approach addresses this by using straight-through estimators and other techniques from the quantization-aware training literature to enable effective fine-tuning of sub-4-bit models. This work is important because it demonstrates that the benefits of aggressive quantization need not come at the cost of adaptability; heavily compressed models can still be fine-tuned for specific tasks, enabling the combination of low inference cost with task-specific performance. The complementary work in [arxiv_2210.03858] proposes AlphaTuning, which combines quantization-aware adaptation with parameter-efficient fine-tuning by selectively quantizing different components of the model based on their sensitivity to quantization error. This sensitivity-aware approach achieves better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs than uniform quantization and provides a principled framework for deciding which parts of the model to compress most aggressively.

The L4Q framework presented in [arxiv_2402.04902] further advances the integration of quantization and parameter-efficient fine-tuning by proposing a joint optimization framework that simultaneously minimizes quantization error and fine-tuning loss. Unlike approaches that treat quantization and fine-tuning as sequential steps, L4Q optimizes both objectives together, allowing the fine-tuning process to compensate for quantization-induced accuracy loss and vice versa. The authors demonstrate that this joint optimization approach achieves better results than either quantization or fine-tuning alone, particularly at very low bit widths where the accuracy degradation from quantization is most severe. This work exemplifies a broader trend in the efficient inference literature toward integrated approaches that treat multiple optimization objectives simultaneously rather than addressing them in isolation.

The Muon-optimized distillation and quantization framework described in [arxiv_2601.09865] represents another integrated approach, combining GPTQ-based quantization with LoRA fine-tuning and knowledge distillation within a unified deployment pipeline. The use of the Muon optimizer, which applies second-order information to accelerate convergence, is particularly notable as it addresses one of the practical challenges of quantization-aware training: the difficulty of optimizing in the presence of the non-smooth loss landscape induced by quantization. By combining distillation with quantization, the framework allows the compressed model to benefit from the soft targets provided by the teacher model, which carry more information about the model's output distribution than hard labels and can help the student model recover accuracy lost to quantization. This combination of techniques is representative of the direction in which the field is moving, toward integrated pipelines that leverage multiple complementary optimization strategies rather than relying on any single technique.

Returning to the question of KV cache optimization, it is worth examining in greater depth the diversity of approaches that have been proposed and the trade-offs between them. The survey in [arxiv_2508.06297] provides a systematic taxonomy of KV cache optimization techniques, organizing them into categories including token eviction, quantization, low-rank compression, and architectural modifications. Each category addresses the KV cache memory bottleneck from a different angle and has different implications for accuracy, implementation complexity, and compatibility with other optimizations. Token eviction methods, such as those discussed in [arxiv_2403.17312] and [arxiv_2404.15949], reduce memory consumption by selectively discarding KV pairs for tokens that are deemed less important for future attention computations. The challenge with these methods is defining an appropriate importance criterion; different criteria lead to different accuracy-efficiency trade-offs, and the optimal criterion may depend on the specific task and input distribution.

The GEAR framework proposed in [arxiv_2403.05527] takes a different approach to KV cache compression, using a combination of low-rank approximation and sparse residual representation to achieve near-lossless compression of the KV cache. Unlike token eviction methods, which discard information entirely, GEAR retains a compressed representation of all tokens, allowing the model to recover information that would be lost by eviction. The authors demonstrate that this approach achieves substantially lower approximation error than uniform quantization or token eviction at the same compression ratio, particularly for tasks that require attending to tokens distributed throughout the context. The coupled quantization approach in [arxiv_2405.03917] similarly addresses the limitations of independent per-channel quantization by exploiting correlations between different channels of the KV cache, achieving effective compression to approximately one bit per channel without the severe accuracy degradation that would be expected from such aggressive quantization applied independently to each channel.

The LoRC framework in [arxiv_2410.03111] addresses KV cache compression through progressive low-rank decomposition, which allows the compression ratio to be adapted dynamically based on the available memory budget. This progressive approach is particularly valuable in deployment scenarios where the memory budget varies over time, for example due to varying batch sizes or context lengths, since it allows the system to maintain the best possible accuracy within the available memory rather than committing to a fixed compression ratio at design time. The layer-condensed KV cache approach in [arxiv_2405.10637] takes a more radical approach by eliminating the KV cache for most layers entirely, computing and caching KV pairs only for a small subset of layers and reusing them across the remaining layers. This approach achieves dramatic reductions in KV cache memory consumption but requires modifications to the model architecture and training procedure, making it most applicable to models trained from scratch with this constraint in mind.

The Bounded-Cache Transformer (BCT) introduced in [arxiv_2411.15785] similarly addresses the KV cache memory problem through an architectural modification, implementing a fixed-capacity KV cache that is dynamically updated as new tokens are generated. Unlike approaches that compress or evict KV pairs from a cache of unbounded size, the BCT maintains a cache of fixed size from the outset, ensuring that memory consumption does not grow with sequence length. This property is particularly valuable for deployment scenarios with strict memory constraints, such as edge devices or systems serving many concurrent users. The dynamic update mechanism used by the BCT determines which KV pairs to retain and which to discard as new tokens are generated, effectively implementing an online eviction policy that is learned during training rather than specified by hand. The comprehensive survey in [arxiv_2412.19442] situates these and other KV cache management techniques within a broader framework, analyzing their relative merits and identifying open research questions, including the challenge of developing eviction policies that are robust across diverse tasks and input distributions.

The InfiniGen framework described in [arxiv_2406.19707] addresses the KV cache management problem specifically in the context of long-text generation, where the memory footprint of the KV cache is most severe. InfiniGen uses a dynamic management strategy that prefetches KV pairs from CPU memory to GPU memory based on predicted attention patterns, allowing the effective context length to exceed the GPU memory capacity without requiring the model to discard any KV pairs permanently. This approach leverages the observation that attention patterns in LLMs are often sparse and predictable, so that the KV pairs needed for future attention computations can be anticipated and loaded into GPU memory before they are needed. The ALISA framework in [arxiv_2403.17312] similarly exploits the sparsity of attention patterns to reduce KV cache memory consumption, using a sparsity-aware caching strategy that retains only the KV pairs that are likely to receive significant attention weight. The YOCO++ architecture described in [arxiv_2604.13556] takes yet another approach, using cross-layer KV sharing with residual connections to reduce the total number of unique KV pairs that need to be stored, achieving memory savings while mitigating the performance degradation that can result from naive cross-layer sharing.

The KV Pareto framework in [arxiv_2512.01953] provides a systems-level perspective on the joint optimization of KV cache compression and model weight compression, mapping the Pareto frontier between total memory consumption and model accuracy for a range of combined compression strategies. This work makes explicit the trade-offs that arise when multiple compression techniques are applied simultaneously and provides a principled framework for selecting the combination of techniques that best satisfies the constraints of a given deployment scenario. The authors find that the optimal combination of KV cache and weight compression depends strongly on the target hardware and the length of the contexts being processed, reinforcing the broader point that there is no universally optimal compression strategy and that deployment decisions must be made with careful attention to the specific requirements of the application.

Speculative decoding has emerged as one of the most promising approaches for accelerating the sequential token generation process that is inherent to autoregressive LLMs, and the literature on this topic has grown substantially in recent years. The core idea, as reviewed in [arxiv_2402.09748] and [arxiv_2408.03130], is to use a smaller, faster draft model to generate candidate tokens that are then verified in parallel by the larger target model, achieving a speedup whenever the draft model's predictions are accepted. The practical effectiveness of this approach depends critically on the alignment between the draft model and the target model, and the work in [arxiv_2403.00858] addresses this challenge by proposing a direct alignment procedure that fine-tunes the draft model to match the output distribution of a chat-fine-tuned target model. This alignment procedure is particularly important when the target model has been fine-tuned for a specific task or style, since a draft model trained on general text may produce candidates that are systematically rejected by the fine-tuned target model, negating the speedup that speculative decoding is intended to provide.

The Minions framework in [arxiv_2402.15678] extends the speculative decoding paradigm by using aggregated speculative execution across multiple small models, allowing the system to exploit the diversity of different draft models to improve the acceptance rate and thus the overall speedup. The SPIN framework in [arxiv_2503.15921] addresses the challenge of handling requests with varying difficulty by using heterogeneous speculative models, selecting the draft model based on the estimated difficulty of each request. This adaptive approach avoids the inefficiency of using a powerful draft model for easy requests, where a simpler model would suffice, and of using a weak draft model for difficult requests, where a more capable model is needed to achieve a high acceptance rate. The ALISE framework in [arxiv_2410.23537] complements these model-level innovations with a scheduling-level approach, using speculative scheduling to predict the output length of each request and schedule requests accordingly, reducing head-of-line blocking and improving overall system throughput.

The broader question of how to optimize the serving infrastructure for LLM inference, beyond the model-level optimizations discussed above, is addressed in [arxiv_2401.08294], which presents Inferflow as a highly configurable inference engine that supports a wide range of transformer model architectures and optimization techniques. The modular design of Inferflow allows users to combine different optimization techniques—including quantization, tensor parallelism, and pipeline parallelism—without writing custom code, lowering the barrier to deploying optimized LLM inference in production. This kind of flexible, composable serving infrastructure is increasingly important as the diversity of LLM architectures and deployment scenarios continues to grow, and as the combination of multiple optimization techniques becomes the norm rather than the exception.

The efficiency optimization of LLMs from a deep learning perspective is analyzed in [arxiv_2405.11704], which provides a theoretical treatment of the computational bottlenecks in transformer architectures and evaluates the contribution of different optimization strategies to addressing them. The analysis covers both the training and inference phases, identifying the specific operations that dominate computational cost in each phase and evaluating the effectiveness of techniques such as mixed-precision training, adaptive optimization algorithms, and massively parallel computing in reducing these costs. This theoretical grounding is valuable for understanding why certain optimization techniques are more effective than others and for identifying the fundamental limits of what can be achieved through algorithmic optimization alone, as opposed to hardware improvements.

The comprehensive study in [arxiv_2407.15904] bridges the gap between traditional deep learning model compression and the specific challenges of compressing LLMs, providing a systematic comparison of compression techniques across both domains. The authors find that while many of the same techniques—quantization, pruning, distillation—apply in both settings, the scale and architecture of LLMs introduce unique challenges that require specialized adaptations of these techniques. In particular, the sensitivity of LLMs to quantization error varies dramatically across layers and attention heads, requiring more sophisticated mixed-precision strategies than are typically used for smaller models. Similarly, the structured pruning of LLMs requires careful attention to the interdependencies between different components of the model, since removing a head or layer can have cascading effects on the model's ability to perform certain tasks. The survey in [arxiv_2505.02309] provides a complementary perspective focused specifically on resource-constrained environments, evaluating the applicability of different compression techniques for deployment on mobile and edge devices where memory and compute budgets are most severely limited.

The architectural search perspective on efficient LLMs, as discussed in [arxiv_2409.17372] and [arxiv_2501.16372], represents an important complement to the post-hoc compression approaches that dominate the literature. Rather than starting with a large, over-parameterized model and compressing it, architecture search aims to identify intrinsically efficient architectures that achieve good performance with fewer parameters and operations. The work in [arxiv_2409.17372] demonstrates that architecture search can identify models that are more efficient than those obtained by compressing larger models, suggesting that the common practice of training large models and then compressing them may not always be optimal. This finding is consistent with the earlier analysis in [arxiv_2002.11794], which showed that the relationship between model size, training efficiency, and inference efficiency is complex and that the optimal model size depends on the specific constraints of the deployment scenario. The integration of low-rank adapters with neural architecture search in [arxiv_2501.16372] further demonstrates that these two approaches can be combined synergistically, with the architecture search identifying efficient structural configurations and the low-rank adapters providing efficient parameterizations of the weights within those configurations.

The thesis work in [arxiv_2404.05741] provides an empirical perspective on the effectiveness of different compression techniques for LLMs, demonstrating through systematic experiments that simple techniques such as skipping latter attention sublayers can achieve substantial efficiency gains with minimal accuracy degradation. This finding is noteworthy because it suggests that not all of the complexity of large transformer models is equally important for performance, and that targeted removal of less important components can yield efficiency gains that are disproportionate to the reduction in model size. This observation is consistent with the broader literature on model redundancy in LLMs, which has shown that large models contain substantial redundancy that can be exploited by compression techniques without significant accuracy loss. The challenge is identifying which components are redundant in a given model and for a given task, which requires either expensive sensitivity analysis or the development of more principled theoretical frameworks for understanding the role of different components in the model's computations.

The dynamic memory compression approach in [arxiv_2403.09636] addresses the KV cache bottleneck through a learned online compression strategy that adapts the compression ratio dynamically based on the content of the input. Unlike static compression strategies that apply a fixed compression ratio to all inputs, dynamic compression can allocate more memory to inputs that require longer context and less memory to inputs that can be processed with a shorter effective context. This adaptivity is particularly valuable in production serving scenarios where the distribution of input lengths is highly variable, since a static compression strategy must be designed for the worst case and will therefore be suboptimal for the majority of inputs. The authors demonstrate that the model can learn effective compression policies through training, without requiring hand-designed importance criteria or explicit supervision on which tokens to compress.

Taken together, the body of work reviewed in this section reveals a field that is simultaneously broad in scope and deep in technical detail. The efficient inference problem for LLMs is not a single problem but a family of related problems, each arising from a different aspect of the mismatch between the computational demands of large models and the constraints of available hardware and deployment scenarios. Progress on this family of problems requires contributions from multiple disciplines, including machine learning, systems engineering, computer architecture, and numerical methods, and the most impactful advances tend to be those that bridge multiple disciplines simultaneously. The continued growth of LLM capabilities and the expanding diversity of deployment scenarios ensure that efficient inference will remain an active and important research area for the foreseeable future, with ample opportunities for both incremental improvements to existing techniques and more fundamental innovations in model design, hardware architecture, and serving infrastructure.
