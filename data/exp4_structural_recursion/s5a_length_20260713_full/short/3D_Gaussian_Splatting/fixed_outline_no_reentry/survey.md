# 3D Gaussian Splatting (short)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) represents a pivotal shift in the landscape of novel view synthesis and 3D scene representation, fundamentally altering the trajectory of research that had previously been dominated by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). To appreciate the significance of this transition, it is necessary to situate 3DGS within the broader historical context of radiance field methods and to examine the core technical principles that distinguish it from its predecessors.

Neural Radiance Fields, introduced in 2020, revolutionized computer vision by enabling implicit, neural network-based scene representation and novel view synthesis [arxiv_2210.00379]. NeRF-based methods encode a 3D scene through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks, queried via ray-marching to produce volumetric renderings of high visual fidelity. However, this principled approach comes at a substantial computational cost: training is slow, and inference requires dense network evaluations along each camera ray, making real-time rendering at high resolutions effectively intractable [arxiv_2308.04079]. While subsequent NeRF variants sought to address these limitations, they typically traded rendering quality for speed, and no method prior to 3DGS achieved real-time display rates for unbounded scenes at 1080p resolution [arxiv_2308.04079].

The seminal work introducing 3D Gaussian Splatting [arxiv_2308.04079] addressed these shortcomings by proposing an explicit scene representation based on a set of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, initialized from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration. Rather than relying on costly neural network inference, the scene is rendered by rasterizing these Gaussian primitives directly onto the image plane, enabling high-quality novel view synthesis at frame rates exceeding 30 fps at 1080p resolution. This approach preserves the desirable properties of continuous volumetric radiance fields for optimization purposes while dramatically accelerating rendering through the efficiency of primitive-based rasterization [arxiv_2308.04079]. The impact was immediate and far-reaching: as documented in comprehensive surveys of the field, Gaussian Splatting rapidly overtook NeRF-based research as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis following its introduction in August 2023 [arxiv_2210.00379].

The rapid adoption of 3DGS has been accompanied by a proliferation of derivative works spanning reconstruction, editing, and downstream applications. Survey literature characterizes 3DGS methods along these functional axes, noting that the explicit nature of the Gaussian representation—in contrast to the implicit encoding of NeRF—facilitates a range of editing tasks including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry manipulation, and physical simulation [arxiv_2403.11134]. This editability constitutes one of the most practically significant advantages of the explicit paradigm, as it enables direct manipulation of scene elements in ways that are considerably more challenging with neural implicit representations.

Despite its successes, the rendering pipeline of 3DGS involves a set of approximations relative to principled volumetric rendering theory. While 3DGS shares a similar image formation model with NeRF, it adopts a hybrid solution that blends volume rendering concepts with primitive rasterization, introducing approximations for the sake of computational efficiency [arxiv_2502.19318]. A natural and important question that has emerged in subsequent research is whether replacing these approximations with more rigorous volumetric rendering solutions would yield improvements in reconstruction quality, or whether the approximations are in fact sufficient or even beneficial [arxiv_2502.19318]. This tension between theoretical rigor and practical performance reflects a broader theme in the evolution of 3DGS: the field has grown rapidly not only by extending the method's capabilities but also by revisiting and interrogating its foundational assumptions. Together, these threads—historical context, core technical innovation, downstream extensibility, and critical re-examination of rendering fidelity—define the intellectual landscape within which contemporary 3DGS research is situated.

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has prompted a substantial body of research addressing its core limitations, particularly those related to memory efficiency, storage overhead, and robustness to imperfect input data. While 3DGS offers compelling advantages over Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) approaches in terms of real-time rendering and training speed, the field has rapidly identified several structural weaknesses that constrain its practical deployment.

A central concern is the memory and storage burden imposed by 3DGS's reliance on explicit per-Gaussian parameters. Because high-quality scene reconstruction typically requires millions of Gaussians, each carrying attributes such as position, covariance, opacity, and spherical harmonic coefficients, the aggregate memory footprint becomes prohibitive for resource-constrained environments. This problem is acknowledged across multiple lines of work. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] addresses it through quantized embeddings that reduce per-point storage requirements, combined with a coarse-to-fine training strategy that stabilizes optimization and a pruning stage that reduces the total number of Gaussians, yielding faster training and rendering without proportional quality loss. Similarly, F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083] draws on classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a factorized coordinate and representation space, dramatically reducing storage while preserving image quality. Both approaches share the intuition that the redundancy inherent in large Gaussian point clouds can be exploited for compression, though they differ in mechanism: EAGLES operates at the level of individual Gaussian attribute encoding, whereas F-3DGS targets the structural organization of Gaussian clusters through factorization.

A more architecturally ambitious response to the memory problem is offered by HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083], which argues that purely compressing explicit Gaussians is insufficient because such methods struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details. HyRF instead proposes a hybrid representation that decomposes the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians responsible for critical high-frequency parameters and grid-based neural fields that predict the remaining properties. This hybrid strategy attempts to combine the rendering efficiency of explicit representations with the expressive capacity of neural fields, positioning itself as a qualitative step beyond compression-only approaches like EAGLES and F-3DGS. The distinction is meaningful: where EAGLES and F-3DGS seek to retain the 3DGS paradigm while reducing its footprint, HyRF questions whether a purely explicit representation can ever adequately model certain scene properties without neural assistance.

Beyond memory and storage, the quality of 3DGS reconstruction is also sensitive to the quality of training data. Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting [arxiv_2401.00834] identifies a distinct failure mode: when training images are corrupted by blur arising from lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake, standard 3DGS suffers severe degradation in rendering quality. This work situates itself within a broader tradition of neural field methods that attempt to recover sharp scene representations from blurry inputs, but adapts that goal specifically to the 3DGS framework, where the explicit and differentiable nature of Gaussian rasterization opens different avenues for blur modeling compared to volumetric NeRF approaches.

Taken together, these works reveal that the 3DGS literature is simultaneously pursuing improvements along at least two orthogonal axes: reducing the computational and storage cost of the representation itself [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2405.17083, arxiv_2509.17083] and improving robustness to real-world data imperfections [arxiv_2401.00834]. The tension between representational compactness and reconstruction fidelity, particularly for high-frequency scene content, remains a central open challenge, with hybrid approaches [arxiv_2509.17083] representing one promising direction for reconciling these competing demands.
