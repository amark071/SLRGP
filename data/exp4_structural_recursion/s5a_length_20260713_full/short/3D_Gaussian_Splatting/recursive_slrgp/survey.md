# 3D Gaussian Splatting (short)

### Foundational Methods and Surveys of 3DGS

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most significant developments in scene representation and rendering research in recent years. Prior to its introduction, Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) had established themselves as the dominant framework for implicit neural scene representation, leveraging position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks to synthesize photorealistic novel views [arxiv_2210.00379]. While NeRF-based methods achieved impressive visual fidelity, they were fundamentally constrained by the computational demands of ray-marching-based volumetric rendering, resulting in training and inference times that precluded real-time applications [arxiv_2308.04079]. The introduction of 3DGS in 2023 directly addressed these limitations, rapidly gaining momentum and, according to survey evidence, overtaking NeRF-based research as the dominant framework for novel view synthesis [arxiv_2210.00379].

The foundational contribution of 3DGS, as introduced by Kerbl et al. [arxiv_2308.04079], rests on three key technical elements. Rather than encoding scene geometry and appearance implicitly within neural network weights, 3DGS represents a scene as a collection of 3D Gaussian ellipsoids, initialized from sparse point clouds produced during camera calibration. These ellipsoids are then rasterized directly into images, enabling high-quality novel view synthesis at real-time frame rates of 30 frames per second or greater at 1080p resolution — a benchmark that no prior method had achieved for unbounded, complete scenes [arxiv_2308.04079]. This shift from implicit to explicit scene representation is consequential not only for rendering speed but also for the broader utility of the representation. As noted in subsequent survey work, the explicit nature of 3D Gaussians facilitates downstream editing tasks including dynamic scene reconstruction, geometry manipulation, and physical simulation — capabilities that are considerably more difficult to realize within the implicit NeRF paradigm [arxiv_2403.11134].

The rapid proliferation of work building upon 3DGS has prompted several comprehensive literature reviews. Fei et al. [arxiv_2403.11134] organize the growing body of 3DGS research into three broad functional categories: 3D reconstruction, 3D editing, and other downstream applications, reflecting the versatility of the representation across diverse problem settings. This taxonomic framing underscores how quickly the field has expanded beyond the original rendering-focused contribution into areas such as scene understanding, generation, and simulation.

A particularly important theoretical question concerns the rendering fidelity of 3DGS relative to the more principled volumetric rendering framework underlying NeRF. While both approaches share a similar image formation model, 3DGS achieves its performance advantages through a hybrid solution that combines elements of volume rendering with primitive rasterization, incorporating several approximations with respect to strict volumetric rendering theory [arxiv_2502.19318]. This raises the question of whether replacing these approximations with more rigorous volumetric rendering solutions would yield meaningful quality improvements. Recent work has undertaken an in-depth analysis of precisely this question, examining whether the approximations inherent to 3DGS are a significant limiting factor or whether they are largely inconsequential for practical rendering quality [arxiv_2502.19318]. Such investigations are important for understanding the theoretical foundations of the method and for guiding future improvements.

Taken together, the literature establishes 3DGS as a transformative approach that successfully reconciles the competing demands of rendering speed and visual quality. Its explicit Gaussian representation offers practical advantages over neural implicit methods in terms of both computational efficiency and editability, while ongoing theoretical scrutiny continues to refine understanding of its rendering approximations and their consequences for scene fidelity [arxiv_2308.04079; arxiv_2403.11134; arxiv_2502.19318].

### Compression, Efficiency, and Memory Optimization

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a scene representation paradigm has prompted a substantial body of work addressing its core limitations, particularly those concerning memory efficiency, storage overhead, and robustness to imperfect input data. While 3DGS offers compelling advantages over Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) approaches in terms of real-time rendering and training speed, the explicit, per-Gaussian parameterization that enables these advantages simultaneously introduces significant resource demands that have motivated numerous subsequent refinements.

A central concern in the literature is the memory and storage cost incurred by representing scenes with millions of individual Gaussians, each carrying its own set of parameters. EAGLES [arxiv_2312.04564] directly confronts this issue by introducing quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements, complemented by a coarse-to-fine training strategy designed to accelerate and stabilize optimization. Crucially, EAGLES also incorporates a pruning stage that yields scene representations with fewer Gaussians overall, thereby improving both training times and rendering speeds without sacrificing visual fidelity. This approach treats compression primarily as a quantization and pruning problem, operating directly on the Gaussian point cloud itself.

F-3DGS [arxiv_2405.17083] takes a structurally different route to the same storage problem, drawing inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques. Rather than quantizing individual Gaussian attributes, F-3DGS represents and approximates dense clusters of Gaussians through factorized coordinates and representations, drastically reducing storage requirements while aiming to preserve image quality. The factorization perspective is conceptually distinct from the quantization approach of EAGLES, as it seeks to exploit redundancy across groups of Gaussians rather than compressing the representation of each Gaussian individually.

HyRF [arxiv_2509.17083] offers a more architecturally hybrid solution to the memory problem. Its central observation is that while prior works propose compressing 3DGS with neural fields, such methods struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details. To address this, HyRF decomposes the scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians that store only critical high-frequency parameters, while grid-based neural fields predict the remaining properties. This hybrid design attempts to combine the representational strengths of explicit Gaussians with the compactness of neural field encodings, positioning itself as a response to the limitations of purely neural compression strategies rather than purely explicit ones. In this sense, HyRF can be read as a synthesis of the two paradigms that EAGLES and F-3DGS each represent in more isolated form.

Beyond memory and storage, the quality of 3DGS reconstructions is also sensitive to the quality of training data. Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting [arxiv_2401.00834] addresses the specific degradation that occurs when training images are affected by blur arising from lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake. The work notes that while several prior studies have attempted to render sharp images from blurry inputs using neural fields, 3DGS suffers from severe quality degradation under such conditions and requires dedicated treatment. By extending the 3DGS framework to handle blurry training inputs, this work broadens the practical applicability of the approach to real-world capture scenarios where perfect image acquisition cannot be guaranteed.

Taken together, these works illustrate that the 3DGS literature has evolved along at least two parallel axes: one concerned with reducing the resource footprint of Gaussian-based representations through quantization, factorization, or hybrid neural-explicit architectures [arxiv_2312.04564, arxiv_2405.17083, arxiv_2509.17083], and another concerned with improving robustness to degraded or imperfect input conditions [arxiv_2401.00834]. Both axes reflect the broader goal of making 3DGS viable across a wider range of deployment scenarios and input modalities.
