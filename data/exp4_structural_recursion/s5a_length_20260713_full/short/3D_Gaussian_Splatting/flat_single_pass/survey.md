# 3D Gaussian Splatting (short)

The emergence of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as a dominant paradigm for novel view synthesis represents one of the most significant shifts in scene representation research in recent years. Introduced by Kerbl and colleagues, the foundational method models a three-dimensional scene using a set of explicit Gaussian ellipsoids, each parameterized by position, covariance, opacity, and view-dependent color encoded through spherical harmonics [arxiv_2308.04079]. By rasterizing these Gaussian primitives directly onto the image plane rather than relying on computationally expensive ray-marching procedures, the approach achieves real-time rendering at 1080p resolution at frame rates exceeding 30 frames per second, a threshold that prior neural rendering methods had consistently failed to meet for unbounded, complete scenes [arxiv_2308.04079]. This fundamental departure from the implicit, network-centric paradigm of Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) catalyzed an extraordinarily rapid expansion of follow-on research, with the field diversifying into reconstruction, editing, compression, and a wide array of downstream applications within a remarkably short period [arxiv_2403.11134].

To appreciate the significance of 3DGS, it is instructive to situate it against the NeRF landscape it largely supplanted. NeRF, introduced in 2020, represented scenes implicitly through position- and viewpoint-conditioned neural networks queried via volumetric ray marching, enabling photorealistic novel view synthesis but at substantial computational cost during both training and inference [arxiv_2210.00379]. The years following NeRF's introduction saw extensive work on accelerating training and rendering, yet a persistent trade-off between speed and quality remained. The post-Gaussian Splatting era, as characterized in comprehensive surveys of the field, saw research interest shift decisively toward 3DGS-based frameworks, which offered explicit, directly optimizable scene representations amenable to real-time rasterization [arxiv_2210.00379]. Unlike NeRF's continuous volumetric representation, the explicit nature of 3DGS facilitates not only fast rendering but also scene manipulation, making it attractive for editing tasks including dynamic reconstruction, geometry modification, and physical simulation [arxiv_2403.11134].

A central and recurring question in the literature concerns the theoretical fidelity of 3DGS's rendering pipeline. While 3DGS shares a conceptual image formation model with NeRF—both can be understood within the framework of volumetric rendering—the practical implementation of 3DGS relies on a series of approximations relative to principled volumetric rendering theory, trading theoretical rigor for computational efficiency through a hybrid approach combining volume rendering intuitions with primitive rasterization [arxiv_2502.19318]. Recent analytical work has interrogated whether replacing these approximations with more principled volumetric rendering solutions would yield meaningful quality improvements, providing an in-depth examination of the rendering assumptions embedded in the standard 3DGS formulation [arxiv_2502.19318]. This line of inquiry is important because it clarifies the extent to which the method's impressive empirical performance is robust to its theoretical compromises, and it opens avenues for targeted improvements in specific rendering scenarios where the approximations are most consequential.

Beyond rendering fidelity, memory consumption constitutes one of the most pressing practical limitations of the baseline 3DGS framework. Because each scene is represented by potentially millions of individual Gaussians, each carrying its own set of parameters, the storage and memory requirements during both training and inference can be prohibitive [arxiv_2312.04564]. Several distinct strategies have been proposed to address this challenge. The EAGLES framework introduces quantized embeddings to reduce per-point memory storage requirements and employs a coarse-to-fine training strategy that stabilizes optimization while also incorporating a pruning stage to reduce the total number of Gaussians, thereby improving both training speed and rendering throughput without sacrificing reconstruction quality [arxiv_2312.04564]. A complementary approach is taken by Factorized 3D Gaussian Splatting (F-3DGS), which draws inspiration from classical matrix and tensor factorization techniques to represent and approximate dense clusters of Gaussians in a compact factorized form, drastically reducing storage overhead while preserving image quality [arxiv_2405.17083]. These two methods illustrate a broader tension in the compression literature: quantization-based approaches operate at the level of individual Gaussian attributes, while factorization-based approaches restructure the collective representation of Gaussian clusters, and both offer complementary pathways toward more memory-efficient scene representations.

A different perspective on the memory problem is offered by hybrid representations that combine explicit Gaussians with neural fields. The HyRF framework decomposes a scene into a compact set of explicit Gaussians responsible for storing critical high-frequency parameters, while grid-based neural fields predict the remaining properties [arxiv_2509.17083]. This decomposition is motivated by the observation that while neural field-based compression of 3DGS can reduce memory overhead, such methods often struggle to capture high-frequency spatial variations in Gaussian properties, leading to degraded reconstruction of fine details [arxiv_2509.17083]. By reserving explicit representation for precisely those parameters where neural approximation is least reliable, HyRF attempts to capture the complementary strengths of both paradigms, echoing a broader trend in the field toward hybrid architectures that resist the false dichotomy between purely explicit and purely implicit representations [arxiv_2210.00379].

The robustness of 3DGS to imperfect input data represents another active research direction. The standard 3DGS optimization assumes sharp, well-exposed training images, yet in practice images are frequently degraded by lens defocus, object motion, or camera shake. Deblurring 3DGS addresses this limitation by extending the Gaussian splatting framework to handle blurry training inputs, a problem that prior neural field methods had begun to tackle but which had not been adequately addressed within the explicit Gaussian representation paradigm [arxiv_2401.00834]. The challenge is compounded by the fact that blur introduces ambiguities in the optimization landscape that can cause Gaussians to converge to incorrect configurations, making robust initialization and regularization strategies essential [arxiv_2401.00834].

Taken together, these works illustrate that the flat, scene-level representation introduced by the original 3DGS method [arxiv_2308.04079] has served as a productive foundation for a diverse research agenda. The field has simultaneously pursued improvements along multiple axes—rendering accuracy, memory efficiency, hybrid representation design, and robustness to input degradation—reflecting both the method's practical importance and the genuine theoretical questions its approximations raise [arxiv_2502.19318, arxiv_2403.11134]. The breadth and pace of this activity underscore why comprehensive surveys have become necessary to organize the rapidly expanding literature [arxiv_2403.11134, arxiv_2210.00379].
