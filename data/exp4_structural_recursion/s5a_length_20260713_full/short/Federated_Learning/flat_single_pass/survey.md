# Federated Learning (short)

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a compelling paradigm for training machine learning models across distributed data sources without requiring the centralization of raw data. At its core, FL enables multiple participants—ranging from mobile devices to institutional data silos—to collaboratively optimize a shared model by exchanging only locally computed updates rather than underlying data [arxiv_2104.14362]. This architectural choice directly addresses the growing tension between the demand for large-scale machine learning and the legal, regulatory, and ethical constraints that govern data sharing across organizational and jurisdictional boundaries. The foundational appeal of FL thus lies in its capacity to harness distributed computational resources and heterogeneous data while nominally preserving data locality [arxiv_2301.01299].

The transition from conventional distributed machine learning to federated learning represents more than a technical rebranding. Traditional distributed learning frameworks were designed primarily for homogeneous, co-located computing clusters where data could be freely shuffled and replicated across nodes [arxiv_2104.14362]. FL, by contrast, must contend with data that is generated organically at the edge, is often highly heterogeneous in both volume and statistical distribution, and cannot be relocated without violating privacy norms or regulatory mandates. This distinction has motivated a substantial body of research aimed at characterizing the unique challenges of the federated setting and developing methods suited to its constraints [arxiv_2106.06843]. Among the most extensively studied of these challenges is the problem of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data, wherein the local datasets held by individual participants reflect divergent underlying distributions. Such statistical heterogeneity has been shown to degrade model convergence and final performance relative to centralized training baselines, with the severity of degradation varying across both parametric models such as deep neural networks and non-parametric alternatives [arxiv_2106.06843]. The survey literature distinguishes between horizontal FL, where participants share the same feature space but differ in their data samples, and vertical FL, where participants hold different feature subsets for overlapping populations, each presenting distinct manifestations of the non-IID problem [arxiv_2106.06843].

Beyond statistical heterogeneity, communication overhead constitutes a second major axis of challenge in FL systems. Because model updates must traverse potentially bandwidth-constrained and unreliable networks between clients and a central server, the communication cost of iterative training can become prohibitive at scale [arxiv_2208.01200]. Research in this area has pursued several complementary strategies, including gradient compression, quantization, sparsification, and the reduction of communication rounds through increased local computation per round. The survey by [arxiv_2208.01200] organizes this body of work along three dimensions—communication efficiency, communication environment, and resource allocation—and highlights that these concerns are deeply intertwined: techniques that reduce per-round communication volume may increase the number of rounds required for convergence, while strategies that exploit heterogeneous network environments must balance fairness and efficiency across participants with vastly different connectivity profiles.

Privacy, however, remains the most philosophically central concern in FL, and it is here that the literature reveals the greatest complexity. While FL's data-locality property provides a baseline level of privacy by preventing direct data exposure, it does not, by itself, constitute a rigorous privacy guarantee. Gradient updates and model parameters exchanged during training can leak sensitive information about local datasets through inference attacks, including membership inference and gradient inversion [arxiv_2311.16008]. To address this vulnerability, differential privacy (DP) has become the dominant formal framework for quantifying and bounding privacy leakage in FL. DP mechanisms introduce calibrated noise into the training process—typically into the gradients or model updates before they are transmitted—such that the presence or absence of any individual data point cannot be reliably inferred from the released information [arxiv_2003.13761]. However, the application of DP to FL is not without cost: the noise necessary to achieve meaningful privacy guarantees can substantially impair model utility, and the trade-off between privacy and accuracy is highly sensitive to the choice of privacy parameters, the number of communication rounds, and the size of the participating population [arxiv_2311.16008].

Efforts to mitigate this privacy-utility trade-off have taken several directions. One line of work focuses on tighter privacy accounting, arguing that standard DP analyses are overly conservative and that more refined composition theorems—such as those based on concentrated differential privacy or Rényi divergence—can yield stronger utility guarantees for the same nominal privacy budget [arxiv_2003.13761]. The work in [arxiv_2003.13761] specifically develops a federated learning approach grounded in concentrated differential privacy that achieves convergence and utility properties competitive with non-private baselines, demonstrating that careful algorithmic design can substantially close the gap between private and non-private training. A complementary direction involves the use of secure aggregation protocols, which cryptographically ensure that the server observes only the aggregate of client updates rather than individual contributions, thereby reducing the information available to a potentially curious or adversarial server [arxiv_2012.07183]. Secure aggregation and differential privacy are often treated as orthogonal mechanisms that can be combined: DP bounds the information leakage from the aggregate, while secure aggregation prevents the server from accessing individual updates in the first place [arxiv_2407.19286].

The interaction between secure aggregation and multiple local optimization steps has received renewed attention in recent work. A practical tension arises because the privacy accounting benefits of secure aggregation are most cleanly realized when clients communicate after every local gradient step; allowing multiple local steps before aggregation—a common efficiency optimization—complicates the formal privacy analysis [arxiv_2407.19286]. The analysis presented in [arxiv_2407.19286] demonstrates that a refined accounting approach permits clients to perform multiple local steps while retaining the formal privacy benefits of secure aggregation, thereby reconciling the efficiency and privacy objectives that had previously appeared to be in conflict. This result is significant because it suggests that the design space for private and efficient FL is larger than previously understood.

Decentralized aggregation represents yet another architectural variant that has attracted interest as a means of simultaneously addressing privacy and communication concerns. Rather than routing all updates through a central server, decentralized protocols allow clients to aggregate updates through peer-to-peer communication patterns, reducing the trust placed in any single entity and distributing the communication load [arxiv_2012.07183]. The protocol developed in [arxiv_2012.07183] formulates aggregation using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers and controls the communication topology to minimize privacy leakage against honest-but-curious adversaries, offering a privacy guarantee without relying on noise injection. When combined with differential privacy mechanisms, decentralized aggregation has been shown to provide stronger end-to-end privacy properties than server-centric FL, though the choice of DP parameters and aggregation topology remains critical to achieving a favorable privacy-utility balance [arxiv_2311.16008].

Taken together, the surveyed literature reveals that flat federated learning—wherein all participants interact within a single-tier architecture coordinated by a central server or through peer-to-peer protocols—faces a constellation of interrelated challenges spanning statistical heterogeneity, communication efficiency, and privacy preservation [arxiv_2301.01299]. Progress on any one of these dimensions frequently implicates the others, and the most impactful recent contributions are those that address multiple challenges simultaneously through principled algorithmic and architectural design.
