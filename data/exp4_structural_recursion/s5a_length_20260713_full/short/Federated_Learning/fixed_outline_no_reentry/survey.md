# Federated Learning (short)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a prominent paradigm for enabling collaborative model training across distributed participants without requiring the direct sharing of raw data. This approach is particularly motivated by the growing prevalence of data distributed across end-user devices, regional silos, and organizational boundaries, where legal frameworks and privacy regulations preclude centralized data aggregation [arxiv_2104.14362]. Rather than transmitting sensitive data to a central server, FL allows participants to train models locally and share only model updates or gradients, thereby offering a degree of privacy protection by design [arxiv_2301.01299]. The foundational appeal of this framework lies in its capacity to reconcile the competing demands of leveraging large-scale distributed data and complying with data governance requirements.

Several comprehensive surveys have sought to systematize the rapidly expanding body of FL research. Lo et al. [arxiv_2104.14362] propose a functional architecture for FL systems and develop a taxonomy of related techniques, covering distributed training procedures, data communication protocols, and security mechanisms. Their work traces the conceptual lineage of FL from classical distributed machine learning, highlighting how FL introduces additional constraints—particularly around data locality and privacy—that distinguish it from conventional parallelized training. In a complementary effort, [arxiv_2301.01299] provides a more recent systematic survey that explicitly aims to capture the latest methodological advances, noting that earlier surveys tend to focus on narrow perspectives or fail to reflect the current state of the field. Together, these surveys underscore that FL is not a monolithic technique but rather a broad framework encompassing a diverse set of algorithmic, systems, and application-level concerns.

One of the most persistently studied challenges within FL is the statistical heterogeneity of local data distributions. When data across clients are non-independent and non-identically distributed (non-IID), the performance of globally aggregated models can degrade substantially relative to centralized training baselines [arxiv_2106.06843]. This survey provides a detailed analysis of how non-IID conditions affect both parametric models, such as neural networks, and non-parametric models, across both horizontal FL—where participants share the same feature space but hold different data samples—and vertical FL—where participants hold different feature spaces for overlapping sample populations. The authors review existing mitigation strategies, including data-sharing approaches, globally regularized objectives, and personalization techniques, while candidly discussing their respective limitations. This work thus situates data heterogeneity as a central axis around which much of the FL optimization literature is organized.

Beyond statistical challenges, communication efficiency represents another critical bottleneck in practical FL deployments. Because FL systems typically involve a large number of clients communicating iteratively with a central server, the cumulative communication overhead can be prohibitive, particularly in bandwidth-constrained or unreliable network environments [arxiv_2208.01200]. This survey examines FL communication research along three dimensions: communication efficiency, the nature of the communication environment, and resource allocation strategies. Techniques such as gradient compression, quantization, and sparsification are reviewed as means of reducing the volume of transmitted information, while adaptive scheduling and asynchronous aggregation protocols are discussed in the context of heterogeneous network conditions. Compared to the broader architectural surveys [arxiv_2104.14362; arxiv_2301.01299], this work offers a more granular treatment of the systems-level trade-offs inherent in FL communication design.

Taken together, these surveys delineate the principal dimensions along which FL research has developed: privacy-preserving architecture, statistical robustness under data heterogeneity, and communication efficiency. They collectively establish that while FL offers a compelling solution to distributed learning under privacy constraints, its practical realization demands coordinated advances across algorithmic design, systems engineering, and network optimization [arxiv_2106.06843; arxiv_2208.01200; arxiv_2301.01299].

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Privacy preservation in federated learning has emerged as one of the most actively investigated research directions, driven by the recognition that the standard federated paradigm, while avoiding direct data sharing, remains vulnerable to inference attacks and information leakage through the model updates exchanged between clients and the central server. The foundational tension in this space is the trade-off between the strength of privacy guarantees and the utility of the resulting model, a challenge that has motivated a diverse array of technical approaches spanning differential privacy, secure aggregation, and decentralized communication protocols.

Differential privacy (DP) has become the dominant formal framework for quantifying and bounding privacy leakage in federated learning. However, early applications of DP mechanisms to federated settings were criticized for imposing substantial costs on model convergence and final utility. Addressing this limitation directly, the work of [arxiv_2003.13761] proposes a concentrated differentially private federated learning approach that preserves model utility by carefully calibrating the noise injection process, demonstrating that strong privacy guarantees need not come at the expense of learning performance. This contribution is significant because it challenges the assumption that privacy and utility are fundamentally at odds, instead framing the problem as one of mechanism design and algorithmic optimization.

Building on the DP foundation, more recent work has examined how the interaction between secure aggregation and differential privacy can be exploited to achieve better privacy-utility trade-offs, particularly in settings where clients perform multiple local optimization steps before communicating with the server. [arxiv_2407.19286] identifies a key limitation in existing combined approaches: when fine-grained, sample-level protection is desired, current techniques typically require communication after each local step to fully benefit from secure aggregation's privacy guarantees. The authors introduce a new analytical framework that decouples this constraint, allowing multiple local steps while retaining the formal privacy benefits of secure aggregation. This advance is practically important because multiple local steps are a standard efficiency mechanism in federated learning, and prior work had forced practitioners to choose between communication efficiency and rigorous privacy accounting.

Complementing these centralized approaches, a parallel line of research has explored decentralized aggregation as an alternative architectural strategy for privacy preservation. [arxiv_2012.07183] formulates the aggregation problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) and proposes a protocol that controls the communication topology among participants in each aggregation round, thereby minimizing privacy leakage without relying on DP noise or homomorphic encryption. The protocol is shown to provide guarantees against honest-but-curious adversaries, representing a distinct threat model from those typically addressed by DP-based methods. This decentralized perspective is further developed in [arxiv_2311.16008], which deploys an experimental federated learning environment combining decentralized aggregation with differential privacy and evaluates the resulting privacy-utility trade-off across benchmark datasets. Notably, [arxiv_2311.16008] emphasizes that the selection of DP parameters is central to navigating this trade-off, and that the federated setting remains susceptible to membership inference attacks even when local data is retained at the node level, underscoring the necessity of layered privacy mechanisms.

Taken together, these works illustrate a convergence toward hybrid strategies that combine structural communication controls with formal privacy mechanisms. While [arxiv_2003.13761] and [arxiv_2407.19286] focus on refining DP-based guarantees within centralized or semi-centralized architectures, [arxiv_2012.07183] and [arxiv_2311.16008] demonstrate that decentralized aggregation offers a complementary and sometimes more communication-efficient path to privacy. The collective implication is that no single mechanism is universally optimal, and that the choice of privacy technique must be calibrated to the specific threat model, communication constraints, and utility requirements of the deployment context.
