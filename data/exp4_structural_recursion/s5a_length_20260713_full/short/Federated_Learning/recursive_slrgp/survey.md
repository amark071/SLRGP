# Federated Learning (short)

### Surveys and Overviews of Federated Learning

Federated learning (FL) has rapidly emerged as a prominent paradigm for distributed machine learning, enabling multiple parties to collaboratively train models without centralizing their raw data. This architectural shift is largely motivated by growing regulatory constraints and privacy concerns that prevent organizations and individuals from directly sharing sensitive information [arxiv_2104.14362]. Unlike conventional centralized learning, FL allows participants to retain data locally while exchanging only locally trained model parameters or computed gradients, thereby offering a degree of privacy protection by design [arxiv_2301.01299]. The foundational appeal of this approach lies in its capacity to harness distributed data and computational resources across heterogeneous devices and institutions while remaining compliant with data protection laws and regulations [arxiv_2104.14362].

Despite its promise, federated learning introduces a set of technical challenges that distinguish it from standard distributed machine learning. One of the most extensively studied among these is the problem of statistical heterogeneity, commonly referred to as the Non-IID (non-independent and identically distributed) data problem. When local datasets across participating clients reflect different underlying distributions—as is typical in real-world deployments—the globally aggregated model tends to perform significantly worse than its centrally trained counterpart [arxiv_2106.06843]. This degradation affects both parametric models, such as deep neural networks, and non-parametric models, and manifests differently depending on whether the federation is organized horizontally, where clients share the same feature space but differ in data samples, or vertically, where clients hold different feature spaces for overlapping sample populations [arxiv_2106.06843]. Addressing Non-IID data has consequently become a central research thread, with proposed solutions spanning data-sharing strategies, personalized aggregation mechanisms, and novel optimization objectives, each carrying distinct trade-offs in terms of convergence guarantees and communication overhead [arxiv_2106.06843].

Communication efficiency represents another critical bottleneck in federated systems. Because FL typically involves a large number of clients—potentially mobile or edge devices with limited bandwidth—the repeated exchange of high-dimensional model updates between clients and a central server can impose substantial communication costs [arxiv_2208.01200]. Researchers have approached this challenge from multiple angles, including gradient compression, quantization, sparsification, and asynchronous communication protocols, with the goal of reducing the volume of transmitted information without critically compromising model quality [arxiv_2208.01200]. Beyond raw efficiency, the communication environment itself introduces further complications: network heterogeneity, intermittent connectivity, and varying latency across clients all affect the stability and fairness of the training process. Resource allocation strategies that account for these environmental factors have therefore become an active area of investigation [arxiv_2208.01200].

More recent surveys have sought to synthesize these diverse threads into a coherent picture of the field's trajectory. While earlier reviews tended to focus on specific sub-problems or lacked coverage of the latest methodological advances, more comprehensive and systematic treatments have emerged to map the full landscape of FL methods and applications [arxiv_2301.01299]. These works collectively propose taxonomies that organize FL research along dimensions such as system architecture, privacy mechanisms, aggregation strategies, and application domains, providing a structured basis for understanding how individual contributions relate to one another [arxiv_2104.14362; arxiv_2301.01299]. Together, the surveyed literature underscores that federated learning is not a monolithic solution but rather a multifaceted framework whose practical deployment requires carefully balancing privacy, communication efficiency, statistical robustness, and system scalability—challenges that continue to drive active and interdisciplinary research efforts.

### Differential Privacy in Federated Learning

Privacy preservation in federated learning has emerged as one of the most actively investigated research challenges, driven by the recognition that the mere act of retaining local data on edge devices does not, by itself, constitute a sufficient privacy guarantee. Although federated learning avoids the direct transmission of raw training data, the exchange of model updates, gradients, or aggregated statistics between participants and a central server creates meaningful attack surfaces. Membership inference attacks, for instance, can exploit these communications to infer sensitive information about individual training samples [arxiv_2311.16008]. This vulnerability has motivated a substantial body of work exploring how formal privacy guarantees can be integrated into federated learning pipelines without unacceptably degrading model utility.

Differential privacy (DP) has become the dominant formal framework for quantifying and bounding privacy leakage in federated settings. However, naively applying differential privacy mechanisms to federated learning introduces a well-documented tension: the noise required to satisfy strong privacy budgets tends to impair convergence and reduce the utility of the resulting model. This trade-off is central to the contribution of [arxiv_2003.13761], which develops a concentrated differentially private federated learning approach specifically designed to preserve model utility. By leveraging concentrated differential privacy rather than standard DP formulations, the authors demonstrate that it is possible to achieve meaningful privacy guarantees with substantially less degradation to model performance than prior mechanisms typically incur. The work thus positions itself as an improvement over earlier privacy-preserving approaches that, while technically applicable to federated learning, imposed prohibitively high costs on convergence.

A complementary line of research has explored whether the architectural choices governing how model updates are aggregated can themselves serve as a privacy mechanism. Rather than relying exclusively on noise injection, [arxiv_2012.07183] proposes a decentralized aggregation protocol formulated through the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM). The key insight is that by carefully controlling the communication topology among participants in each aggregation round, it is possible to minimize privacy leakage without resorting to differential privacy or homomorphic encryption. The protocol provides formal guarantees against an honest-but-curious adversary, and the authors propose an efficient algorithm for constructing the requisite communication patterns. This approach is notable for demonstrating that structural constraints on information flow can substitute for, or complement, cryptographic and statistical privacy mechanisms.

Building on the recognition that both differential privacy and secure aggregation offer distinct but complementary protections, more recent work has examined how these techniques interact in practice. [arxiv_2407.19286] addresses a specific technical limitation that arises when fine-grained, sample-level differential privacy is combined with secure aggregation: existing analyses generally require parties to communicate after every local optimization step in order to fully realize the privacy benefits of secure aggregation. This requirement conflicts with the standard federated learning practice of performing multiple local gradient steps between communication rounds, a strategy that is otherwise desirable for reducing communication overhead. The authors introduce a new analytical approach that permits multiple local optimization steps while preserving the formal privacy guarantees associated with secure aggregation, thereby achieving higher utility without sacrificing privacy.

Taken together, these works illustrate the multi-dimensional nature of the privacy-utility trade-off in federated learning. Whether the strategy involves refined noise mechanisms [arxiv_2003.13761], restructured communication topologies [arxiv_2012.07183], or improved theoretical analyses of existing technique combinations [arxiv_2407.19286], the overarching challenge remains consistent: providing strong, formally verifiable privacy guarantees while retaining the practical utility that motivates federated learning in the first place. Empirical investigations further underscore that parameter selection within differential privacy frameworks is itself a critical determinant of where a given system falls along this trade-off curve [arxiv_2311.16008].
