# Multimodal Large Language Models (short)

### Surveys and Overviews of Multimodal LLMs

The emergence of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) represents one of the most consequential developments in contemporary artificial intelligence research, bringing together advances in natural language processing and computer vision to produce systems capable of reasoning across heterogeneous data modalities. The intellectual foundations of this field are rooted in earlier work on vision-language pre-training (VLP), which established the core methodological principles that later scaled into the MLLM paradigm. As surveyed by [arxiv_2210.09263], VLP methods can be organized into three broad categories: those targeting image-text tasks such as visual question answering and image captioning, those addressing core computer vision tasks including open-set object detection and segmentation, and those handling video-text tasks such as video captioning and retrieval. This taxonomic framing highlights how the field progressively expanded the scope of cross-modal alignment, laying the groundwork for more general-purpose systems.

Building on these foundations, subsequent research shifted attention toward the development of models capable of functioning as general-purpose assistants rather than task-specific specialists. This transition is thoroughly documented by [arxiv_2309.10020], which traces the evolution from specialist multimodal foundation models—pre-trained for narrowly defined purposes such as visual understanding or text-to-image generation—toward unified architectures that leverage the instruction-following and generalization capabilities of large language models. Crucially, [arxiv_2309.10020] identifies end-to-end training of multimodal LLMs and the chaining of multimodal tools with language model backbones as two of the most active and open research directions, underscoring the degree to which the field remains in a state of rapid, exploratory development.

The architectural and training dimensions of this transition are examined in considerable depth by [arxiv_2402.12451], which provides a comprehensive review of recent visual-based MLLMs with particular attention to their multimodal alignment strategies and training techniques. Where [arxiv_2309.10020] emphasizes the historical arc from specialist to generalist models, [arxiv_2402.12451] focuses more granularly on the design choices that enable seamless integration of visual and textual modalities, including dialogue-based interfaces and instruction-following capabilities. The survey also extends its analysis across a diverse range of downstream tasks—visual grounding, image generation and editing, and domain-specific applications—offering a more task-oriented perspective than the taxonomy-driven approach of [arxiv_2309.10020]. Together, these two surveys provide complementary lenses through which the MLLM landscape can be understood: one foregrounding evolutionary trajectory, the other foregrounding architectural and functional diversity.

A further dimension of the field concerns the expansion of multimodality beyond the canonical vision-language dyad. [arxiv_2410.05608] addresses this directly, surveying models capable of integrating not only text and images but also audio, video, and sensor data. This broader conception of multimodality reflects a growing recognition that real-world intelligence requires the fusion of diverse signal types, and that the techniques developed for vision-language alignment can, with appropriate adaptation, generalize to other modality pairs. [arxiv_2410.05608] also emphasizes instruction tuning as a critical mechanism for optimizing multimodal models for specific tasks, a theme that resonates with the alignment strategies discussed in [arxiv_2402.12451] and the end-to-end training paradigms outlined in [arxiv_2309.10020].

Taken together, these works illustrate a field characterized by rapid architectural innovation, expanding modality coverage, and an ongoing negotiation between task-specific optimization and general-purpose capability. The progression from VLP methods [arxiv_2210.09263] through specialist foundation models [arxiv_2309.10020] to instruction-tuned, broadly multimodal systems [arxiv_2402.12451, arxiv_2410.05608] reflects both the maturation of underlying techniques and the increasing ambition of the research agenda, setting the stage for continued investigation into how language model reasoning can be most effectively grounded in perceptual experience.

### Model Architectures and Pretraining for Vision-Language

The development of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has emerged as one of the most active frontiers in artificial intelligence research, driven by the ambition to endow language models with the capacity to perceive, reason about, and generate content across multiple sensory modalities. Early foundational work demonstrated that jointly scaling vision and language components could yield substantial gains in cross-modal understanding. PaLI exemplifies this trajectory by presenting a jointly-scaled multilingual language-image model that generates text conditioned on both visual and textual inputs [arxiv_2209.06794]. A central insight from PaLI is that the vision encoder must be scaled commensurately with the language model; to this end, the authors trained a 4-billion parameter Vision Transformer (ViT-e), finding that larger-capacity vision models consistently improve performance across a wide range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks in multiple languages [arxiv_2209.06794]. This emphasis on joint scaling established an important design principle: neither modality should become a bottleneck, and pre-trained encoders for both vision and language can be leveraged to reduce the prohibitive cost of training from scratch.

Despite the promise of large-scale joint training, practical deployment of MLLMs is constrained by the computational expense of retraining or fine-tuning billion-parameter language models. CROME addresses this challenge by proposing an efficient vision-language instruction tuning framework built around a gated cross-modal adapter [arxiv_2408.06610]. Rather than updating the language model backbone, CROME inserts a lightweight adapter that fuses visual and textual representations before they are passed into a frozen LLM, thereby achieving effective cross-modal understanding with a minimal parameter budget [arxiv_2408.06610]. This approach contrasts with the full-scale joint training paradigm exemplified by PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794]: whereas PaLI invests heavily in co-training large vision and language components, CROME prioritizes parameter efficiency and adaptability, demonstrating that competitive zero-shot performance on visual question answering and instruction-following benchmarks can be attained without retraining the underlying language model [arxiv_2408.06610]. Furthermore, CROME explicitly addresses the gap between zero-shot evaluation and task-specific tuning, offering a pathway for fine-grained adaptation that purely zero-shot-oriented models tend to overlook [arxiv_2408.06610].

While the majority of MLLM research has concentrated on the vision-language dyad, a growing body of work argues that genuine multimodal intelligence requires the incorporation of additional sensory streams, most notably audio. VALOR advances this agenda by jointly modeling vision, audio, and language within a single end-to-end framework [ss_478472]. The model employs three separate unimodal encoders feeding into a shared decoder for multimodal conditional text generation, and is pre-trained with two complementary objectives: Multimodal Grouping Alignment, which projects all three modalities into a common representational space, and Multimodal Grouping Captioning, which conditions text generation on vision, audio, or their combination [ss_478472]. This trimodal design represents a meaningful departure from dual-modality systems and raises the question of how audio-visual grounding can enrich language understanding in ways that vision alone cannot.

Complementing these architectural innovations, Veagle focuses on the persistent challenge of accurate visual interpretation in real-world scenarios, where existing Vision Language Models and MLLMs frequently fail to correctly answer questions that require fine-grained image understanding [arxiv_2403.08773]. By introducing enhancements to multimodal representation learning, Veagle targets the gap between benchmark performance and robust deployment, situating itself within a broader concern shared across the literature: that scaling and architectural elegance must ultimately translate into reliable, interpretable cross-modal reasoning [arxiv_2403.08773]. Taken together, these works illuminate a field navigating tensions between scale and efficiency, dual-modality and omni-modal coverage, and zero-shot generalization and task-specific adaptability.
