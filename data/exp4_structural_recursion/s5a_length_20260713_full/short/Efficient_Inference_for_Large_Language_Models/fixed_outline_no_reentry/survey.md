# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (short)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated a parallel and urgent body of research devoted to making their inference computationally tractable. Several comprehensive surveys have emerged to map this landscape, each offering a distinct organizational lens through which the field can be understood and navigated.

A foundational concern motivating this literature is the mismatch between the capabilities of modern LLMs and the resource budgets available for their deployment. As [arxiv_2404.14294] identifies, the primary sources of inefficiency are threefold: the sheer scale of model parameters, the quadratic complexity of the attention mechanism with respect to sequence length, and the inherently sequential nature of autoregressive decoding. These structural bottlenecks are not merely engineering inconveniences but represent fundamental algorithmic challenges that constrain where and how LLMs can be deployed. [arxiv_2402.09748] similarly frames the problem in terms of the prohibitive memory and computational costs incurred at inference time, emphasizing that resource-constrained devices remain largely inaccessible to state-of-the-art models without significant intervention.

The surveys broadly converge on a shared taxonomy of optimization strategies, though they differ in how they partition and prioritize these categories. Quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, and architectural redesign appear consistently across [arxiv_2402.09748], [arxiv_2404.14294], and [arxiv_2408.03130] as the canonical pillars of model compression. [arxiv_2404.14294] extends this taxonomy by introducing a tripartite framework organized around data-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations, which has the advantage of capturing not only algorithmic interventions but also deployment-oriented strategies such as memory scheduling and parallel computation. This broader scope is echoed in [arxiv_2401.02038], which situates inference optimization within the full lifecycle of LLM development, tracing the continuum from data preprocessing and pre-training through to inference-time techniques including structural optimization and parallel computation.

A particularly salient distinction raised by [arxiv_2402.09748] concerns the degree to which compression techniques developed for smaller models transfer cleanly to the LLM regime. The authors argue that two characteristics set LLMs apart: the prohibitive cost of fine-tuning or retraining after compression, and the emergent capabilities that may be disproportionately sensitive to parameter reduction. This observation has important implications for the applicability of classical pruning and quantization methods, which often assume that a post-compression fine-tuning stage is feasible. [arxiv_2408.03130] reinforces this concern by cataloguing the unique challenges associated with each optimization category when applied at scale, noting that practical deployment considerations frequently diverge from the assumptions embedded in benchmark evaluations.

The question of how to evaluate and compare these methods is itself a point of methodological interest. [arxiv_2404.14294] notably includes comparative experiments on representative methods, moving beyond purely descriptive taxonomy to offer empirical grounding for the claims made in the broader literature. This stands in contrast to [arxiv_2401.02038] and [arxiv_2402.09748], which adopt a more encyclopedic approach oriented toward comprehensiveness rather than direct experimental comparison.

Taken together, these surveys establish that efficient LLM inference is a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated solutions at the algorithmic, architectural, and systems levels. The convergence on shared taxonomic categories suggests a maturing field, while the divergences in scope and emphasis reflect ongoing debates about which bottlenecks are most consequential and which optimization strategies offer the most generalizable gains. The present review builds on this foundation by examining specific techniques in greater depth, with attention to both their theoretical underpinnings and their practical trade-offs in deployment settings.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The pursuit of efficient inference for large language models has generated a rich body of research spanning multiple complementary strategies, each targeting different sources of computational and memory overhead. A recurring theme across this literature is the recognition that modern LLMs, while extraordinarily capable, harbor significant internal redundancy that can be exploited to reduce deployment costs without proportionate losses in model quality.

Weight pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation represent the three most widely studied compression paradigms. These techniques are often treated as modular components that can be combined into unified pipelines. A compelling demonstration of this integrative approach is provided by [arxiv_2211.07715], which proposes a production-oriented pipeline for deploying fast Transformer models on CPUs. Their system chains hardware-aware pruning, knowledge distillation, quantization, and a custom inference runtime engine with optimized sparse and quantized kernels, yielding a Fast DistilBERT model that achieves substantial throughput gains with minimal accuracy degradation. This work is notable for its explicit attention to the gap between research-grade compression and industrial deployment, acknowledging that specialized software infrastructure is a prerequisite for realizing theoretical efficiency gains at scale.

Quantization in particular has attracted growing attention as LLMs have scaled to billions of parameters, where even modest reductions in numerical precision translate into significant memory savings and faster matrix operations. However, deploying quantized models efficiently on commodity hardware introduces non-trivial engineering challenges. [arxiv_2408.08554] identifies two core obstacles: the performance degradation associated with aggressive low-bit quantization, and the difficulty of accelerating mixed-precision matrix operations on GPUs whose integer computing units support only a restricted set of precision types. Their proposed framework, ABQ-LLM, addresses both issues through a novel arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm that decouples the choice of numerical precision from hardware constraints, enabling efficient inference across a broad range of quantization configurations. Compared to the pipeline described in [arxiv_2211.07715], which targets CPU deployment and combines quantization with pruning and distillation, ABQ-LLM focuses specifically on GPU-side quantization flexibility, reflecting the distinct hardware considerations that arise at different points in the deployment landscape.

A less explored but increasingly important dimension of efficient inference concerns model architecture itself. Most compression research, as [arxiv_2409.17372] observes, concentrates on optimizing the weights of a fixed architecture while neglecting the question of whether the architecture itself is optimal for a given efficiency target. Traditional neural architecture search methods are poorly suited to LLMs due to the enormous parameter counts involved. To address this, [arxiv_2409.17372] proposes a training-free architecture search framework that identifies optimal subnets inheriting weights from a pretrained LLM, thereby preserving the model's core capabilities while achieving inference acceleration. This approach is conceptually distinct from weight-centric methods such as those in [arxiv_2211.07715] and [arxiv_2408.08554], as it treats the network topology as a variable rather than a fixed constraint.

Underlying many of these compression strategies is a broader insight about the relationship between model size and efficiency during training. [arxiv_2002.11794] challenges the intuition that smaller models are inherently more efficient, demonstrating that larger and deeper Transformer models converge in substantially fewer training steps, and that this convergence advantage frequently outweighs the per-iteration computational cost. This finding motivates a "train large, then compress" paradigm, wherein oversized models are trained briefly and subsequently reduced through compression techniques—a strategy that aligns naturally with the post-training quantization approach of [arxiv_2408.08554] and the subnet extraction methodology of [arxiv_2409.17372]. Together, these works suggest that efficient inference is best understood not as a single-stage optimization problem but as a multi-phase process encompassing training strategy, architectural search, and deployment-time compression.
