# Efficient Inference for Large Language Models (short)

### Surveys and Overviews of Efficient LLM Inference

The rapid proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has generated a parallel and urgent research agenda focused on making these systems computationally tractable at inference time. As transformer-based architectures have scaled to billions of parameters, the memory footprints and arithmetic demands of serving them have grown correspondingly, creating a substantial gap between model capability and practical deployability. Several comprehensive surveys have emerged to map this landscape, each offering a distinct organizational perspective on the same underlying challenge.

At the broadest level, the inefficiency of LLM inference can be traced to three structural causes: the sheer size of modern model weights, the quadratic complexity of the self-attention operation with respect to sequence length, and the sequential, auto-regressive nature of token generation [arxiv_2404.14294]. This diagnostic framing is useful because it motivates the different families of solutions that have been proposed. Quantization reduces the numerical precision of weights and activations, thereby shrinking memory consumption and accelerating arithmetic; pruning removes redundant parameters or attention heads; knowledge distillation transfers the predictive behavior of a large teacher model into a smaller student; and architectural redesigns seek to replace or approximate the quadratic attention mechanism with more scalable alternatives [arxiv_2402.09748, arxiv_2408.03130]. These categories are broadly consistent across the surveyed literature, suggesting a degree of consensus in how the field organizes its own contributions.

Nevertheless, the surveys differ in the granularity and framing of their taxonomies. Zhou et al. [arxiv_2404.14294] adopt a three-tier hierarchy distinguishing data-level, model-level, and system-level optimizations, which usefully separates algorithmic interventions on the model itself from engineering decisions about scheduling, batching, and memory management. By contrast, Zhu et al. [arxiv_2402.09748] retain the classical compression taxonomy inherited from the smaller-model literature—quantization, pruning, distillation, compact architecture, and dynamic networks—while emphasizing two characteristics that make LLMs qualitatively different from their predecessors: the prohibitive cost of fine-tuning after compression, and the emergence of in-context learning as a capability that must be preserved through any optimization pipeline. This distinction is practically significant, because many compression techniques developed for convolutional networks or smaller transformers implicitly assume that a post-compression fine-tuning stage is affordable, an assumption that breaks down at the scale of contemporary LLMs.

The survey by Wan et al. [arxiv_2401.02038] takes a broader scope, situating inference optimization within the full lifecycle of LLMs from data preprocessing and pre-training through to deployment. This holistic framing allows the authors to connect inference-time decisions—such as model compression and parallel decoding—to upstream choices made during training, including architecture selection and fine-tuning strategies. While this breadth is valuable for understanding interdependencies, it necessarily sacrifices the depth of coverage that more inference-focused surveys provide.

A more practitioner-oriented perspective is offered by [arxiv_2408.03130], which explicitly foregrounds the challenges and trade-offs that arise when applying optimization techniques in real deployments. This work highlights that methods such as quantization and pruning interact in non-trivial ways with model behavior, and that gains in throughput or memory efficiency often come at the cost of increased engineering complexity or degraded performance on specific task types. Taken together, these four surveys establish that efficient LLM inference is not a single problem but a multidimensional design space, in which algorithmic, architectural, and systems-level decisions must be co-optimized under constraints that vary substantially across deployment contexts.

### Model Compression: Pruning, Quantization, and Distillation

The pursuit of efficient inference for large language models has generated a rich body of research spanning multiple complementary strategies, each targeting different sources of computational overhead. At a high level, the dominant approaches can be grouped into weight-centric compression methods—including pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation—and architecture-level search techniques that seek structurally leaner models. Understanding how these strategies relate to one another, and where each falls short, is essential for situating more recent advances in the field.

A foundational tension in this space concerns the relationship between model size and computational efficiency during both training and inference. The work of [arxiv_2002.11794] challenges the intuitive assumption that smaller models are inherently more efficient. Through systematic study of Transformer models on compute-limited tasks such as self-supervised pretraining and high-resource machine translation, the authors demonstrate that wider and deeper models converge in substantially fewer training steps, and that this acceleration in convergence typically outweighs the additional per-iteration cost of larger architectures. The practical implication is that the most compute-efficient training strategy is to train very large models but halt early—a counterintuitive finding that reframes the efficiency problem as one of training dynamics rather than raw parameter count alone. Crucially, however, this strategy creates a downstream challenge: the resulting large models still impose significant memory and latency burdens at inference time, motivating the need for post-training compression.

Quantization has emerged as one of the most widely adopted post-training compression techniques, reducing the numerical precision of model weights and activations to decrease memory footprint and accelerate computation. [arxiv_2408.08554] identifies two persistent obstacles in this area: first, aggressive low-bit quantization degrades model performance in ways that are difficult to recover without retraining; second, the limited variety of integer computing units available on modern GPUs means that mixed-precision quantized matrix operations cannot be straightforwardly accelerated in hardware. Their proposed ABQ-LLM framework addresses both issues through an arbitrary-bit quantization algorithm paired with a custom inference framework, enabling efficient execution of varied-precision operations on GPU hardware. This work highlights that quantization is not merely an algorithmic problem but also a systems-level challenge requiring co-design of compression schemes and runtime infrastructure.

The systems perspective is equally prominent in [arxiv_2211.07715], which targets CPU deployment—a setting often neglected in favor of GPU-centric solutions despite its prevalence in production environments. The authors construct an end-to-end pipeline combining hardware-aware pruning, knowledge distillation, and quantization, and deploy the resulting compressed model using a custom inference runtime with optimized sparse and quantized kernels. Their Fast DistilBERT achieves substantial throughput improvements with minimal accuracy loss, demonstrating that the combination of multiple compression techniques, when tightly integrated with hardware-specific optimizations, can yield practical gains that no single technique achieves in isolation. Compared to [arxiv_2408.08554], which focuses on GPU-side quantization, this work underscores that the target hardware platform fundamentally shapes which compression strategies are most effective.

Despite the maturity of weight-centric compression, [arxiv_2409.17372] argues that the field has systematically neglected the question of optimal model architecture. Most compression research optimizes the weights of a fixed architecture, leaving unexplored whether a structurally different subnet might achieve better efficiency-accuracy trade-offs. Traditional neural architecture search methods are ill-suited to this problem because their complexity scales poorly with the enormous parameter counts of modern LLMs. To address this, the authors propose a training-free architecture search framework that identifies optimal subnets inheriting weights from a pretrained LLM, preserving core capabilities while accelerating inference. This approach is conceptually distinct from quantization or pruning in that it targets the topology of the model rather than the precision or magnitude of its weights, and it complements the findings of [arxiv_2002.11794] by suggesting that the large models produced by compute-efficient training may themselves contain more efficient sub-architectures waiting to be discovered.

Taken together, these works reveal that efficient LLM inference is a multi-dimensional problem requiring coordinated advances in training strategy, compression algorithms, architecture search, and hardware-aware deployment.
