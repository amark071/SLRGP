# Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Large Language Models — naive_recursive_chunking

### Surveys and Overviews of RAG — evidence cluster A

The emergence of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) as a paradigm for enhancing large language models (LLMs) has generated a substantial and rapidly growing body of survey literature, reflecting both the breadth of the approach and the urgency of the problems it addresses. Across this literature, a consistent set of motivating limitations is identified: LLMs, despite their remarkable empirical performance across a wide range of tasks, are fundamentally constrained by the static nature of their parametric knowledge, their susceptibility to hallucination, and their inability to reliably incorporate domain-specific or temporally current information [arxiv_2312.10997] [arxiv_2405.06211]. These limitations are not merely theoretical concerns but practical obstacles that have motivated the development of RAG as a mechanism for grounding generative outputs in dynamically retrieved external evidence, thereby improving both the accuracy and the verifiability of model responses [arxiv_2404.10981].

The foundational insight shared across these surveys is that LLMs store knowledge implicitly within their parameters during pretraining, a process that is both computationally expensive and inherently frozen at a particular point in time. As [arxiv_2407.13193] observes, this reliance on parametric storage means that models cannot easily be updated to reflect new information without costly retraining or fine-tuning. RAG addresses this by augmenting the generation process with an external knowledge base, allowing the model to retrieve relevant documents or passages at inference time and condition its outputs on this retrieved context. This approach is described by [arxiv_2405.06211] as enabling LLMs to harness external and authoritative knowledge bases rather than relying solely on internal knowledge, a shift that is particularly consequential in the era of AI-generated content, where the quality and reliability of outputs are under increasing scrutiny.

While the motivating problems are broadly consistent across the literature, the surveys differ meaningfully in their organizational frameworks and the aspects of RAG they foreground. The survey by [arxiv_2312.10997] offers one of the most widely cited taxonomic treatments, organizing the evolution of RAG into three successive paradigms: Naive RAG, Advanced RAG, and Modular RAG. Naive RAG refers to the straightforward pipeline of retrieving documents and prepending them to the generation prompt, a design that, while effective in many settings, suffers from issues of retrieval noise, redundancy, and a lack of coordination between the retrieval and generation components. Advanced RAG introduces improvements such as query rewriting, reranking of retrieved passages, and more sophisticated indexing strategies to address these shortcomings. Modular RAG represents a further evolution in which the pipeline is decomposed into interchangeable components that can be configured and optimized independently, enabling greater flexibility and task-specific adaptation [arxiv_2312.10997]. This tripartite framework provides a useful historical and architectural lens, though it is primarily descriptive rather than prescriptive.

In contrast, [arxiv_2404.10981] adopts a retrieval-centric perspective, organizing the RAG paradigm into four stages: pre-retrieval, retrieval, post-retrieval, and generation. This four-stage decomposition is particularly attentive to the processes that occur before and after the core retrieval step, including query formulation, document chunking and indexing strategies, passage reranking, and the mechanisms by which retrieved content is integrated into the generation process. By foregrounding these surrounding processes, this survey highlights that retrieval quality is not solely a function of the retrieval model itself but is shaped by a constellation of design choices that span the entire pipeline. This perspective complements the paradigm-level analysis of [arxiv_2312.10997] by providing a more granular account of the operational components that determine system performance.

The survey by [arxiv_2407.13193] takes yet another organizational approach, focusing specifically on the mechanisms by which retrieved information is fused with the generative model. It introduces a novel taxonomy of retrieval fusion strategies, distinguishing among query-based fusion, logits-based fusion, latent fusion, and parametric fusion. Query-based fusion operates at the input level, modifying or augmenting the query before retrieval; logits-based fusion integrates retrieved information at the output distribution level; latent fusion incorporates retrieved representations into the model's internal hidden states; and parametric fusion involves updating model parameters based on retrieved content. This taxonomy is notable for its attention to the architectural diversity of RAG systems and for making explicit the different points in the model pipeline at which external knowledge can be introduced. The structured comparisons across accessibility, efficiency, and use cases offered by [arxiv_2407.13193] provide a practical framework for evaluating the trade-offs among these approaches, a dimension that is less systematically addressed in [arxiv_2312.10997] and [arxiv_2404.10981].

The survey by [arxiv_2405.06211] situates RAG within the broader context of retrieval-augmented LLMs, examining the full stack of components including the retrieval module, the language model backbone, and the augmentation strategies that connect them. It provides a comprehensive treatment of the capabilities that retrieval augmentation enables, including the incorporation of up-to-date information, the reduction of hallucination, and the support for knowledge-intensive tasks such as open-domain question answering and fact verification. This survey is particularly attentive to the practical deployment considerations of RAG systems, including the trade-offs between retrieval latency and generation quality, and the challenges of scaling retrieval to large and heterogeneous knowledge bases.

Taken together, these surveys reveal a field that is converging on a shared understanding of the core problems RAG addresses while diverging in the frameworks used to analyze and categorize solutions. The progression from Naive to Modular RAG described by [arxiv_2312.10997], the pipeline decomposition of [arxiv_2404.10981], the fusion taxonomy of [arxiv_2407.13193], and the system-level perspective of [arxiv_2405.06211] are complementary rather than competing accounts, each illuminating different facets of a complex and rapidly evolving research landscape. What unifies them is the recognition that the integration of retrieval and generation represents a fundamental architectural shift in how LLMs are designed and deployed, one with significant implications for the reliability, transparency, and adaptability of AI systems across a wide range of natural language processing tasks.

### Surveys and Overviews of RAG — evidence cluster B

The integration of retrieval mechanisms into large language model (LLM) pipelines has emerged as one of the most consequential developments in natural language processing research, addressing fundamental limitations of parametric knowledge storage while simultaneously introducing new technical challenges that demand systematic investigation. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has attracted substantial scholarly and industrial attention as a framework capable of grounding generative outputs in dynamically retrieved external knowledge, thereby reducing the propensity of LLMs to produce factually unsupported or hallucinated content [arxiv_2410.20598]. Yet, as the deployment of RAG systems has grown more widespread, so too has recognition of the framework's inherent complexities, prompting researchers to interrogate not only its benefits but also the conditions under which it succeeds or fails [arxiv_2309.01431].

A central motivation for adopting RAG architectures is the hallucination problem endemic to large language models. When LLMs are required to respond to queries that fall outside their training distribution or that demand precise factual recall, they frequently generate plausible-sounding but inaccurate information. By embedding relevant retrieved passages directly into the prompt context, RAG provides the model with a factual anchor that can constrain generation toward more accurate outputs [arxiv_2405.13008]. This basic insight, while straightforward in principle, conceals a range of technical difficulties that have become the subject of active research. The quality of retrieved documents is not guaranteed, and the model's ability to appropriately leverage, discount, or reject retrieved content varies considerably depending on both the retrieval mechanism employed and the intrinsic capabilities of the underlying language model.

Systematic evaluation of these capabilities has been undertaken through the construction of dedicated benchmarks. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation Benchmark (RGB) represents one such effort, providing a structured corpus in both English and Chinese designed to assess LLM performance across four fundamental competencies considered essential for effective RAG: noise robustness, negative rejection, information integration, and counterfactual robustness [arxiv_2309.01431]. Noise robustness refers to the model's ability to identify and utilize relevant information even when retrieved passages contain substantial irrelevant or distracting content. Negative rejection captures the capacity to abstain from answering when retrieved documents do not contain sufficient information to support a response. Information integration measures the ability to synthesize evidence distributed across multiple retrieved passages, while counterfactual robustness assesses whether models can appropriately handle retrieved content that contradicts their parametric knowledge. The RGB benchmark thus provides a multidimensional lens through which the strengths and weaknesses of different LLMs in RAG settings can be rigorously compared, revealing that performance is far from uniform across these dimensions and that identifying bottlenecks requires targeted evaluation rather than aggregate metrics alone [arxiv_2309.01431].

The retrieval component itself has received considerable attention as a locus of failure. Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), which encodes both queries and documents into a shared embedding space to enable semantic similarity search, has become a widely adopted retrieval backbone for RAG systems. However, standard DPR models exhibit limitations in retrieval accuracy, particularly in domain-specific settings where the vocabulary and conceptual structures of the target corpus may diverge from the general-domain data on which the retriever was trained [arxiv_2405.13008]. One approach to addressing this limitation involves the incorporation of control tokens into the DPR framework, enabling the model to better align its retrieval behavior with domain-specific requirements. Empirical results demonstrate that this augmentation yields a 13% improvement in Top-1 accuracy and a 4% improvement in Top-20 accuracy over the standard DPR baseline, suggesting that even relatively targeted modifications to the retrieval mechanism can produce meaningful gains in downstream RAG performance [arxiv_2405.13008].

A more fundamental challenge, however, concerns the semantic gap that exists between the retriever and the language model components of a RAG system. Because retrievers and LLMs are typically trained with different objectives and architectural assumptions, there is no guarantee that the documents a retriever ranks highly will be those that the language model can most effectively utilize. This misalignment places an undue burden on the LLM, which must independently adjudicate the relevance and reliability of retrieved documents using only its parametric knowledge, a task for which it may be poorly equipped [arxiv_2406.13249]. The R²AG framework has been proposed as a principled response to this problem, operating by incorporating nuanced retrieval-side features directly into the generation process. Specifically, R²AG employs a dedicated R²-Former module to capture and encode retrieval information, which is then integrated into the generation pipeline through a retrieval-aware prompting strategy [arxiv_2406.13249]. This approach contrasts with systems that treat the retriever and generator as largely independent components, instead establishing a tighter coupling that allows the generator to be informed not merely by the content of retrieved documents but by the retriever's own assessment of their relevance and quality.

The broader research community has increasingly recognized that addressing these challenges requires coordinated effort across multiple fronts. The R³AG workshop initiative, organized at SIGIR-AP 2024, reflects institutional acknowledgment that RAG has matured sufficiently as a research area to warrant dedicated forums for re-examining foundational principles and practical implementation strategies [arxiv_2410.20598]. The workshop's framing around "refined and reliable" RAG signals a shift in the field's orientation from demonstrating the basic utility of retrieval augmentation toward achieving consistent, trustworthy performance across diverse downstream tasks and deployment conditions. This trajectory mirrors the evolution of other foundational NLP paradigms, wherein initial proof-of-concept demonstrations give way to systematic efforts to characterize failure modes, establish evaluation standards, and develop architectural innovations that address identified limitations.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here delineates a research landscape in which RAG is simultaneously celebrated as a powerful mechanism for grounding LLM outputs and scrutinized for the technical gaps that prevent it from fulfilling its potential reliably. The interplay between retrieval quality, semantic alignment between retriever and generator, and the intrinsic capabilities of the language model itself constitutes the central problematic of the field, with each strand of work contributing partial solutions that collectively advance understanding of how robust RAG systems can be constructed and evaluated [arxiv_2309.01431][arxiv_2405.13008][arxiv_2406.13249][arxiv_2410.20598].

### RAG System Components and Architecture Improvements — evidence cluster A

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms in the development of large language models (LLMs), addressing a fundamental limitation of parametric knowledge systems: their inability to access up-to-date, domain-specific, or otherwise externally grounded information at inference time. By coupling a retrieval mechanism with a generative model, RAG frameworks expand the effective knowledge horizon of LLMs without requiring costly retraining or fine-tuning, making them particularly well-suited to knowledge-intensive tasks such as open-domain question answering (ODQA). The literature surveyed here reflects both the promise and the persistent challenges of this approach, spanning issues of retrieval quality, document relevance, query formulation, knowledge organization, and the comparative merits of retrieval-based versus generation-based strategies.

A foundational tension in the RAG literature concerns the reliability and completeness of the retrieval stage. Standard single-pass retrieval, in which a query is issued once against an external knowledge base and the top-ranked documents are passed directly to the generator, has been shown to be insufficient for complex queries where relevant evidence is distributed across multiple documents with varying degrees of surface-level similarity to the original query. Addressing this limitation directly, the DR-RAG framework proposes a two-stage dynamic document relevance approach that improves recall by recognizing that certain critical documents may exhibit low direct relevance to the original query yet can be recovered by combining partial document content with the query in a subsequent retrieval pass [arxiv_2406.07348]. This insight—that relevance is not always a static, pairwise property between a query and a document but can be dynamically constructed through iterative combination—represents a meaningful departure from conventional retrieval assumptions. DR-RAG further incorporates a compact classifier to manage efficiency, ensuring that the additional retrieval stage does not impose prohibitive computational overhead [arxiv_2406.07348]. The framework thus illustrates a broader principle: that improving retrieval recall in RAG systems may require moving beyond single-query paradigms toward multi-step or iterative retrieval architectures.

A complementary line of inquiry focuses not on the number of retrieval passes but on the quality and diversity of the queries themselves. BlendFilter addresses the problem of noisy and incomplete retrieval by introducing a query generation blending strategy that integrates both external knowledge augmentation and internal LLM knowledge with the original query before retrieval is performed [arxiv_2402.11129]. By enriching the query signal prior to retrieval, BlendFilter aims to ensure more comprehensive information gathering from the knowledge base. Crucially, however, the authors recognize that broader retrieval inevitably introduces irrelevant or contradictory content, and they therefore pair the blending mechanism with a knowledge filtering module that leverages the intrinsic capabilities of the LLM to eliminate extraneous retrieved passages [arxiv_2402.11129]. This dual-component design—expand the retrieval net, then prune the results—reflects a recurring architectural logic in the RAG literature, wherein the challenges of under-retrieval and over-retrieval are treated as complementary problems requiring coordinated solutions. Compared to DR-RAG, which addresses incompleteness through dynamic multi-stage retrieval, BlendFilter addresses it through richer query formulation, suggesting that these two strategies may be orthogonal and potentially complementary.

A structurally distinct approach to improving retrieval quality is pursued by the KG²RAG framework, which argues that the dominant paradigm of semantic chunk retrieval is fundamentally limited by its treatment of retrieved passages as isolated units, ignoring the relational structure that connects them [arxiv_2502.06864]. By integrating knowledge graphs (KGs) into the retrieval pipeline, KG²RAG is able to represent and exploit fact-level relationships between chunks, enabling a KG-guided chunk expansion process that surfaces contextually related passages that purely semantic similarity metrics would miss, as well as a KG-based chunk organization process that arranges retrieved content into coherent, well-structured paragraphs for the generator [arxiv_2502.06864]. Evaluated on the multi-hop reasoning benchmark HotpotQA, this approach demonstrates particular strength in scenarios requiring the synthesis of information from multiple sources—precisely the setting where isolated chunk retrieval is most likely to fail. In this respect, KG²RAG addresses a limitation that is also implicitly targeted by DR-RAG's dynamic relevance mechanism, though through a fundamentally different representational strategy: where DR-RAG relies on iterative query reformulation to discover latent document connections, KG²RAG encodes those connections explicitly in a structured knowledge graph.

Situating these specialized RAG architectures within a broader comparative context, recent systematic evaluations of retrieval and generation paradigms provide important empirical grounding. A comprehensive comparison of retrieval-based, generation-based, and hybrid models on ODQA and related tasks finds that dense retrievers such as Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) achieve strong performance—50.17% top-1 accuracy on the Natural Questions benchmark—while hybrid models that combine sparse retrieval methods like BM25 with dense approaches improve nDCG@10 scores on the BEIR benchmark from 43.42 to 52.59, demonstrating the complementary strengths of lexical and semantic matching [arxiv_2502.20245]. Generative models such as GPT-4-o offer richer contextual understanding but face well-documented challenges in maintaining factual consistency, underscoring the continued relevance of retrieval augmentation as a grounding mechanism [arxiv_2502.20245]. These findings contextualize the architectural innovations described above: the gains pursued by DR-RAG, BlendFilter, and KG²RAG are built upon a retrieval foundation whose baseline capabilities and limitations are now relatively well characterized empirically.

Taken together, the works reviewed here converge on a shared diagnosis—that standard single-query, semantically-driven retrieval is insufficient for complex, knowledge-intensive tasks—while proposing diverse remedies ranging from iterative retrieval and query blending to structured knowledge graph integration. The field is thus characterized by a productive pluralism of approaches, each illuminating a different facet of the retrieval-generation interface.

### RAG System Components and Architecture Improvements — evidence cluster B

The foundational challenge motivating retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lies in the inherent limitations of purely parametric language models. Large pre-trained language models store factual knowledge implicitly within their parameters and achieve strong performance on many downstream tasks through fine-tuning; however, their capacity to access and precisely manipulate knowledge remains constrained, causing them to underperform on knowledge-intensive tasks relative to task-specific architectures [arxiv_2005.11401]. Beyond accuracy, two further problems compound this limitation: providing verifiable provenance for model decisions and updating world knowledge without retraining. The seminal work by Lewis and colleagues addressed these concerns by introducing a general-purpose fine-tuning recipe that combines pre-trained parametric memory with a differentiable, non-parametric retrieval component [arxiv_2005.11401]. In this formulation, a retriever fetches relevant documents from an external corpus at inference time, and a sequence-to-sequence generator conditions its output on both the original query and the retrieved passages. By separating the storage of knowledge from the model weights, RAG enables knowledge to be updated simply by modifying the external index, without any retraining of the underlying language model. This architectural insight established a paradigm that has since been extensively studied, extended, and critiqued.

Subsequent survey work has sought to systematize the rapidly expanding landscape of retrieval-augmented language models. The survey by Hu and colleagues distinguishes between Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and Retrieval-Augmented Understanding (RAU), treating them together under the umbrella of Retrieval-Augmented Language Models (RALMs) [arxiv_2404.19543]. This taxonomy is useful because it clarifies that retrieval augmentation is not confined to generative tasks but extends to comprehension and classification settings as well. The survey decomposes RALMs into three interacting components—retrievers, language models, and augmentation mechanisms—and argues that the diversity of model structures observed in the literature arises from the different ways these components are combined and trained [arxiv_2404.19543]. Hallucination and the need for domain-specific knowledge are identified as the two primary failure modes that retrieval augmentation is designed to mitigate, a framing consistent with the original motivation articulated by Lewis and colleagues [arxiv_2005.11401]. Together, these two works establish both the historical origins and the conceptual vocabulary of the RAG paradigm.

Despite its widespread adoption, RAG is not without practical drawbacks. Retrieval introduces latency, because each query requires a search over a potentially large index before generation can begin. Retrieval also introduces error, since the selected documents may be irrelevant, contradictory, or only partially informative, and these errors propagate into the generated output. Furthermore, the overall system complexity increases substantially compared to a standalone language model, raising engineering and maintenance costs [arxiv_2412.15605]. These concerns have motivated researchers to explore alternative paradigms. One such alternative, cache-augmented generation (CAG), exploits the dramatically extended context windows available in recent large language models to preload all relevant documents into the model's context at initialization time, caching the resulting key-value runtime parameters [arxiv_2412.15605]. During inference, the model answers queries by attending to this preloaded context without any additional retrieval step, thereby eliminating retrieval latency and the risk of document selection errors. Comparative analyses reported by the authors indicate that CAG can match or exceed RAG performance on tasks where the relevant knowledge base is of limited and manageable size [arxiv_2412.15605]. This finding is significant because it suggests that the necessity of real-time retrieval is contingent on the scale and variability of the knowledge domain; for bounded, stable corpora, the complexity of a retrieval pipeline may be unnecessary overhead.

However, CAG's reliance on extended context windows also exposes a fundamental tension within the RAG paradigm more broadly: the context window, however large, remains finite, and the information placed within it must be carefully selected to maximize utility. This concern is directly addressed by work on retrieval quality and passage diversity. A recurring problem in RAG systems is that retrieved passages, while individually relevant, may be highly redundant with one another, consuming context window space without contributing new information to the model's response [arxiv_2407.12101]. Prior methods such as Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) have attempted to balance relevance and diversity through explicit trade-off objectives, but such approaches require manual calibration of the trade-off weight and do not directly optimize for the informational value added by each retrieved passage. To address this, a novel metric called relevant information gain has been proposed, which measures the probabilistic contribution of each candidate passage to the model's informational state given the passages already selected [arxiv_2407.12101]. By greedily selecting passages that maximize this metric, the method ensures that the context window is populated with passages that are both relevant to the query and non-redundant with respect to one another. This approach represents a more principled solution to the diversity-relevance trade-off than MMR, and its simplicity makes it practically attractive for deployment in real systems.

Taken together, these works illuminate the core tensions that define the current state of RAG research. The original RAG formulation [arxiv_2005.11401] established that combining parametric and non-parametric memory improves performance on knowledge-intensive tasks, and subsequent survey work [arxiv_2404.19543] has mapped the breadth of architectures and applications that have emerged from this insight. At the same time, critiques of retrieval latency and system complexity have motivated alternatives such as CAG [arxiv_2412.15605], while work on relevant information gain [arxiv_2407.12101] addresses the subtler problem of how to make the best use of a finite context window once retrieval has occurred. The field is thus moving simultaneously in two directions: toward reducing or eliminating retrieval where possible, and toward improving the quality and efficiency of retrieval where it remains necessary.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster A

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms in natural language processing, offering a principled mechanism for grounding the outputs of large language models (LLMs) in externally retrieved evidence. At its core, RAG integrates two complementary processes: a retrieval component that queries external knowledge sources such as document corpora, databases, or structured repositories, and a generative component that conditions its outputs on the retrieved material. This architectural marriage addresses a fundamental limitation of purely parametric LLMs, namely their tendency to produce plausible but factually incorrect statements—a phenomenon widely referred to as hallucination. The literature surveying this field has grown substantially in recent years, reflecting both the practical importance of RAG and the rapid pace of methodological innovation.

Comprehensive surveys of the RAG landscape have traced its development from early retrieval-augmented language models through to contemporary systems that operate across diverse task types and domains. One such survey situates RAG within a broader evolutionary arc, documenting how foundational retrieval mechanisms have been progressively refined and integrated with increasingly capable generative models [arxiv_2410.12837]. This work highlights that the basic architecture of RAG—wherein a retriever selects relevant passages and a generator conditions on them—has remained conceptually stable even as the specific instantiations of both components have grown considerably more sophisticated. The same survey identifies knowledge-intensive tasks such as open-domain question answering, summarization, and knowledge-based reasoning as the primary application domains in which RAG has demonstrated measurable gains over purely parametric baselines [arxiv_2410.12837].

A complementary survey adopts a knowledge-oriented framing, examining how RAG systems leverage external knowledge sources to augment generative models with dynamic, up-to-date information that cannot be fully encoded in static model parameters [arxiv_2503.10677]. This perspective is particularly valuable because it foregrounds the epistemological challenge at the heart of RAG: retrieved information must not only be accurate and relevant but must also be successfully aligned with the generative objectives of the downstream model. The survey proposes a taxonomy of RAG methods that spans from basic retrieval-then-generate pipelines to more elaborate architectures involving iterative retrieval, adaptive query reformulation, and multi-step reasoning [arxiv_2503.10677]. Together, these two surveys establish that the field has moved well beyond treating retrieval as a simple lookup operation; instead, retrieval is increasingly understood as an active, context-sensitive process that must be co-designed with the generation component.

Despite the conceptual clarity offered by these high-level surveys, the practical performance of RAG systems is heavily contingent on the quality of the queries submitted to the retrieval component. A recurring finding in the empirical literature is that vague or poorly specified queries lead to the retrieval of documents that are only tangentially related to the user's information need, which in turn propagates errors into the generated response and can itself be a source of hallucination [arxiv_2407.12325]. This observation motivates a line of research focused on query optimization as a first-class concern within the RAG pipeline. One study addresses this problem directly by introducing a query-document alignment score that is used to iteratively refine queries through LLM-based rewriting, with the goal of improving the precision and efficiency of document retrieval [arxiv_2407.12325]. Experiments reported in that work demonstrate an average accuracy gain of 1.6% attributable to the optimized query generation process, a modest but consistent improvement that underscores the sensitivity of end-to-end RAG performance to upstream retrieval quality. This finding is broadly consistent with the theoretical framing offered by the knowledge-oriented survey, which identifies the alignment between retrieved information and generative objectives as one of the central challenges in the field [arxiv_2503.10677].

A distinct but related challenge concerns the adequacy of general-purpose RAG frameworks when applied to specialized, domain-specific tasks. Standard RAG pipelines are typically designed around general document corpora and may lack the structural representations necessary to capture the nuanced relational knowledge that characterizes professional or scientific domains. This limitation has motivated the development of graph-based RAG architectures that replace or supplement flat document indices with knowledge graphs capable of encoding entities, relations, and domain-specific ontological structure. One recent proposal in this vein, DSRAG, introduces a multimodal knowledge graph-driven retrieval framework explicitly designed for domain-specific question answering [arxiv_2509.10467]. The system derives its knowledge graph from domain-specific documents, treating those documents as the primary source of structured knowledge rather than relying on pre-existing general-purpose graphs. The authors argue that this approach addresses two interrelated deficiencies of traditional RAG: insufficient domain knowledge accuracy and inadequate context modeling, both of which are particularly acute in specialized question answering scenarios where factual precision is paramount [arxiv_2509.10467].

Taken together, these contributions illuminate a set of recurring tensions that structure the current RAG research agenda. The first is the tension between generality and specialization: general-purpose RAG frameworks offer broad applicability but may underperform in domains requiring precise, structured knowledge, while domain-specific systems like DSRAG achieve higher accuracy at the cost of reduced transferability [arxiv_2509.10467; arxiv_2410.12837]. The second tension concerns the locus of intelligence within the pipeline: early RAG systems treated the retriever as a relatively passive component, but more recent work increasingly distributes reasoning across both retrieval and generation stages, as evidenced by query optimization strategies that use the LLM itself to reformulate queries [arxiv_2407.12325]. The third tension is between scalability and alignment: as retrieval corpora grow larger and more heterogeneous, ensuring that retrieved content is genuinely aligned with generative objectives becomes more difficult, a challenge that both major surveys identify as an open problem requiring further methodological development [arxiv_2503.10677; arxiv_2410.12837]. These tensions collectively define the frontier of RAG research and motivate the more targeted investigations reviewed in subsequent sections of this literature review.

### Evidence theme 3 — evidence cluster B

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has rapidly matured from a straightforward retrieve-then-read pipeline into a sophisticated ecosystem of interacting components, each introducing its own set of design choices, optimization challenges, and evaluation demands. Understanding the architectural evolution of RAG systems, the mechanisms by which their constituent parts are trained and coordinated, and the frameworks through which their behavior can be assessed represents a central preoccupation of recent scholarship. The literature surveyed here reflects this multidimensional complexity, addressing improvements in retrieval quality, end-to-end optimization strategies, modular evaluation methodologies, and the interpretability of retrieval-generation dynamics.

At the most foundational level, the basic RAG pipeline—retrieve a set of documents, then condition a language model's generation on those documents—has proven insufficient for the demands of real-world deployment. As [arxiv_2405.06683] observes, the integration of diverse components has caused the pipeline to evolve into an extended, sometimes loop-structured process. This evolution is driven by persistent failure modes: poor retrieval quality when questions require multifaceted semantic information, inefficiencies arising from redundant knowledge re-retrieval during long-term serving, and the absence of personalized response generation. These limitations are not merely incidental but reflect structural tensions between the retrieval and generation stages that simple pipeline concatenation cannot resolve. The ERAGent framework introduced in [arxiv_2405.06683] responds to these tensions by proposing a set of synergistically operated modules, including an Enhanced Question Rewriter and a Knowledge Filter, designed to improve retrieval quality at the query formulation stage before generation is ever attempted. This upstream intervention reflects a broader recognition in the field that the quality of retrieved context is a bottleneck that cannot be compensated for by improvements to the language model alone.

The question of how to optimize the retrieval and generation components jointly, rather than in isolation, has attracted considerable theoretical and empirical attention. [arxiv_2505.03075] provides a pointed critique of prevailing optimization strategies, noting that most prior work either fine-tunes the retriever while keeping the language model frozen or trains the language model to consume documents retrieved by a static, off-the-shelf retriever. Both approaches lack end-to-end training supervision and therefore cannot account for the interdependencies between the two components. More recent efforts have attempted joint training, but [arxiv_2505.03075] argues that these approaches rely on an overly simplifying assumption of document independence—treating each retrieved document as contributing independently to the generation objective—which diverges substantially from real-world retrieval scenarios where documents interact, overlap, and sometimes contradict one another. To address this, the authors propose the Direct Retrieval-Augmented Optimization (DRO) framework, which enables end-to-end training of a generative knowledge selection component alongside the language model. The emphasis on joint optimization and the explicit rejection of the document independence assumption marks a meaningful departure from earlier paradigms and situates DRO within a growing body of work that treats the retriever and generator as a unified system requiring coherent training signals.

While architectural and optimization advances have driven much of the RAG literature, the question of how to evaluate these systems rigorously has emerged as an equally pressing concern. [arxiv_2505.11180] identifies a significant gap in existing evaluation practice: most RAG benchmarks focus exclusively on text retrieval and rely on opaque, end-to-end assessments of generated outputs, making it difficult to diagnose which component of the pipeline is responsible for observed performance. This critique aligns with the broader movement in machine learning toward modular, interpretable evaluation. The mmRAG benchmark introduced in [arxiv_2505.11180] addresses this gap by integrating queries from six diverse question-answering datasets spanning text, tables, and knowledge graphs, converting all sources into a uniform retrievable document format. Crucially, mmRAG annotates document relevance following standard information retrieval procedures, enabling direct, granular evaluation of individual RAG components—such as retrieval accuracy and query routing—independently of end-to-end generation quality. This modular design philosophy contrasts sharply with the holistic evaluation approaches that have dominated the field and provides a methodological foundation for isolating the contributions and failure modes of specific pipeline stages.

The interpretability challenge extends beyond evaluation benchmarks to the internal dynamics of RAG workflows themselves. [arxiv_2508.06056] argues that despite significant advances in retrieval accuracy and response quality, the internal knowledge integration and retrieval-generation interactions within RAG systems remain largely opaque. This opacity is not merely an academic concern; it impedes practitioners' ability to diagnose errors, refine system behavior, and build warranted trust in deployed systems. RAGTrace, the interactive evaluation system introduced in [arxiv_2508.06056], is designed to surface these dynamics through a multi-level analysis approach that spans high-level performance evaluation, fine-grained examination of retrieval relevance, generation fidelity, and cross-component interactions. The system's design was informed by a comprehensive literature review and expert interviews, grounding its analytical categories in the practical concerns of researchers and developers. Compared with mmRAG [arxiv_2505.11180], which focuses on benchmark construction and quantitative modular evaluation, RAGTrace emphasizes interactive, qualitative inspection of retrieval-generation dynamics, suggesting that the two approaches are complementary rather than competing.

Taken together, these works reveal a field grappling simultaneously with the complexity of RAG architectures, the difficulty of end-to-end optimization, and the inadequacy of existing evaluation and interpretability tools. The trajectory from pipeline extension [arxiv_2405.06683] to joint optimization [arxiv_2505.03075], modular benchmarking [arxiv_2505.11180], and dynamic interpretability [arxiv_2508.06056] reflects a maturing research agenda in which improving RAG performance and understanding RAG behavior are increasingly recognized as inseparable goals.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster A

The evolution of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems has prompted substantial scholarly attention toward refining the architectural and functional relationships between retrieval and generation components. A central tension in this body of work concerns whether retrieval and generation should remain modular and separable or be integrated into unified frameworks that allow each process to inform the other more fluidly. Addressing this tension requires understanding both the capabilities that RAG models must possess and the evaluation criteria by which their performance can be meaningfully assessed.

Traditional RAG architectures treat retrieval and generation as sequential, largely independent stages, with explicit textual queries serving as the interface between them. While this separation has practical advantages in terms of modularity and interpretability, it introduces a structural mismatch: the retriever and the language model are optimized under different objectives, and the explicit query formulation step may not faithfully represent the model's underlying informational needs. This limitation has been identified as a key bottleneck in generalizing RAG systems across diverse knowledge-intensive tasks [arxiv_2506.02279]. The ImpRAG framework directly confronts this problem by proposing a query-free RAG system in which retrieval and generation are integrated into a single unified model. Rather than requiring human-specified queries to mediate between the two processes, ImpRAG allows the model to implicitly express its information needs by dividing a pretrained decoder-only language model into specialized layer groups that are jointly optimized for both retrieval and generation tasks. Crucially, this approach employs a two-stage inference process that uses the same model parameters and forward pass for both functions, thereby minimizing the representational disparity that typically arises when separate retriever and generator models are trained independently [arxiv_2506.02279]. This architectural unification represents a significant departure from conventional pipeline-based RAG designs and raises important questions about the trade-offs between integration and interpretability.

While ImpRAG addresses the structural separation between retrieval and generation at the model architecture level, other work has focused on the specific capabilities that the generation component of a RAG system must develop in order to handle the imperfect and heterogeneous outputs that real-world retrieval systems produce. The HIRAG framework articulates this concern by arguing that RAG generative models require three progressively hierarchical abilities: filtering, which involves selecting relevant information from retrieved documents; combination, which involves synthesizing semantic information across multiple sources; and deeper reasoning capabilities that build upon these foundational skills [arxiv_2507.05714]. This hierarchical framing is notable because it moves beyond the common assumption that in-context learning capabilities of large language models are sufficient for effective RAG generation. HIRAG's authors observe that existing fine-tuning approaches for RAG generative models often lack granular focus on RAG-specific tasks and fail to leverage chain-of-thought reasoning in a structured way, resulting in systems that are poorly equipped to handle inconsistent document quality or retrieval imperfections [arxiv_2507.05714]. Compared to ImpRAG's emphasis on architectural unification, HIRAG's contribution is more focused on the training and capability development of the generation model itself, suggesting that these two lines of inquiry are complementary rather than competing.

A third dimension of RAG system design concerns personalization and user-centricity. Standard RAG frameworks retrieve documents based on query similarity without accounting for the specific context, preferences, or interaction history of individual users, which can result in responses that are technically accurate but poorly tailored to user needs. PersonaRAG addresses this gap by incorporating user-centric agents that adapt both retrieval and generation processes based on real-time user data and interactions [arxiv_2407.09394]. Evaluated across multiple question answering datasets, PersonaRAG demonstrates performance advantages over baseline models by providing answers that are more closely aligned with individual user requirements. This work highlights an important dimension of RAG system design that is often overlooked in architecture-focused research: the relevance of a retrieved document is not an objective property but is partly determined by who is asking the question and in what context. In this respect, PersonaRAG's contribution is orthogonal to both ImpRAG and HIRAG, as it addresses the social and contextual layer of retrieval rather than the structural or capability-based layers.

Across all of these architectural and functional innovations, a persistent challenge is the evaluation of RAG systems. Because RAG combines a retrieval component with a generative component, and because its outputs depend on dynamic external knowledge sources, standard NLP evaluation metrics are often insufficient or poorly suited to capturing system quality. The survey by [arxiv_2405.07437] provides a comprehensive overview of this challenge, proposing a Unified Evaluation Process of RAG (Auepora) that examines and compares quantifiable metrics for both retrieval and generation components. These metrics include relevance, accuracy, and faithfulness, each of which captures a different aspect of system performance and corresponds to different potential failure modes [arxiv_2405.07437]. Relevance concerns whether retrieved documents are topically appropriate; accuracy concerns whether the generated output is factually correct; and faithfulness concerns whether the generated output is grounded in the retrieved content rather than in the model's parametric knowledge. The survey also analyzes existing benchmarks and datasets, identifying limitations in how current evaluation frameworks handle the hybrid structure of RAG systems and the variability introduced by dynamic knowledge sources [arxiv_2405.07437].

Taken together, these works reveal that progress in RAG research is occurring along several simultaneous fronts: architectural integration of retrieval and generation [arxiv_2506.02279], capability-oriented training of generative models [arxiv_2507.05714], personalization of retrieval and generation processes [arxiv_2407.09394], and the development of robust evaluation methodologies [arxiv_2405.07437]. Each of these directions addresses a distinct limitation of conventional RAG pipelines, and their convergence suggests that future systems will need to be simultaneously more unified in architecture, more capable in generation, more responsive to user context, and more rigorously evaluated against multi-dimensional criteria.

### Evidence theme 4 — evidence cluster B

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has emerged as one of the most consequential paradigms for addressing the well-documented limitations of large language models (LLMs), particularly their susceptibility to hallucination and their reliance on static, potentially outdated parametric knowledge. By coupling generative models with external information retrieval mechanisms, RAG systems aim to ground model outputs in verifiable, dynamically accessible evidence. The literature surveyed here reflects a broad and rapidly evolving research agenda, spanning architectural innovations, iterative retrieval strategies, unified corpus-level modeling, and the increasingly complex challenge of evaluating these hybrid systems in the era of frontier LLMs.

The foundational motivation for RAG research is well articulated across the literature: LLMs, despite their remarkable capabilities as text processors and reasoners, remain subject to critical limitations including knowledge cutoffs and the generation of factually unsupported content [arxiv_2305.15294]. These limitations are especially pronounced in knowledge-intensive tasks, where accurate factual grounding is not merely desirable but essential [arxiv_2402.01176]. Traditional retrieval modules, while effective in many settings, have historically operated as disconnected components that rely on large static document indices and are not jointly optimized with the generative objectives they are meant to support [arxiv_2402.01176]. This architectural disconnect has motivated a wave of research seeking tighter integration between retrieval and generation, with the goal of producing systems that are simultaneously more accurate, more adaptable, and more computationally coherent.

One significant line of inquiry concerns the challenge of relevance modeling in retrieval, particularly for queries with complex or multi-faceted information needs. Standard single-pass retrieval pipelines often fail to capture the full informational requirements of a query, especially when the answer depends on synthesizing multiple pieces of evidence or when the query itself is underspecified at the time of initial retrieval. To address this, Iter-RetGen proposes a method that synergizes retrieval and generation in an iterative manner, using the model's intermediate outputs as informative context for subsequent retrieval steps [arxiv_2305.15294]. The core insight is that a model's partial output reveals what additional knowledge may be needed to complete a task, thereby enabling progressively more targeted retrieval across multiple rounds. This iterative synergy represents a meaningful departure from static retrieval pipelines and demonstrates that strong performance on knowledge-intensive benchmarks can be achieved without fundamentally restructuring the underlying model architecture.

A complementary but architecturally more ambitious approach is pursued by CorpusLM, which proposes a unified language model capable of handling generative retrieval, closed-book generation, and retrieval-augmented generation within a single framework governed by a unified greedy decoding procedure [arxiv_2402.01176]. Rather than treating retrieval as a preprocessing step external to the generative model, CorpusLM integrates the corpus directly into the model's generative process by having the model produce document identifiers (DocIDs) as part of its output. This generative retrieval paradigm offers several theoretical advantages over traditional dense or sparse retrieval approaches, including the potential for end-to-end optimization and the elimination of the index-generation disconnect that characterizes pipeline-based systems. Importantly, CorpusLM also explores the previously underexamined relationship between generative retrieval and downstream task performance, arguing that the two objectives can be mutually reinforcing rather than competing. Compared to the iterative approach of Iter-RetGen [arxiv_2305.15294], CorpusLM represents a more radical unification, though both share the underlying conviction that tighter coupling between retrieval and generation is essential for advancing the state of the art.

More recent work has extended these architectural concerns to the domain of dynamic retrieval optimization. The context-guided dynamic retrieval framework proposed in [arxiv_2504.19436] addresses what the authors characterize as the limitations of static RAG structures in context adaptation and knowledge access. The system introduces a state-aware dynamic knowledge retrieval mechanism alongside a multi-level perceptive retrieval vector construction strategy and a differentiable document matching path, enabling end-to-end joint training and collaborative optimization of the retrieval and generation modules. Evaluated across multiple frontier models including GPT-4, GPT-4o, and DeepSeek on the Natural Questions dataset, this approach demonstrates that dynamic, context-sensitive retrieval can yield meaningful improvements in semantic understanding and knowledge scheduling efficiency [arxiv_2504.19436]. This work shares with Iter-RetGen [arxiv_2305.15294] a concern for the inadequacy of static retrieval in handling complex generation tasks, but pursues a solution through differentiable architectural design rather than iterative prompting strategies.

Cutting across all of these architectural developments is the challenge of evaluation, which has itself become a major research focus as RAG systems grow in complexity. The hybrid architecture of RAG systems—combining retrieval and generation components with dependence on dynamic knowledge sources—introduces evaluation challenges that traditional NLP benchmarks are ill-equipped to address [arxiv_2504.14891]. A comprehensive survey of RAG evaluation methods and frameworks identifies the need to assess not only system performance and factual accuracy, but also safety and computational efficiency, across both traditional and emerging evaluation paradigms [arxiv_2504.14891]. The survey further compiles and categorizes RAG-specific datasets and evaluation frameworks, conducting a meta-analysis of evaluation practices that reveals significant inconsistencies in how the field measures progress. This observation is particularly salient when comparing the experimental setups of the architectural papers reviewed here: Iter-RetGen [arxiv_2305.15294] and CorpusLM [arxiv_2402.01176] each employ distinct benchmarks and metrics, making direct comparison difficult and underscoring the need for standardized evaluation infrastructure of the kind advocated in [arxiv_2504.14891].

Taken together, the literature reflects a field in active transition, moving from pipeline-based RAG architectures toward more deeply integrated, dynamically adaptive, and jointly optimized systems. The progression from iterative retrieval-generation synergy [arxiv_2305.15294] to unified corpus-level modeling [arxiv_2402.01176] and context-guided dynamic retrieval [arxiv_2504.19436] illustrates a consistent trajectory toward eliminating the boundaries between retrieval and generation. Simultaneously, the growing attention to evaluation methodology [arxiv_2504.14891] signals a maturing awareness that architectural innovation must be accompanied by rigorous, standardized assessment if the field is to make reliable cumulative progress.
