# Continual Learning

## Continual Learning: A Survey

### 1. Benchmarks, Scenarios, and Evaluation Frameworks

#### 1.1 Continual Learning Scenarios and Taxonomies

The establishment of standardized scenarios and evaluation frameworks has been central to advancing continual learning (CL) research. A foundational contribution is the taxonomy proposed by van de Ven and Tolias [arxiv_1904.07734], which delineates three distinct CL scenarios—task-incremental, domain-incremental, and class-incremental learning—each differing in what information is available at test time and what the model is expected to infer. This structured decomposition addressed a critical problem: the lack of comparability across studies arising from inconsistent evaluation protocols. The taxonomy has since become a standard reference point for situating experimental results, and subsequent surveys have built directly upon it [arxiv_1909.08383, ss_475710].

Building on this need for rigorous evaluation, De Lange et al. [arxiv_2205.13452] identified a further methodological gap by demonstrating that standard evaluation practices—which typically assess model performance only at the end of each task—obscure a phenomenon they term the "stability gap": a transient but substantial drop in performance on previously learned tasks that occurs at the onset of training on a new task. This finding underscores the importance of continual, fine-grained evaluation rather than snapshot-based assessment, and implies that end-of-task accuracy metrics alone present an overly optimistic picture of method stability.

Cossu et al. [arxiv_2112.02925] further challenged the prevailing tendency to evaluate CL systems almost exclusively under class-incremental conditions, contending that such scenarios alone are insufficient to capture the full complexity of real-world CL demands. This critique underscores the need for more diverse and representative benchmarking environments. Harun et al. [arxiv_2303.18171] shifted the evaluative lens toward computational efficiency, noting that despite the motivating premise of avoiding full retraining, many contemporary CL algorithms have not been rigorously assessed for their efficiency properties—revealing a gap between theoretical motivation and empirical benchmarking practice.

#### 1.2 Benchmark Proposals Across Modalities

At the benchmark level, the CVPR 2020 Continual Learning in Computer Vision Competition [arxiv_2009.09929] documented both the progress made and the persistent challenges in benchmarking diverse CL approaches under unified conditions. Lomonaco et al. [arxiv_2011.01844] provided a comprehensive empirical study of class-incremental learning algorithms on visual tasks, offering systematic comparisons across methods and datasets.

Several works have extended evaluation frameworks to underexplored modalities. Ehret et al. [arxiv_2006.12109] provide a comprehensive evaluation of established CL methods applied to sequential data processed by recurrent neural networks, filling a notable gap given that most benchmarks focus on static, image-based inputs. Srinivasan et al. [arxiv_2206.09059] introduce CLiMB, a benchmark specifically designed for vision-and-language tasks, highlighting that existing benchmarks are largely confined to unimodal settings and thus fail to reflect the multimodal nature of many practical applications. In the video domain, Villa et al. [arxiv_2201.09381] present vCLIMB, addressing the imbalanced class distributions and unsuitable datasets that have hampered prior video CL research, and demonstrating that findings from image-based CL do not straightforwardly transfer to the video domain.

Collectively, these contributions reveal a consistent pattern: as the field matures, benchmark proposals increasingly challenge the sufficiency of established evaluation norms, pushing toward more diverse, realistic, and comprehensive assessment frameworks.

### 2. Architectural and Model Design Strategies

#### 2.1 Stability–Plasticity Trade-offs and Modular Designs

Architectural and model design strategies represent a central axis of CL research, with investigators pursuing fundamentally different philosophies regarding how network parameters should be structured and shared across tasks. The recurring tension is the stability–plasticity trade-off: models must retain previously acquired knowledge while remaining sufficiently flexible to incorporate new information. Kim et al. [arxiv_2303.09483] address this challenge directly by introducing auxiliary networks that mediate between stability and plasticity, arguing that standard single-network architectures are structurally ill-suited to balance these competing demands across sequential tasks.

One prominent line of work explores modular and multi-network designs as an alternative to monolithic architectures. Wang et al. [arxiv_2207.06543] theoretically analyze the generalization properties of continual learners and propose CoSCL, a framework in which cooperation among multiple small learners outperforms a single large model. Their analysis highlights that shared parameter sets induce severe inter-task interference, while fully dedicated subspaces limit scalability—a dichotomy that motivates their cooperative design. This stands in productive contrast to parameter-efficient approaches such as Self-Net [arxiv_1805.10354], which seeks to avoid linear parameter growth with task count, the need for rehearsal buffers, and restrictions on learning capacity simultaneously, framing these three constraints as the core obstacles any viable architectural solution must overcome.

Mirzadeh et al. [arxiv_2110.11526] demonstrate that wider neural networks exhibit systematically less catastrophic forgetting, suggesting that network capacity itself is a meaningful variable in CL performance, independent of the specific algorithm employed. This finding redirects attention toward model design as a lever for mitigating forgetting. However, Chen et al. [arxiv_2303.08207] challenge the assumption that reducing forgetting is inherently beneficial, presenting evidence that minimizing forgetting does not reliably correlate with improved forward transfer to new tasks—suggesting that the field's dominant optimization target may be incomplete.

#### 2.2 Dynamic Expansion and Transformer-Based Architectures

Mirzadeh et al.'s capacity findings motivate dynamic expansion methods that grow the network as new tasks arrive. Yan et al. [arxiv_2111.11326] propose DyTox, which uses transformer-based dynamic token expansion to reduce forgetting while managing parameter growth, though it acknowledges challenges around test-time task identity and tuning complexity. Complementing this, modular approaches decompose networks into reusable components: Mendez and Eaton [arxiv_2011.01272] propose automatically extracting modular subnetworks and estimating inter-task relatedness from these modules, enabling selective reuse and reducing interference between tasks.

Representation learning perspectives have also shaped architectural thinking. Douillard et al. [arxiv_2004.13513] propose PODNet, which applies pooled output distillation across intermediate network representations to preserve feature geometry learned on prior tasks. By anchoring the distillation signal to multiple spatial scales rather than only final outputs, PODNet demonstrates that architectural choices about where and how knowledge is consolidated are as consequential as the overall network topology. Collectively, these works illustrate that architectural design for CL is not a solved problem but an active frontier requiring careful co-design of network structure, parameter allocation, and knowledge preservation mechanisms [arxiv_2202.00275].

### 3. Replay and Generative Memory Methods

#### 3.1 Rehearsal and Exemplar-Based Replay

Rehearsal and replay-based approaches represent one of the most extensively studied strategies for mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The core intuition is that retaining and periodically revisiting information from previous tasks can prevent the neural network from losing previously acquired knowledge. Broad surveys confirm that replay-based methods constitute a major family of CL techniques alongside regularization and architectural approaches [arxiv_1909.08383, ss_475710].

A fundamental distinction exists between methods that store raw exemplars in a memory buffer and those that employ generative models to synthesize pseudo-rehearsal data. In the exemplar-based setting, the challenge of operating under strict memory budgets has motivated dedicated research: Fini et al. [arxiv_2008.01510] formalize the problem of Memory-Constrained Online Continual Learning, demonstrating that effective rehearsal remains feasible even under extreme storage limitations. Soutif-Cormerais et al. [arxiv_2308.10328] provide a comprehensive empirical evaluation of online CL methods, highlighting the trade-offs between memory size and performance in class-incremental scenarios.

A recurring concern with purely exemplar-based replay is the risk of overfitting to the stored buffer. As demonstrated by Verwimp et al. [arxiv_2210.06443], repeated optimization over a small, fixed memory pool can introduce a hidden pitfall whereby the rehearsed representations become overfitted, undermining the very stability the approach seeks to preserve. To address representational drift, Soutif-Cormerais et al. [arxiv_2302.11344] propose error sensitivity modulation during experience replay, drawing inspiration from biological learning mechanisms to moderate abrupt shifts in learned representations as new tasks arrive. Complementing this, Aljundi et al. [arxiv_1908.04742] argue that the selection strategy for replayed samples is critical, advocating for retrieval of maximally interfered examples to improve gradient updates in the online CL setting.

Vijayan et al. [arxiv_2310.08217] propose TriRE, a multi-mechanism framework that integrates experience rehearsal with weight regularization and parameter isolation, arguing that treating these strategies as orthogonal limits their individual effectiveness. Gao et al. [arxiv_2105.02085] draw inspiration from cognitive science, distinguishing between memory persistence and transience to selectively rehearse only the most schema-consistent experiences, thereby improving both efficiency and robustness.

#### 3.3 Prototype-Based and Representation-Centric Approaches

Prototype-based and representation-centric methods offer lightweight alternatives to both raw sample replay and generative synthesis. Wei et al. [arxiv_2308.00301] propose online prototype learning that maintains class prototypes updated from a single-pass data stream, complementing replay-based retention without requiring large memory banks. Wang et al. [arxiv_2207.13516] employ a contrastive vision transformer framework that uses focal contrastive learning to stabilize representations across sequential tasks without explicit task boundaries—a property particularly relevant to video streams where temporal continuity blurs task demarcations. Cui et al. [arxiv_2302.05936] extend this line of work to few-shot CL by combining contrastive adapters with mixture-of-experts mechanisms, enabling generalization across domains with minimal labeled data.

Addressing unsupervised CL, [arxiv_2204.05462] explicitly targets the setting without class labels by incorporating out-of-distribution detection to identify whether new data genuinely belong to novel tasks or to previously encountered distributions. SurpriseNet [arxiv_2310.20052] draws on anomaly detection to identify novel classes and accumulate cross-task knowledge without any replay mechanism. Feature-level generative approaches [arxiv_2005.03490, arxiv_2106.05350] synthesize or reconstruct feature-level representations of past tasks to counteract forgetting, offering a middle ground between raw exemplar storage and full generative replay. Together, these works suggest a broader shift toward representation-centric CL strategies that reduce dependence on labeled exemplars or costly generative models while still preserving the structural knowledge needed to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

### 4. Regularization and Knowledge Distillation Approaches

#### 4.1 Regularization-Based Methods

Regularization-based methods represent one of the most principled approaches to mitigating catastrophic forgetting, operating by constraining parameter updates to preserve knowledge acquired from previous tasks. The foundational intuition is that not all network parameters contribute equally to prior task performance, and selectively protecting the most important ones can substantially reduce forgetting [arxiv_1909.08383]. Memory Aware Synapses (MAS) [arxiv_1711.09601] exemplifies this paradigm by estimating parameter importance in an unsupervised manner, penalizing changes to weights deemed critical for previously learned tasks—a practically appealing approach given its ability to operate without labeled data when computing importance weights.

Building on synaptic consolidation ideas, Park et al. [arxiv_1908.02984] propose an asymmetric loss approximation strategy that overestimates the loss on one side, thereby more conservatively protecting parameters associated with earlier tasks. This asymmetric treatment addresses a known limitation of symmetric regularization penalties, which may insufficiently penalize parameter drift in directions particularly damaging to prior task performance. He and Lin [arxiv_2006.15078] reframe CL through an information-theoretic lens, defining forgetting as an increase in the description length of previous data under a sequentially updated model. This compression-based view provides theoretical grounding for variational posterior approximation methods, showing that such approaches implicitly perform regularization by constraining how far the posterior over parameters can shift between tasks.

Dropout has also been reframed as an implicit gating mechanism: [arxiv_2004.11545] demonstrates that dropout can partition network capacity across tasks, thereby reducing interference between old and new knowledge. More recent work by [arxiv_2301.00620] integrates dynamic modularity and sparsity into the network architecture, enabling selective parameter updates that protect consolidated knowledge while preserving plasticity for incoming tasks. While regularization methods are praised for their memory efficiency and scalability, surveys note that their effectiveness can degrade as the number of tasks grows, since cumulative regularization constraints may become overly restrictive or insufficiently precise [arxiv_1904.07734].

#### 4.2 Knowledge Distillation and Learning Without Forgetting

Knowledge distillation, as a mechanism for preserving previously acquired representations during sequential learning, was notably popularized in the CL context by Learning without Forgetting (LwF) [arxiv_2107.01349], which demonstrated that distillation losses could constrain a network's outputs on new tasks to remain consistent with its prior behavior. This foundational insight has since motivated a broad family of methods that treat the previously trained model as a teacher whose soft predictions guide the student network during subsequent training phases. The core appeal lies in the ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting without requiring explicit storage of past training data—a constraint particularly relevant given privacy and security concerns that prohibit data retention in many real-world deployments [ss_478606].

Building on this foundation, subsequent work has explored increasingly sophisticated distillation targets. Rather than restricting distillation to final output logits, researchers have investigated feature-level and response-level signals. In incremental object detection, distillation applied jointly to intermediate features and detection responses has been shown to better preserve the spatial and semantic structure of previously learned representations [arxiv_2204.02136]. This reflects a broader recognition that standard KL-divergence-based objectives may fail to capture important structural relationships encoded in teacher representations, motivating alternatives such as contrastive and Wasserstein-based distillation formulations [arxiv_2012.08674].

PODNet [arxiv_2004.13513] exemplifies a sophisticated distillation strategy by applying pooled output distillation across multiple spatial scales of a network's feature maps, enabling the model to better balance the trade-off between retaining old class representations and accommodating new ones over long incremental sequences. A complementary direction involves data-free distillation, wherein generative or variational mechanisms synthesize proxy inputs to approximate the distribution of previous tasks, entirely circumventing the need for stored exemplars [ss_478606]. Knowledge amalgamation strategies have also been proposed as an alternative paradigm, consolidating information from multiple specialist models rather than distilling sequentially from a single evolving network [arxiv_2209.02112].

Replay-free CL through prototype-sample relation distillation [arxiv_2303.14771] preserves relational structure in feature space rather than raw data, while [arxiv_2310.08217] argues that weight regularization, rehearsal, and parameter isolation have remained largely orthogonal research directions and proposes a unified multi-mechanism paradigm to overcome this fragmentation. Collectively, these approaches illustrate how the distillation framework has expanded well beyond its LwF origins, diversifying both the signals being preserved and the architectural assumptions under which forgetting is addressed.

#### 4.3 Architecture and Modular Approaches

Architecture and modular approaches offer a complementary perspective by structurally isolating parameters across tasks. Mirzadeh et al. [arxiv_2202.00275] make the important observation that most CL research fixes the network architecture and focuses solely on algorithmic innovations, largely overlooking how architectural choices themselves influence forgetting. Dynamic expansion methods address this gap by growing the network as new tasks arrive. #### 5.2 Episodic Memory and Lifelong Language Learning

Within the replay paradigm, episodic memory methods have been particularly influential in language settings. De Masson d'Autume et al. [arxiv_1906.01076] introduced sparse experience replay combined with local adaptation for lifelong language learning, demonstrating complementary benefits across text classification and question answering tasks. Building on such foundations, D'Autume et al. [arxiv_1909.11763] proposed improved schemes for episodic memory-based lifelong learning, extending gradient-based approaches such as GEM to achieve more reliable knowledge retention across sequential tasks.

Jodelet et al. [arxiv_2306.17560] leverage pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to generate class-representative images for distillation and replay in class-incremental learning, demonstrating that large-scale generative priors can substitute for stored data buffers. This approach contrasts with Graffieti et al. [arxiv_2204.05842], who introduce generative negative replay wherein synthesized out-of-distribution samples are interleaved with new experiences to sharpen decision boundaries and reduce interference, rather than simply reconstructing old class distributions.

### 6. Transfer, Forward Transfer, and Novel Settings

#### 6.1 Beyond Forgetting: Forward Transfer and Ecological Validity

CL research has increasingly moved beyond simply mitigating catastrophic forgetting toward understanding how sequential learning can actively facilitate knowledge transfer and generalize to novel, realistic settings. A central tension concerns whether reducing forgetting is itself a sufficient objective. Chen et al. [arxiv_2303.08207] directly challenge this assumption, demonstrating that the reduction of catastrophic forgetting—the primary metric optimized by most CL algorithms—does not correlate well with forward transfer of knowledge to new tasks. This finding calls into question whether forgetting less constitutes a good inductive bias for building systems that genuinely accumulate and reuse knowledge, motivating a reorientation of evaluation criteria in the field.

The question of distributional shift is central to ecological validity. Realistic deployment conditions involve not only class-incremental challenges but also distributional shifts across contexts, making the problem substantially harder than canonical benchmarks suggest. Models must generalize beyond the domains encountered during training, a gap between laboratory benchmarks and real-world applicability that standard evaluation frameworks have historically underemphasized [arxiv_1904.07734, arxiv_2303.18171].

#### 6.2 Prompt-Based and Vision-Language Continual Learning

Continual learning in prompt-based and vision-language settings has increasingly drawn on replay and generative memory strategies to address catastrophic forgetting across modality-rich data streams. Wang et al. [arxiv_2207.13516] employ a contrastive vision transformer framework that uses focal contrastive learning to stabilize representations across sequential tasks without explicit task boundaries. Cui et al. [arxiv_2302.05936] extend this to few-shot CL by combining contrastive adapters with mixture-of-experts mechanisms, enabling generalization across domains with minimal labeled data—a scenario closely aligned with prompt-based CL paradigms where task identity is conveyed implicitly.

The extension of generative negative replay to the vision-language domain [arxiv_2210.17322] illustrates how replay strategies developed for unimodal settings can be adapted to richer modalities. Wei et al. [arxiv_2308.00301] propose online prototype learning that maintains class prototypes updated from a single-pass data stream, complementing replay-based retention without requiring large memory banks. Together, these works illustrate a convergence between generative memory, prototype-based retention, and prompt-driven adaptation as complementary strategies for CL in complex, high-dimensional settings.

#### 6.3 Meta-Learning for Continual Learning

Meta-learning has emerged as a promising paradigm for addressing catastrophic forgetting, with researchers proposing frameworks that leverage learning-to-learn principles to enable rapid adaptation while preserving prior knowledge. A central challenge motivating this line of work is that standard CL procedures struggle to balance plasticity and stability across sequentially arriving tasks—a limitation that meta-learning approaches attempt to overcome through explicit optimization of cross-task generalization.

An important conceptual distinction within this space is drawn by Caccia et al. [arxiv_2004.11545], who differentiate between meta-continual learning, in which a model is pre-trained to minimize forgetting, and continual-meta learning, which targets faster adaptation to new tasks. This taxonomy clarifies that these two objectives, though related, require different algorithmic designs and evaluation criteria. Complementing this, Gao et al. [arxiv_2105.02085] demonstrate that meta-learning principles can be productively integrated with memory-based approaches, combining replay with meta-learning to enable rapid CL from few and noisy examples.

#### 6.4 Semi-Supervised and Unsupervised Continual Learning

The predominant focus of CL research on fully supervised settings has increasingly been recognized as a significant limitation, prompting growing interest in semi-supervised and unsupervised formulations that better reflect real-world data conditions. Addressing this gap, [arxiv_2204.05462] explicitly targets unsupervised CL by incorporating out-of-distribution detection to identify whether new data genuinely belong to novel tasks or to previously encountered distributions, enabling more realistic incremental learning without human annotations. This work highlights that the absence of supervision introduces an additional layer of complexity beyond catastrophic forgetting itself—namely, the challenge of correctly attributing incoming samples to their appropriate task context.

Anomaly detection principles have similarly been leveraged to support class-incremental learning without stored replay buffers. SurpriseNet [arxiv_2310.20052] draws on anomaly detection to identify novel classes and accumulate cross-task knowledge, explicitly avoiding any replay mechanism. This contrasts with generative replay approaches [arxiv_2005.03490, arxiv_2106.05350], which synthesize or reconstruct feature-level representations of past tasks to counteract forgetting rather than eliminating memory entirely.

Collectively, these works highlight a broader taxonomic expansion of the field beyond class-incremental supervised benchmarks—a concern also raised by Cossu et al. [arxiv_2112.02925], who argued that the community's narrow focus on class-incremental scenarios risks overlooking more ecologically valid learning conditions, including those involving limited or absent supervision. The semi-supervised variant of the problem is equally motivated by practical constraints: existing CL approaches largely fail to leverage unlabeled data in an ongoing fashion, and methods that incrementally exploit partially labeled data streams represent an important frontier for the field.

### 7. Synthesis and Open Challenges

The foregoing review reveals several cross-cutting themes and persistent open challenges in continual learning. First, evaluation remains a central concern: the stability gap identified by De Lange et al. [arxiv_2205.13452], the scenario diversity advocated by Cossu et al. [arxiv_2112.02925], the efficiency critique of Harun et al. [arxiv_2303.18171], and the forward-transfer challenge raised by Chen et al. [arxiv_2303.08207] collectively suggest that the field's standard metrics capture only a narrow slice of what it means to learn continually and well.

Second, the three classical families of approaches—regularization, replay, and architectural methods—have matured considerably but have largely developed in parallel. The argument of Vijayan et al. [arxiv_2310.08217] that these strategies have remained orthogonal, and their proposal of a unified multi-mechanism framework, points toward integration as a productive direction. Similarly, the convergence of Bayesian inference [arxiv_1710.10628, arxiv_2312.17210] and generative replay [arxiv_1907.10107, arxiv_2204.05842] suggests that principled probabilistic frameworks and flexible generative models are increasingly complementary rather than competing.

Third, the field is broadening its scope in several important directions: from unimodal image classification to video [arxiv_2201.09381], vision-language [arxiv_2206.09059, arxiv_2210.17322], and sequential text settings [arxiv_2006.12109, arxiv_1906.01076]; from fully supervised to semi-supervised and unsupervised formulations [arxiv_2204.05462, arxiv_2310.20052]; and from forgetting prevention toward richer notions of forward transfer and ecological validity [arxiv_2303.08207]. Architectural choices—long treated as fixed background—are increasingly recognized as first-class variables in CL performance [arxiv_2202.00275, arxiv_2110.11526], and modular designs [arxiv_2011.01272, arxiv_2111.11326] offer promising structural inductive biases for managing the stability–plasticity trade-off.

Taken together, these developments suggest that continual learning is transitioning from a field primarily concerned with preventing catastrophic forgetting in controlled benchmarks toward one grappling with the full complexity of lifelong, adaptive intelligence—a transition that will require tighter integration of theoretical foundations [arxiv_1710.10628, arxiv_2006.15078], richer evaluation frameworks [arxiv_2205.13452, arxiv_2303.18171], and more ecologically valid problem formulations [arxiv_2112.02925, arxiv_2204.05462].
