# Machine Learning for Drug Discovery

## Generative Models for De Novo Molecular Design

Generative deep learning models have emerged as a transformative approach to de novo molecular design, offering a computationally driven alternative to traditional drug discovery pipelines burdened by escalating costs and high attrition rates [arxiv_2008.09000]. Early frameworks demonstrated that sequence-based generative models could be steered toward desirable chemical properties through reinforcement learning; Olivecrona and colleagues showed that augmented episodic likelihood enables a recurrent model to produce analogues of query structures and to generate compounds predicted to be active against specific biological targets [arxiv_1704.07555]. This goal-directed paradigm represented a significant advance over unconditional generation, as it allowed practitioners to encode multi-objective criteria directly into the training signal.

Despite these successes, a persistent criticism of generative models is their tendency to propose molecules that are chemically novel yet practically unsynthesizable. Gao and Coley systematically evaluated this limitation, demonstrating that a substantial fraction of molecules proposed by leading generative frameworks cannot be readily accessed through known synthetic routes, thereby undermining their translational value [arxiv_2002.07007]. This finding underscores the need to incorporate synthesizability constraints as an explicit objective during model training or post-hoc filtering. The GuacaMol benchmarking framework was introduced specifically to address the related problem of inconsistent evaluation protocols, providing standardized tasks against which neural generative models can be systematically profiled and compared to established computational chemistry baselines [arxiv_1811.09621].

A further limitation of many early generative approaches is their chemocentric focus, which neglects the biological context in which a molecule must operate. Target-aware generation methods have been proposed that condition molecular graph generation on protein target information, directly bridging biological activity and structural design [arxiv_2202.04829]. Flow-based autoregressive models operating directly on molecular graphs address the dual challenge of producing chemically valid structures while simultaneously optimizing chemical properties [arxiv_2001.09382]. More recently, transformer architectures have been adapted for de novo design, with joint transformer frameworks combining decoder, encoder, and predictor components in a shared-weight generative model that formulates molecular generation as a probabilistic black-box optimization problem, explicitly targeting the difficulty of generating novel molecules outside the training distribution [arxiv_2310.02066]. Latent space methods such as LIMO employ latent inceptionism for targeted molecule generation, addressing the computational expense associated with reinforcement learning and Markov sampling when binding affinity calculations are involved [arxiv_2206.09010].

Extending the scope further, generative models have also been applied to the more complex problem of drug combination design, where graph-set generative architectures leverage network-level pharmacological principles to propose synergistic small-molecule combinations [arxiv_2004.07782]. Collectively, these developments illustrate a field progressively moving from unconstrained novelty toward biologically grounded, practically actionable molecular generation.

## Molecular Property Prediction and QSAR

Machine learning has become increasingly central to drug discovery, particularly in the prediction of molecular properties and the development of quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models. A comprehensive overview of this landscape is provided by [arxiv_2308.12354], which surveys a wide range of ML methods applied to small molecule property prediction, encompassing binding affinities, solubility, and ADMET profiles. Broad surveys of AI in drug discovery consistently identify molecular property prediction as one of the two central tasks driving the field, alongside molecule generation [arxiv_2106.05386].

A key distinction in the field lies between feature engineering and feature learning approaches. Traditional QSAR methods rely on handcrafted molecular descriptors, as exemplified by [arxiv_1904.05052], which employs protein star graph descriptors combined with classical ML classifiers to categorize signaling proteins relevant to drug targets. While this approach offers interpretability and leverages domain knowledge about molecular topology, it depends heavily on the quality and expressiveness of the chosen descriptors. In contrast, deep neural networks that perform feature learning directly from molecular structure promise to outperform both traditional physics-based and knowledge-based approaches by circumventing the limitations of manual feature engineering [arxiv_1803.04465]. PotentialNet, introduced in that work, is specifically designed to capture molecular properties across multiple length scales, from solubility to protein–ligand binding, using staged graph convolutional networks that encode both covalent and non-covalent interactions.

Directed message-passing neural networks have been shown to construct expressive learned molecular representations that compare favorably to models relying on pre-computed fingerprints or expert-crafted descriptors [arxiv_1904.01561]. Subsequent architectural refinements sought to balance expressiveness with computational efficiency; multiplex graph formulations incorporated molecular mechanics information to improve predictive accuracy [arxiv_2011.07457], while attention-based directed message passing further refined how structural information is aggregated across molecular graphs [arxiv_2305.14819]. Transformer-based chemical language models trained on large unlabeled corpora have demonstrated that unsupervised pretraining can capture rich structural and physicochemical information, alleviating the perennial problem of scarce property labels [arxiv_2106.09553]. These sequential token representations and graph-structured representations are complementary rather than mutually exclusive, each capturing different facets of molecular structure.

A meta-learning perspective on QSAR, wherein knowledge accumulated across many target-specific datasets is leveraged to improve predictions on new, data-scarce targets, has been advanced by Meta-QSAR [arxiv_1709.03854] and meta-learning GNN initializations [arxiv_2003.05996], the latter specifically targeting low-resource molecular property prediction. Parameter-free approaches such as Pareto-Optimal Embedded Modeling (POEM) seek to reduce the hyperparameter burden associated with representation and algorithm selection for ADMET prediction [arxiv_2002.04555]. Beyond graph-based representations, topological and algebraic descriptors provide an alternative means of encoding molecular structure; multicomponent persistent homology and electrostatic persistence have been shown to retain chemically and biologically relevant information, yielding competitive performance in scoring and virtual screening benchmarks [arxiv_1708.08135].

Beyond forward prediction, the field has also advanced toward inverse QSAR, where the goal is to design novel molecules with desired properties rather than merely evaluate existing ones. One framework combines artificial neural networks with mixed integer linear programming to solve this inverse problem, first learning a mapping from molecular feature vectors to target properties and then using mathematical programming to identify candidate structures satisfying specified property constraints [arxiv_2107.02381]. Together, these works illustrate a progression from descriptor-based classification toward end-to-end generative and optimization-driven paradigms in ML-assisted drug discovery.

## Structure-Based Drug Discovery and Binding Site Analysis

### Binding Site Detection and Protein–Ligand Scoring

Accurate identification of binding sites and reliable scoring of protein–ligand interactions are foundational challenges in structure-based drug discovery, and machine learning has increasingly been applied to address both. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) applied directly to three-dimensional representations of protein–ligand complexes can learn scoring functions capable of ranking binding affinities and poses, leveraging the growing availability of structural and binding data to outperform several classical scoring approaches [arxiv_1612.02751]. This work underscored how deep learning can capture spatial interaction patterns that hand-crafted energy terms may miss. Complementing this, a deep learning model for binding affinity prediction that learns task-relevant features directly from structural data highlights the advantage of end-to-end feature extraction over manual feature engineering [arxiv_1712.07042], and 3D segmentation networks have been employed to improve the identification of protein–ligand binding sites [arxiv_1904.06517], illustrating how deep learning can address multiple interconnected challenges within the structure-based drug discovery (SBDD) pipeline.

A comprehensive review by Özçelik et al. [arxiv_2212.13295] situates SBDD within the broader landscape of AI-driven drug discovery, noting that while ligand-based approaches have historically dominated deep learning efforts, structure-based methods offer distinct advantages by explicitly incorporating three-dimensional protein–ligand interaction information. Graph-based representations offer an alternative to voxelization by encoding molecular topology more naturally. MolTrans [arxiv_2004.11424] addressed drug–target interaction prediction by incorporating sub-structural interaction patterns through a transformer-based architecture, explicitly modeling the interaction between molecular fragments and protein sequences. These studies reflect a broader trend toward architectures that integrate structural priors with learned representations, progressively closing the gap between computational predictions and experimental binding measurements.

### Drug–Target and Drug–Protein Interaction Prediction

Predicting drug–target interactions (DTIs) and drug–protein interactions (DPIs) is a foundational challenge in computational drug discovery, and recent years have seen substantial methodological advances driven by deep learning and graph-based approaches. Early deep learning methods for DTI prediction relied predominantly on basic molecular representations derived from protein sequences and molecular structures, yet struggled to capture the complex interaction patterns between ligands and their targets [arxiv_2101.12547]. To address this, transformer-based architectures have been proposed: MolTrans employs a Molecular Interaction Transformer that moves beyond purely data-driven representation learning by incorporating domain knowledge and augmented interaction modeling, thereby improving prediction accuracy and interpretability for drug–target pairs [arxiv_2004.11424]. Similarly, the interpretable bilinear attention network introduced by Bai et al. explicitly models local interactions between drugs and targets while incorporating domain adaptation strategies to generalize predictions to novel drug–target pairs drawn from different distributions—a challenge that MolTrans and earlier methods do not fully resolve [arxiv_2208.02194].

Graph neural network (GNN)-based approaches offer a complementary perspective by leveraging relational biological data beyond raw sequences. BridgeDPI exemplifies this direction, constructing a GNN framework that bridges drug and protein representations through learned interaction graphs, achieving notable improvements over methods that extract features solely from primary sequence or structure information [arxiv_2101.12547]. Multi-view self-attention has been employed to improve interpretability alongside predictive accuracy [arxiv_2005.00397], and hierarchical graph representations encode both drug and target structures for binding affinity prediction, benefiting from deep learning's capacity to extract multi-scale molecular features [arxiv_2203.11458]. DeepGS jointly encodes molecular graphs and protein sequences to predict drug–target binding affinity, circumventing the need for experimentally resolved 3D target structures that are often unavailable [arxiv_2003.13902]. The DeepPurpose library has been developed as a comprehensive benchmarking and prototyping framework that consolidates multiple encoder architectures for both drugs and targets, lowering the barrier to entry for researchers with limited deep learning expertise and enabling systematic comparison across models [arxiv_2004.08919]. Collectively, these contributions illustrate a progression from sequence-only feature extraction toward richer, interaction-aware and graph-structured representations, with interpretability and cross-distribution generalization emerging as key open challenges.

### Protein–Protein Docking and Binding Affinity Prediction

Protein–protein docking and binding affinity prediction represent two interrelated challenges in structure-based drug discovery, both of which have been substantially advanced by machine learning. A foundational difficulty concerns the quality and representativeness of training data: datasets derived from the Protein Data Bank tend to be large and unwieldy, motivating the development of synthetic benchmarking environments to prototype and evaluate new learning-based docking algorithms more efficiently [arxiv_2212.03456].

Graph neural network architectures have emerged as particularly well-suited to encoding the three-dimensional structural information required for docking and affinity estimation. A dual-graph convolutional network that separately encodes ligand bonded topology and ligand–protein contacts derived from docking poses demonstrates that combining structural rank information with deep learning improves binding mode prediction [arxiv_1910.02845]. Complementarily, a three-dimensionally embedded graph convolutional network (3DGCN) that explicitly incorporates spatial molecular geometry reports superior performance over conventional deep learning approaches on biochemical activity tasks [arxiv_1811.09794]. These two contributions collectively underscore the importance of preserving three-dimensional context when learning binding-relevant representations.

For binding affinity estimation specifically, mathematical deep learning models integrating differential geometry and algebraic graph theory achieve competitive results in the D3R Grand Challenge 4 across pose prediction, affinity ranking, and free energy estimation tasks [arxiv_1909.07784]. The question of model confidence is equally pertinent here: assigning reliable uncertainty estimates to molecular property predictions is essential before such models can be trusted in practical drug discovery pipelines [arxiv_2102.11439]. Together, these works illustrate a field progressively balancing predictive power with structural fidelity and interpretability.

## Self-Supervised, Contrastive, and Transfer Learning for Molecular Representation

Self-supervised and contrastive learning approaches have emerged as powerful strategies for molecular representation learning, largely motivated by the scarcity of labeled chemical data relative to the vastness of chemical space. MolCLR [arxiv_2102.10056] exemplifies this direction by applying contrastive learning over molecular graphs via graph neural networks, leveraging augmentation strategies to learn transferable representations without requiring extensive labeled datasets. The authors demonstrate that such self-supervised pretraining can substantially improve generalization across property prediction benchmarks, directly addressing the challenge that supervised models face when confronted with the enormous diversity of chemical space.

Building on the contrastive paradigm, knowledge-aware approaches have sought to incorporate domain-specific chemical information into representation learning. Fang et al. [arxiv_2103.13047] propose integrating molecular fingerprints and functional group information into contrastive graph learning, arguing that purely structure-driven contrastive objectives struggle to capture property-relevant substructures and complex atomic vocabularies—a meaningful extension beyond MolCLR, which relies primarily on graph-level augmentations without explicit chemical domain knowledge. A complementary line of work explores topological enrichment of self-supervised objectives: Luo et al. [arxiv_2311.17327] introduce persistent homology as an auxiliary signal for self-supervised molecular representation learning, demonstrating that topological descriptors capture multi-scale structural features that standard graph-based SSL paradigms tend to overlook.

Graph Self-Supervised Learning (GSSL) addresses the labeled-data bottleneck by acquiring molecular embeddings without expert labeling, with its primary strength lying in transferability across domains [arxiv_2206.08005]. A systematic survey of chemical pre-trained models documents how self-supervised objectives can transfer generalizable representations to downstream property prediction tasks [arxiv_2210.16484]. Contrastive methods operating over three-dimensional molecular conformations have shown strong performance for property prediction tasks in this low-label regime [arxiv_2208.06360]. Foundation model approaches extend this logic further, using self-supervised pretraining to build general-purpose molecular encoders that can be fine-tuned for diverse downstream tasks with small datasets [arxiv_2211.02657].

Where self-supervised and contrastive methods focus on representation quality, transfer learning frameworks more explicitly target predictive performance on specific drug discovery tasks. Deep transfer and representation learning substantially improves compound activity classification when training data are scarce, directly addressing the data limitation that constrains supervised GNNs in structure–activity relationship modeling [arxiv_2111.07439]. Meta-learning offers a further route to data efficiency: frameworks that interpolate between meta-learning and conventional deep kernel learning within Gaussian process models can acquire transferable feature representations across tasks, enabling rapid adaptation to new property prediction problems with few labeled examples [arxiv_2205.02708]. Few-shot graph learning frameworks have been proposed that enable property prediction from limited experimental data [arxiv_2102.07916]. Beyond molecular property prediction, self-supervised learning has also been applied to drug repositioning, where Yang et al. [arxiv_2206.00262] address label sparsity by developing a self-supervised framework that exploits abundant unlabeled drug–disease association data. Collectively, these works illustrate a convergent recognition that self-supervised, contrastive, and transfer learning frameworks are essential for navigating the labeled-data bottleneck inherent to molecular machine learning.

## Lead Optimization and Chemical Space Exploration

### Probabilistic and Bayesian Methods

Probabilistic and Bayesian approaches have emerged as a principled framework for navigating the vast and complex chemical spaces encountered in drug discovery, offering mechanisms for uncertainty quantification that purely deterministic models cannot provide. Bayesian optimization (BO) has proven particularly influential, efficiently guiding experimental design by balancing exploration and exploitation in expensive black-box settings. Frazier and Wang [arxiv_1506.01349] provide a foundational treatment of BO for materials design, demonstrating that it can identify promising candidates in remarkably few experiments when designs are parameterized in low-dimensional spaces. Building on this foundation, Deshwal et al. [arxiv_2012.07762] extend BO to combinatorial spaces such as molecular graphs and sequences, directly addressing the structural complexity inherent in lead optimization tasks. Folch et al. [arxiv_2312.00622] further advance practical applicability by developing path-based BO that accounts for separable experimental costs, a consideration highly relevant to multi-step drug synthesis workflows.

Uncertainty quantification extends beyond optimization into predictive modeling itself. Liu and Kashima [arxiv_2009.08687] highlight that chemical property datasets are systematically biased due to experimental selection pressures, and propose methods to correct for these biases, thereby improving the reliability of learned predictive models. Isenberg et al. [arxiv_2309.06540] demonstrate how Bayesian optimal experimental design can be applied to pharmacodynamic models with uncertain parameters, enabling the strategic selection of experiments that maximally reduce predictive uncertainty in drug mechanism studies. Active learning frameworks similarly leverage probabilistic surrogates to improve screening efficiency: Graff et al. [arxiv_2205.01753] show that model-guided virtual screening with active design space pruning dramatically reduces the number of evaluations required to identify top candidates, with uncertainty-aware models playing a central role in directing exploration.

### Graph Neural Networks for Lead Optimization

Graph neural networks have emerged as a dominant paradigm for molecular property prediction central to lead optimization. Beyond the foundational GNN architectures discussed in earlier sections, a growing body of work addresses the interpretability of GNN-based models, which remains a barrier to their adoption in regulated drug discovery workflows [arxiv_2007.00523]. Quantitative evaluation frameworks have been developed to benchmark explainability methods applied to molecular GNNs [arxiv_2104.08060], and counterfactual explanation approaches generate chemically meaningful alternative structures to illuminate model decisions [arxiv_2007.00523, arxiv_2104.08060]. More recent work has also highlighted that incorporating inter-molecular relationships, rather than treating each molecule in isolation, can yield further gains in prediction performance [arxiv_2312.16855]. Pre-training on large unlabeled molecular corpora has attracted considerable attention as a means of addressing data scarcity in lead optimization contexts [arxiv_2210.16484], and the question of assigning reliable uncertainty estimates to molecular property predictions remains essential before such models can be trusted in practical pipelines [arxiv_2102.11439].

## Surveys and Overviews of AI in Drug Discovery

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into drug discovery has prompted a growing body of survey literature seeking to map the scope, progress, and limitations of the field. Hasselgren and Oprea offer a broad assessment, arguing that drug discovery is being reshaped by data science, informatics, and AI technologies that collectively aim to accelerate treatment development while reducing costs and reliance on animal experimentation, noting that rising interest from investors, academic scientists, and legislators reflects the field's momentum [arxiv_2307.06521]. Deng and colleagues provide a systematic overview of AI applications across the drug discovery pipeline, organizing the landscape around two principal tasks—molecular property prediction and molecule generation—and surveying the range of techniques deployed in applications such as virtual screening and drug design [arxiv_2106.05386]. Where Hasselgren and Oprea adopt a broader strategic and translational perspective, Deng et al. offer a more technically granular taxonomy of methods and applications.

A distinct but increasingly prominent strand of survey work focuses on the interpretability of AI models in this domain. Alizadehsani and colleagues address the growing complexity of AI and machine learning models in drug discovery, arguing that this complexity creates an urgent need for transparency and interpretability, and positioning Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) as a framework capable of addressing these concerns [arxiv_2309.12177]. This emphasis on explainability represents a meaningful extension of earlier surveys, which tended to foreground predictive performance over model transparency. Özçelik et al. [arxiv_2212.13295] situate structure-based drug discovery within the broader AI landscape, noting the growing imperative to develop architectures that can effectively exploit three-dimensional structural data. Collectively, these overviews establish that while AI has demonstrably transformed numerous stages of drug discovery, outstanding challenges—including data limitations, model interpretability, and translational validation—continue to define the frontier of the field.

Within the repositioning subfield, a recurring challenge is the accurate representation of drugs in a form that captures their known biological and pharmacological relationships. Self-supervised frameworks that exploit abundant unlabeled drug–disease association data have been shown to effectively regularize models when validated associations are scarce [arxiv_2206.00262]. These repositioning efforts connect naturally to the broader DTI prediction literature, where graph-based and attention-based architectures have progressively improved the reliability of predicted compound–target associations [arxiv_2208.02194, arxiv_2004.11424, arxiv_2101.12547].

## Synthesis and Open Challenges

Across the domains surveyed here—generative molecular design, property prediction and QSAR, structure-based drug discovery, representation learning, lead optimization, and AI overviews—several cross-cutting themes emerge. First, the transition from hand-crafted descriptors to end-to-end learned representations has been a defining trend, with graph neural networks [arxiv_1803.04465, arxiv_1904.01561, arxiv_2305.14819], transformer-based language models [arxiv_2106.09553, arxiv_2004.11424], and topological methods [arxiv_1708.08135, arxiv_2311.17327] each contributing complementary representational strengths. Second, data scarcity remains a fundamental bottleneck: self-supervised and contrastive pretraining [arxiv_2102.10056, arxiv_2206.08005, arxiv_2210.16484], meta-learning [arxiv_1709.03854, arxiv_2003.05996, arxiv_2205.02708], transfer learning [arxiv_2111.07439], and few-shot learning [arxiv_2102.07916] have all been mobilized to address this constraint, with meaningful but incomplete success.

Third, the reliability and trustworthiness of ML predictions—encompassing uncertainty quantification [arxiv_2102.11439], dataset bias correction [arxiv_2009.08687], and model interpretability [arxiv_2007.00523, arxiv_2104.08060, arxiv_2309.12177]—have emerged as prerequisites for deployment in regulated drug discovery workflows. Fourth, the synthesizability and practical accessibility of computationally generated molecules remain underappreciated challenges [arxiv_2002.07007], and the field increasingly recognizes that evaluation frameworks must assess not only chemical novelty but also synthetic feasibility and biological relevance [arxiv_1811.09621, arxiv_2308.12354]. Fifth, Bayesian and probabilistic methods [arxiv_1506.01349, arxiv_2012.07762, arxiv_2312.00622, arxiv_2205.01753] provide principled mechanisms for balancing exploration and exploitation in chemical space, and their integration with expressive deep learning representations represents a productive frontier.

Looking forward, the most impactful advances are likely to arise from tighter integration across these themes: generative models that are simultaneously target-aware [arxiv_2202.04829], synthesizability-constrained [arxiv_2002.07007], and uncertainty-calibrated [arxiv_2102.11439]; representation learning frameworks that transfer reliably across chemical domains [arxiv_2210.16484, arxiv_2211.02657]; and evaluation ecosystems that enable reproducible, standardized comparison across the rapidly diversifying methodological landscape [arxiv_1811.09621, arxiv_2308.12354]. The surveys reviewed here [arxiv_2307.06521, arxiv_2106.05386, arxiv_2309.12177, arxiv_2212.13295] collectively affirm that machine learning has already transformed numerous stages of the drug discovery pipeline, while also making clear that the translation of computational promise into clinical impact remains the defining challenge for the decade ahead.
