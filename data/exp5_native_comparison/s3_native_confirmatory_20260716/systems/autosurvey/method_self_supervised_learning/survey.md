# Self-Supervised Representation Learning: Methods, Theories, and Applications Across Domains

## 1 Introduction and Foundations of Self-Supervised Representation Learning

### 1.1 Motivation and the Annotation Bottleneck

## 1.1 The Annotation Bottleneck and the Case for Self-Supervised Learning

The remarkable success of deep learning over the past decade has been fundamentally underpinned by the availability of large-scale, carefully annotated datasets. From image classification and object detection to speech recognition and natural language understanding, supervised deep learning systems have consistently demonstrated that performance scales with the quantity and quality of labeled training data [1]. However, this dependence on human-annotated labels introduces a significant and often prohibitive bottleneck that constrains the broader applicability of deep learning across domains and modalities.

The cost of human annotation is substantial across virtually every data type. For natural images, annotating millions of samples with class labels, bounding boxes, or pixel-wise segmentation masks demands enormous investments of time and expert labor [2]. The challenge is magnified considerably for video data, where an annotator must watch entire recordings end-to-end before providing a single label, making large-scale annotation economically infeasible for many organizations [3; 4]. In speech and audio processing, transcription and speaker labeling require specialized linguistic expertise and careful attention, further escalating costs [5; 6]. For medical imaging modalities such as ultrasound, mammography, and histopathology slides, annotation must be performed by trained clinicians, making labeled data exceptionally scarce and expensive [7; 8]. In domains such as remote sensing, biological graphs, and scientific simulations, the annotation process requires domain expertise that is difficult to scale [9; 10].

Beyond raw cost, the annotation process introduces additional challenges including label noise, annotator disagreement, and inconsistencies arising from subjective or ambiguous category definitions [11]. Large-scale datasets obtained from web sources often carry inherently noisy labels that must be carefully managed [12; 13]. Furthermore, the requirement for human-generated annotations biases learned models toward the specific label taxonomy chosen at dataset creation time, limiting generalization to novel classes, tasks, or domains [3].

In stark contrast to the scarcity of labeled data, unlabeled data is abundant and freely available across essentially every domain and modality. The internet continuously generates vast quantities of unannotated images, videos, audio recordings, text, and sensor readings. Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a compelling paradigm that exploits this wealth of unlabeled data by constructing supervisory signals directly from the intrinsic structure of the data itself, without requiring any human annotation [14; 15]. Rather than relying on externally provided labels, SSL methods define so-called pretext tasks—auxiliary objectives derived automatically from the raw data—whose solution requires the model to learn meaningful and transferable feature representations.

The practical impact of this paradigm shift is substantial. Self-supervised pre-trained representations have been shown to dramatically reduce the need for labeled examples in downstream tasks [16; 17], to improve annotation efficiency when human labeling is still required [2], and to serve as strong initializations for fine-tuning on specialized domains where only small labeled datasets exist [18; 19]. Moreover, SSL representations have demonstrated greater robustness to dataset imbalance compared to their supervised counterparts, since they are not constrained to learn only label-relevant features [20]. Collectively, these properties establish self-supervised learning as a scalable, practical, and increasingly essential alternative to purely supervised approaches—one whose historical development, diverse methodological families, and broad applications this survey aims to comprehensively document.

Having established the fundamental motivation for self-supervised learning, the following section traces the historical arc of the field, from early unsupervised feature learning and handcrafted pretext tasks to the large-scale pretraining frameworks and foundation models that define the current state of the art.

### 1.2 Historical Context and Evolution of Self-Supervised Learning

## 1.2 Historical Context and Evolution of Self-Supervised Learning

Having established the fundamental motivation for self-supervised learning—namely, the annotation bottleneck and the abundance of unlabeled data—this section traces the historical arc of the field, from early unsupervised feature learning and handcrafted pretext tasks to the large-scale pretraining frameworks and foundation models that define the current state of the art. Understanding this evolution provides essential context for appreciating the design choices and motivations behind contemporary methods, and for situating the core concepts and terminology that will be introduced in the following section.

### Early Unsupervised Feature Learning and Autoencoders

The intellectual roots of self-supervised learning lie in classical unsupervised learning, where the primary goal was to discover compact, informative representations of data without relying on human-provided labels. Autoencoders, one of the earliest neural network-based approaches to unsupervised representation learning, were designed to reconstruct their inputs through a bottleneck architecture, forcing the network to learn a compressed representation of the data. Denoising autoencoders extended this principle by training networks to recover clean inputs from corrupted versions, introducing the idea that useful representations should be robust to noise. These early approaches established the foundational principle that the structure of the data itself could serve as a source of supervisory signal—a core insight that underlies all subsequent developments in self-supervised learning and that directly addresses the annotation bottleneck described in the preceding section.

Alongside autoencoders, restricted Boltzmann machines and deep belief networks explored generative approaches to feature learning through probabilistic modeling of data distributions. While these methods demonstrated that meaningful representations could be discovered without labels, they were often computationally demanding, difficult to scale, and outperformed by supervised methods when labeled data was available. As noted in comprehensive reviews of the field, traditional unsupervised methods such as layer-wise clustering or autoencoders remained inferior to supervised pretraining in many practical settings [21].

### The Era of Handcrafted Pretext Tasks in Computer Vision

A significant shift occurred when researchers began constructing explicit pretext tasks—auxiliary prediction problems defined over unlabeled data—to guide representation learning. Rather than asking networks to generalize across all possible tasks, pretext tasks provided concrete objectives that could be optimized end-to-end, encouraging networks to extract semantically meaningful features as a byproduct. As will be formalized in the following section, these pretext tasks are defined over automatically generated pseudo-labels derived from the intrinsic structure of the data itself.

One of the earliest and most influential pretext tasks in computer vision was image colorization, where a network is trained to predict the color channels of an image from its grayscale version. The intuition was compelling: to colorize a sky, a car, or vegetation correctly, the network must first learn to recognize these objects, thereby acquiring visual semantics in a fully self-supervised manner [21]. This work demonstrated that a single, well-designed pretext task could yield representations competitive with those learned by supervised methods on downstream recognition tasks.

Building on this success, a variety of handcrafted pretext tasks were proposed throughout the mid-2010s. Rotation prediction, in which networks are trained to classify which of four canonical rotations has been applied to an input image, became a widely studied approach. The rationale was that recognizing the rotation of an object requires understanding its canonical orientation, which in turn demands a degree of semantic understanding. Empirical studies later confirmed that rotation prediction is among the most semantically meaningful of the classical pretext tasks [22]. Other pretext tasks explored during this period included jigsaw puzzle solving, where the spatial arrangement of image patches is scrambled and the network must recover the correct permutation, relative patch position prediction, and context prediction from image regions.

While these handcrafted pretext tasks succeeded in extracting more meaningful representations than pure autoencoders, they also revealed an important challenge: networks often found shortcuts—low-level statistical regularities such as color aberrations, watermarks, or chromatic aberrations—that allowed them to solve the pretext task without learning semantically meaningful features [23]. This shortcut problem motivated researchers to design more robust pretext tasks and augmentation strategies that would discourage networks from exploiting superficial cues, a challenge that—as will be seen—ultimately shaped the design of the data augmentation pipelines central to modern SSL frameworks.

### Key Milestones in Natural Language Processing

In parallel with developments in computer vision, the NLP community made transformative advances in self-supervised representation learning. The introduction of word embedding methods such as Word2Vec and GloVe established that rich semantic relationships between words could be captured by training shallow neural networks on prediction tasks derived from raw text—predicting surrounding context words from a target word (skip-gram) or predicting a word from its context (continuous bag-of-words). These methods demonstrated at scale that the distributional structure of language provides abundant self-supervisory signals without any human-provided labels, directly instantiating the principle of exploiting intrinsic data structure that was identified in the annotation bottleneck discussion.

The subsequent development of contextualized word representations, exemplified by ELMo and then by the transformer-based GPT and BERT models, marked a qualitative leap in the power of self-supervised NLP pretraining. BERT in particular popularized the masked language modeling (MLM) objective, in which a fraction of input tokens are masked at random and the network is trained to predict them from surrounding context. This approach, combined with the expressive power of transformer architectures, yielded representations that achieved state-of-the-art performance across virtually every NLP benchmark through fine-tuning—often with dramatic reductions in the labeled data required [24]. The success of masked prediction in NLP became a guiding inspiration for subsequent work in other modalities, and the encoder architectures developed during this period form the backbone of many SSL frameworks that will be discussed throughout this survey.

### The Contrastive Learning Revolution in Computer Vision

While NLP advanced through masked prediction objectives, computer vision took a different trajectory. The emergence of contrastive learning methods around 2018–2020 represented a paradigm shift away from handcrafted pretext tasks and toward instance discrimination—a framework in which different augmented views of the same image are treated as positive pairs, while views from different images form negative pairs. By learning to maximize agreement between positive pairs while minimizing it between negative pairs, networks are encouraged to learn representations that are invariant to the specific augmentations applied while remaining discriminative across different instances. This multi-view principle—deriving supervisory signals from the correspondence between different augmented versions of the same data point—constitutes one of the central mechanisms of modern SSL, as will be formalized in the core concepts section that follows.

This period saw the rapid development of foundational contrastive frameworks. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) addressed the computational challenge of maintaining a large set of negative samples by introducing a momentum-updated encoder and a dynamic memory bank. SimCLR simplified the framework by directly contrasting within a large batch, and demonstrated that strong data augmentation and a nonlinear projection head were critical for learning high-quality representations. These methods dramatically narrowed the gap between self-supervised and fully supervised pretraining on ImageNet, and demonstrated strong transfer to a wide range of downstream tasks [25]. The theoretical underpinning of contrastive learning was also explored, with connections drawn to mutual information maximization and noise-contrastive estimation.

An important aspect of the contrastive learning revolution was the discovery that the choice and composition of data augmentations profoundly shapes the representations learned. By defining what counts as a "positive pair," augmentation policies implicitly specify the invariances that representations should exhibit. This insight motivated systematic studies of augmentation strategies and their downstream consequences [26], and laid the groundwork for the multi-view learning perspective that will be elaborated in the following section.

### Beyond Contrastive Learning: Non-Contrastive and Generative Methods

As contrastive methods matured, researchers observed several limitations: they required large batches or memory banks to maintain sufficient negative samples, and the choice of negative pairs introduced potential biases. This spurred the development of non-contrastive methods—frameworks that could learn meaningful representations without explicit negative pairs. Methods such as BYOL and SimSiam used asymmetric network architectures with stop-gradient operations and predictor networks to avoid representational collapse, while Barlow Twins and VICReg employed redundancy-reduction objectives that encouraged each dimension of the representation to capture independent information. The architectural components introduced during this period—including the projection head and its role in preventing collapse—are precisely those whose conceptual definitions will be established in the following section.

Simultaneously, inspired by the success of masked language modeling in NLP, researchers revisited generative approaches to self-supervised visual learning. The Masked Autoencoder (MAE) demonstrated that masking a large fraction of image patches and training a vision transformer to reconstruct the missing content yielded scalable and highly transferable visual representations—effectively bringing the BERT-style paradigm to computer vision [24]. This development sparked renewed interest in generative pretraining for vision and suggested that the field might converge toward a unified masked prediction paradigm spanning both language and vision.

### Extending Self-Supervised Learning Beyond Vision and Language

The principles pioneered in computer vision and NLP have been progressively extended to a growing range of data modalities. In speech processing, a series of increasingly powerful self-supervised models—from contrastive predictive coding approaches to wav2vec and HuBERT—demonstrated that speech representations learned without transcription labels could achieve competitive performance on automatic speech recognition and other downstream tasks [27]. In graph-structured data, self-supervised methods adapted augmentation and contrastive strategies to the topology of graphs, enabling representation learning for molecules, social networks, and knowledge graphs [28]. For temporal and multimodal data, SSL frameworks leveraging cross-modal correspondences provided new supervisory signals [29]. This cross-modal extension is particularly significant given the annotation challenges across diverse domains identified in the preceding section, and will be examined in dedicated chapters later in this survey.

### The Modern Era: Scaling, Unification, and Foundation Models

The most recent phase in the evolution of self-supervised learning is characterized by scale and unification. Large-scale pretraining on hundreds of millions to billions of unlabeled examples, combined with increasingly powerful architectures such as vision transformers, has produced models whose learned representations generalize remarkably well across diverse tasks and domains [15]. Surveys of image-oriented SSL document over 100 unique general-purpose frameworks proposed within the span of just a few years, spanning contrastive, generative, clustering-based, and distillation-based paradigms [26].

Moreover, the boundary between self-supervised learning and broader pretraining paradigms has blurred as large foundation models—trained with SSL objectives at massive scale on diverse multimodal data—have emerged as general-purpose representations for downstream adaptation [30]. The historical arc from handcrafted pretext tasks to modern large-scale SSL frameworks thus reflects a broader shift in how the field conceptualizes representation learning: from task-specific feature engineering toward universal pretraining that leverages the intrinsic structure of data across modalities, scales, and domains. With this historical trajectory established, the following section introduces the precise conceptual vocabulary—pretext tasks, pseudo-labels, encoders, projection heads, and the multi-view framework—that will be used to analyze and organize the diverse methodological families surveyed throughout this work.

### 1.3 Core Concepts and Definitions

## 1.3 Core Concepts and Definitions

Having traced the historical evolution of self-supervised learning—from early autoencoders and handcrafted pretext tasks through the contrastive learning revolution to modern large-scale foundation models—it is now necessary to establish a precise conceptual vocabulary that will underpin the remainder of this survey. Self-supervised learning (SSL) is built upon a set of foundational concepts and terminology that distinguish it from other learning paradigms and define its unique approach to representation learning. This section introduces and clarifies these core concepts, grounding them in the historical developments described above and laying the groundwork for the two-stage pretraining pipeline examined in the following section.

### Pretext Tasks and Pseudo-Labels

At the heart of self-supervised learning lies the notion of a **pretext task** — an auxiliary learning objective constructed automatically from unlabeled data, without any human-provided annotation. As introduced in the historical overview, pretext tasks range from early handcrafted objectives such as rotation prediction and jigsaw puzzle solving to more sophisticated masked reconstruction and contrastive instance discrimination tasks. They are carefully designed so that solving them requires the model to extract semantically meaningful features from the input data. As described in [31], pretext tasks can be decomposed into components such as view data augmentation and view label classification, providing a structured way to understand their design and effectiveness.

The supervisory signals for pretext tasks come in the form of **pseudo-labels** — automatically generated labels derived from the intrinsic structure or properties of the data itself [32]. For example, in rotation prediction, the pseudo-label is the degree of rotation applied to an image; in temporal ordering tasks for video, the pseudo-label encodes the correct ordering of frames. Crucially, these pseudo-labels require no human effort to produce. In [33], it is shown that such self-generated labels can be used to augment original supervised labels, creating a joint label distribution that improves both representation quality and downstream task performance.

### Self-Supervised Learning vs. Other Unsupervised Paradigms

It is important to distinguish self-supervised learning from related unsupervised learning approaches, particularly in light of the historical context established above. Traditional **unsupervised learning** methods such as clustering, dimensionality reduction, and density estimation aim to discover latent structure in data without any form of supervision signal. **Self-supervised learning**, by contrast, constructs explicit supervisory signals from the data itself, framing the learning problem as a form of supervised learning applied to automatically generated tasks [34]. This subtle but important distinction means that SSL benefits from the well-developed machinery of supervised deep learning — including cross-entropy loss, gradient-based optimization, and large-scale neural architectures — while requiring no manual annotation. This is precisely why, as noted in the previous section, early self-supervised methods were able to dramatically outperform classical unsupervised techniques once sufficiently expressive architectures and well-designed pretext tasks were combined.

In contrast to **semi-supervised learning**, which typically assumes access to a small labeled set alongside a larger unlabeled set, self-supervised learning operates purely on unlabeled data during pretraining. The labeled data is reserved for downstream fine-tuning or evaluation [35]. While both paradigms aim to exploit unlabeled data, their mechanisms and assumptions differ substantially.

### Data Augmentation and Multi-View Generation

A central mechanism in modern self-supervised learning — and one whose importance was prominently recognized during the contrastive learning revolution described in the previous section — is **data augmentation**, which refers to the application of stochastic transformations to input data to generate multiple modified versions, or **views**, of the same underlying sample. These views serve as the basis for defining positive pairs — pairs of samples that should be mapped to similar representations — and are fundamental to contrastive and non-contrastive SSL frameworks alike.

The choice of augmentation strategy profoundly shapes the invariances encoded in the learned representation [31]. For instance, if color jitter and random cropping are used as augmentations, the resulting representation will be invariant to color variations and local spatial shifts. This principle is generalized in the **multi-view learning** framework [34], where each data point is associated with two or more views, and the SSL objective encourages the model to capture information that is shared — i.e., redundant — across these views, while discarding view-specific noise. This multi-view perspective provides a clean information-theoretic interpretation: the learned representation should capture the mutual information between views that is predictive of downstream labels [36].

Beyond standard hand-crafted augmentations, augmentation strategies can be applied at the feature level as well [37; 38], where internal feature maps are transformed to generate additional supervisory signals. This demonstrates that the principle of self-supervision extends beyond raw input transformations to encompass richer structural relationships within the model itself.

### Leveraging Intrinsic Data Structure as Supervisory Signals

The overarching principle of self-supervised learning is the exploitation of the **intrinsic structure of data** to create free, abundant supervisory signals — the very insight that, as discussed in the historical overview, was first recognized in the context of autoencoders and denoising objectives and has since been refined and scaled across modalities and architectures. Data in the real world is far from random — images contain spatial coherence, videos exhibit temporal continuity, text has grammatical and semantic structure, audio possesses spectral and temporal regularity, and graphs encode relational topology. SSL methods are designed to identify and leverage these structural regularities as sources of self-supervision.

For example, in natural language processing, the masked language modeling objective exploits the statistical co-occurrence structure of words; in video learning, temporal ordering tasks exploit the causal structure of events; in speech, pretext tasks exploit the acoustic and phonetic structure of utterances [39]. Across modalities, the common thread is that the pretext task is defined such that successfully predicting or reconstructing one part of the data from another requires the model to encode rich, generalizable representations [3].

### The Role of Encoders and Representation Spaces

Central to any SSL pipeline is the **encoder** — a neural network (typically a convolutional network or transformer) that maps raw input data into a compact, fixed-dimensional **representation** (also called an embedding or feature vector). The quality of this representation is the ultimate measure of success in SSL: a good representation should capture semantically meaningful information that generalizes across diverse downstream tasks.

In many modern SSL frameworks, the encoder is coupled with a **projection head** — a lightweight network (often a multi-layer perceptron) that maps representations into a lower-dimensional space where the SSL loss is computed. As demonstrated empirically and theoretically in various works, the projection head plays a crucial role in preventing the collapse of the encoder representation and in preserving useful structural information in the encoder output [35]. This distinction between the encoder and the projection head is of particular practical importance: as will be detailed in the following section on the standard SSL pipeline, the projection head is typically discarded after pretraining, and only the encoder output is used for downstream task adaptation.

### Summary of Key Terminology

To summarize, the core conceptual vocabulary of self-supervised learning includes:
- **Pretext task**: An automatically constructed learning objective derived from unlabeled data.
- **Pseudo-label**: An automatically generated supervisory signal derived from data structure or applied transformations.
- **Data augmentation**: Stochastic transformations applied to inputs to generate multiple views.
- **Multi-view learning**: A framework where multiple augmented versions of data are used to define SSL objectives.
- **Positive pair**: A pair of views derived from the same data point, expected to have similar representations.
- **Encoder**: The neural network backbone that maps inputs to representations.
- **Projection head**: An auxiliary network used during SSL training to map representations to the loss computation space.
- **Downstream task**: The target application task for which the SSL-pretrained encoder is fine-tuned or evaluated.

Together, these concepts define the operating principles of self-supervised representation learning. Having now established both the historical context and the core conceptual vocabulary, the following section turns to the standard two-stage SSL pipeline — pretext task pretraining followed by downstream transfer — examining in detail how each of these components interacts to produce general-purpose representations from unlabeled data.

### 1.4 The General Pipeline: Pretext Tasks, Representation Learning, and Downstream Fine-Tuning

## 1.4 The Standard Two-Stage SSL Pipeline

Having established the core conceptual vocabulary of self-supervised learning—including pretext tasks, pseudo-labels, data augmentation, encoders, and projection heads—we are now in a position to examine how these components are assembled into a coherent learning framework. The standard self-supervised learning (SSL) pipeline follows a two-stage paradigm that has become the dominant framework for leveraging unlabeled data to learn transferable representations, and it is precisely within this pipeline that the concepts defined in the previous section take on their practical meaning.

In the first stage, a deep neural network encoder is trained on one or more pretext tasks—self-defined objectives that do not require human-annotated labels but instead exploit the intrinsic structure of the data to generate supervisory signals. These pretext tasks may involve predicting image rotations, reconstructing masked regions, solving jigsaw puzzles, or contrasting different augmented views of the same input. The encoder learns to extract rich, semantically meaningful feature representations by solving these auxiliary tasks on large unlabeled datasets. In the second stage, the pretrained encoder is transferred to downstream tasks of interest, where task-specific labeled data is typically scarce. This two-stage structure—pretext task pretraining followed by downstream transfer—provides the organizational backbone for much of the methodological taxonomy introduced in the following section.

A critical architectural component introduced in many modern SSL frameworks is the projection head—a shallow multilayer perceptron (MLP) appended on top of the encoder during pretraining. As introduced in the core concepts section, the projection head maps encoder representations into a lower-dimensional space where the SSL loss is computed. Empirical and theoretical investigations have revealed a somewhat counterintuitive finding: the representations immediately before the projection head (i.e., the encoder output) generalize better to downstream tasks than the projected representations themselves, even though the loss is computed on the projected embeddings [40; 41; 42]. This phenomenon has been explained through the lens of data augmentation strength and dimensional collapse: the projection head absorbs augmentation-induced invariances, allowing the encoder to retain richer, more general features [41]. Consequently, the standard practice discards the projection head after pretraining and uses only the encoder for downstream evaluation—a design principle whose importance is reflected in the diverse SSL paradigms surveyed in the following section.

Once pretraining is complete, several transfer strategies can be employed to adapt the learned representations to downstream tasks. **Full fine-tuning** updates all network parameters using labeled downstream data, offering maximum adaptation flexibility at the cost of potentially overwriting useful pretrained features. **Linear probing** (or linear evaluation) freezes the encoder and trains only a linear classifier on top of the fixed representations, providing a direct measure of the quality of the learned features without any representation modification [43; 44]. **Few-shot evaluation** assesses the encoder's ability to generalize to new tasks with very limited labeled examples, highlighting the practical utility of SSL for data-scarce scenarios [45; 46; 47]. Parameter-efficient transfer strategies, such as adapter modules, have also been explored to adapt pretrained models to multiple downstream tasks simultaneously without retraining the full model [48].

The quality of the learned representation is the central determinant of downstream task performance across all transfer strategies. A representation that captures high-level semantic structure, exhibits good alignment between semantically similar samples, and retains diverse informative features will transfer effectively to a wide range of tasks and domains [49; 50]. Several studies have emphasized that the design choices made during pretraining—including the backbone architecture, projection head dimensionality, data augmentation policies, and pretext task formulation—have profound effects on transferability [51; 52]. For instance, simply modifying the size of the backbone's final block has been shown to be a remarkably effective strategy for mitigating pretraining bias and improving downstream transfer [51]. Similarly, adding an MLP projector in supervised pretraining helps preserve intra-class variation and reduces feature distribution mismatch between pretraining and evaluation datasets [52].

Beyond direct fine-tuning, the SSL pipeline has been extended in several important directions. Knowledge distillation frameworks transfer the representational knowledge of large SSL-pretrained teacher models to smaller student architectures, enabling deployment on resource-constrained devices while preserving much of the representational richness [53; 54]. In addition, bi-directional fine-tuning approaches combine discriminative label information with the intrinsic structure of the downstream data, integrating both a classifier head and a projector head during fine-tuning to exploit complementary sources of knowledge [55]. Theoretical analyses of fine-tuning dynamics have further revealed how task head initialization and energy at the beginning of fine-tuning control the degree of feature adaptation, providing principled guidance for designing effective transfer protocols [56; 57].

In summary, the two-stage SSL pipeline—pretext task pretraining followed by downstream fine-tuning or evaluation—provides a powerful and flexible framework for learning general-purpose representations from unlabeled data. The interplay between encoder architecture, projection head design, pretext task formulation, and transfer strategy collectively determines the quality and generalizability of the learned representations, making each component a critical design choice in SSL system development. It is precisely the nature of the pretext task—whether generative, contrastive, predictive, or clustering-based—that serves as the primary axis along which SSL methods diverge, and it is to this taxonomic organization that the following section now turns.

### 1.5 Taxonomy of Self-Supervised Learning Paradigms

A systematic taxonomy of self-supervised learning (SSL) paradigms helps organize the vast and rapidly growing landscape of methods into coherent categories based on the nature of their learning objectives and supervisory signals. Building on the two-stage SSL pipeline introduced in the previous section—where a model is pretrained on a pretext task and subsequently transferred to downstream tasks—it becomes clear that the nature of the pretext task itself is the primary axis along which SSL methods diverge. At the highest level, SSL methods can be grouped into four major paradigms: **generative approaches**, **contrastive approaches**, **predictive approaches**, and **clustering-based approaches**. Each paradigm embodies distinct assumptions about how meaningful representations should be constructed from unlabeled data, and each exhibits characteristic strengths and weaknesses that make it more or less suitable for different data modalities and downstream tasks—the very modalities surveyed in depth in the following section.

---

### Generative Approaches

Generative SSL methods learn representations by training a model to reconstruct the original input data from a corrupted, masked, or compressed version. The underlying intuition is that a model capable of accurately reconstructing high-fidelity data must have learned a rich internal representation of its structure. Autoencoders, variational autoencoders (VAEs), and masked autoencoders (MAE) are canonical examples. In the natural language processing domain, masked language modeling—predicting masked tokens from context—is a quintessential generative pretext task. In computer vision, similar ideas manifest as image inpainting, colorization, and masked patch reconstruction.

Generative approaches are appealing because they can be applied to virtually any data modality with minimal modification, and they naturally capture fine-grained structural details of the input. However, a well-recognized limitation is that reconstruction objectives may encourage the model to allocate representational capacity to low-level pixel or token statistics that are irrelevant to high-level semantic downstream tasks. Furthermore, generative methods often require substantial model capacity and compute for the decoder, much of which is discarded at inference time. Hybrid frameworks that combine generative and contrastive signals, such as those studied in [58], have demonstrated that combining the two paradigms often outperforms either alone. Similarly, the comparative study of contrastive versus generative SSL for time series data [59] reveals that neither paradigm uniformly dominates, and the best choice depends heavily on dataset characteristics and the specific downstream task.

---

### Contrastive Approaches

Contrastive SSL methods learn representations by encouraging the model to produce similar embeddings for semantically related (positive) pairs of samples while pushing apart embeddings of unrelated (negative) pairs. The contrastive objective, typically instantiated as the InfoNCE or NT-Xent loss, provides a powerful self-supervisory signal by leveraging the structure of data augmentation to define positive pairs without human labels. Seminal frameworks such as SimCLR, MoCo, and PIRL established contrastive learning as the dominant SSL paradigm in computer vision, and the approach has since been extended to graphs [28], time series [60], speech, and multimodal settings.

The strength of contrastive methods lies in their ability to learn semantically discriminative representations that transfer well to downstream classification tasks. Theoretical analyses have shown that when the positive pairs provide redundant information about downstream labels, linear functions of contrastive representations achieve near-optimal downstream classification accuracy [36]. However, contrastive methods require careful construction of negative pairs: too few negatives lead to degenerate representations, while randomly sampled negatives may include false negatives—pairs that share the same semantic class but are treated as dissimilar. Debiased contrastive objectives have been proposed to correct for this sampling bias [61]. Additionally, contrastive methods are sensitive to the choice of data augmentation, which implicitly defines what invariances are encoded in the learned representation—a consideration that connects directly to the augmentation policies discussed as critical design choices in the SSL pipeline.

A notable extension of contrastive learning to the graph domain is surveyed in [28], where graph-specific augmentations such as node dropping, edge perturbation, and subgraph sampling define positive pairs. The survey of contrastive SSL for recommender systems [62] provides another illustration of how the contrastive paradigm generalizes across domains, with the view generation strategy and contrastive task formulation serving as the principal axes of differentiation.

---

### Predictive Approaches

Predictive SSL methods learn representations by training a model to forecast some part of the input from another part, where the partition is defined by the structure of the data itself rather than random augmentation. This includes predicting future frames from past frames in video, predicting the relative position or transformation relationship between image patches, and forecasting masked tokens or signals in temporal sequences. Predictive methods differ from purely generative approaches in that they often operate in the latent space rather than pixel space, predicting representations rather than raw inputs, which avoids the pitfall of spending model capacity on perceptually irrelevant reconstruction details.

In the time series domain, predictive SSL is particularly natural: methods predict future time steps from past context, or forecast masked temporal segments, leveraging the inherent sequential structure of the data [63]. Non-contrastive predictive methods such as BYOL and data2vec, which predict the representations of one view from another without using negative pairs, also fall within this category and have demonstrated competitive performance across vision, language, and audio [64]. The success of such approaches further illustrates how pretext task design—here, latent-space prediction—profoundly shapes the nature of the representations produced, echoing the architectural and design considerations outlined in the SSL pipeline discussion.

---

### Clustering-Based Approaches

Clustering-based SSL methods assign pseudo-labels to unlabeled samples by grouping them into clusters or matching them to learned prototypes, and then use these pseudo-labels as self-supervisory signals. The core idea is that samples assigned to the same cluster should have similar representations, and the cluster assignment process and representation learning are performed jointly in an end-to-end fashion. SwAV and DINO are prominent examples, where online clustering or prototype matching provides the supervisory signal. A Bayesian unification of clustering-based and energy-based SSL objectives has been proposed to provide a principled probabilistic grounding for this paradigm [65], demonstrating that clustering objectives can be derived from first principles as approximate inference in a latent variable model.

Clustering-based methods offer an elegant way to capture semantic groupings in the data without requiring explicit negative pairs, and they scale well to large datasets through online assignment mechanisms. Their primary challenge is preventing cluster collapse, in which all samples are assigned to the same cluster, producing degenerate representations. Strategies to prevent collapse include balancing constraints, momentum encoders, and multi-crop augmentations. The integration of clustering-based SSL with semi-supervised learning has also proven highly effective [66], where cluster centroids serve as class prototypes when a small amount of labeled data is available.

---

### Comparative Summary and Taxonomy

Table 1 (conceptual) summarizes the four paradigms along key dimensions: supervisory signal type, treatment of negative samples, reconstruction versus discrimination objective, computational requirements, and suitability for different modalities. As comprehensively reviewed in [35] and [67], no single paradigm universally dominates across all settings. Generative methods excel at capturing structural details and are broadly applicable but may encode task-irrelevant information. Contrastive methods learn highly discriminative semantic features but require careful negative pair construction and are sensitive to augmentation choices. Predictive methods avoid the reconstruction-versus-semantics trade-off by operating in latent space and are particularly powerful for sequential data. Clustering-based methods naturally capture group-level semantics but require collapse prevention mechanisms.

Importantly, these paradigms are not mutually exclusive. A growing body of work explores hybrid frameworks that combine objectives from multiple paradigms [58; 68], with results consistently demonstrating that complementary objectives capture different aspects of the data and together yield more transferable representations than any single paradigm alone. This taxonomic understanding not only provides the conceptual scaffolding for the detailed methodological treatment in subsequent sections of this survey, but also anticipates the modality-specific discussions that follow—where the choice among generative, contrastive, predictive, and clustering-based objectives proves to be a central design consideration for each data domain.

### 1.6 Self-Supervised Learning Across Data Modalities

Self-supervised learning has demonstrated remarkable versatility across an expansive range of data modalities, extending well beyond its early successes in computer vision and natural language processing. While the core principle of deriving supervisory signals from the intrinsic structure of unlabeled data remains constant, the four paradigms surveyed in the previous section—generative, contrastive, predictive, and clustering-based—manifest in distinct and modality-specific ways across different data domains. Each modality presents unique structural properties that necessitate carefully tailored pretext task designs and augmentation strategies, and the choice among paradigms is itself shaped by these structural characteristics.

**Natural Images.** The visual domain has served as a primary testbed for self-supervised learning, with methods exploiting spatial structure through colorization, rotation prediction, patch ordering, contrastive instance discrimination, and masked image modeling. The rich spatial and semantic structure of natural images affords diverse augmentation strategies—crops, color jitter, blurring—that define meaningful positive and negative pairs for contrastive frameworks, while also supporting generative reconstruction objectives such as masked patch prediction. The breadth of SSL paradigms applied to natural images reflects the domain's structural richness and its role as a proving ground for new methodological ideas.

**Video and Temporal Data.** Video extends the image domain with a temporal dimension, providing additional supervisory signals such as temporal ordering, motion consistency, and audio-visual correspondence. Self-supervised methods for video exploit these signals through pretext tasks like temporal ordering prediction, predictive coding, and cross-modal agreement between visual frames and accompanying audio [3]. The temporal structure introduces unique challenges, including handling motion dynamics and long-range dependencies, but also opens opportunities for video-exclusive learning strategies that advance multimodal self-supervised learning [3]. Predictive paradigms are especially natural here, as the sequential structure of video directly supports forecasting-based objectives.

**Speech and Audio.** Speech and audio signals are inherently sequential with rich temporal and spectral structure. Self-supervised representation learning in this domain encompasses generative, contrastive, and predictive approaches, with pretext tasks exploiting properties such as local temporal context, acoustic attributes, and cross-modal correspondences with visual streams [27]. Audio-visual self-supervision, for instance, leverages the natural synchrony between sound and lip movements to enrich audio representations with visual information [69]. Such approaches have demonstrated strong performance on automatic speech recognition, speaker identification, and audio classification tasks.

**Natural Language Text.** In the text domain, self-supervised learning is most prominently instantiated through masked language modeling and next-token prediction objectives, exemplified by BERT and GPT-style models. These approaches leverage the sequential and contextual structure of language, treating masked word prediction or sentence-level relationship modeling as pretext tasks—objectives that sit at the intersection of the generative and predictive paradigms introduced earlier. Multi-view sentence representation learning further exploits distributional and architectural asymmetry to learn robust textual embeddings through contrastive objectives.

**Graphs.** Graph-structured data presents unique challenges for self-supervised learning due to its non-Euclidean topology. Pretext tasks in this domain include node and edge augmentation strategies, subgraph-level contrast, and canonical correlation-inspired objectives. Methods must carefully design augmentations that preserve meaningful structural and semantic properties of graphs while generating diverse views that promote invariant representation learning. As discussed in the contrastive paradigm section, graph-specific augmentations such as node dropping, edge perturbation, and subgraph sampling serve as the primary mechanism for defining positive pairs in this domain.

**Time Series.** Time series data from sensors, physiological monitors, and other sources exhibits temporal autocorrelation and domain-specific frequency characteristics. Self-supervised methods tailored for time series exploit temporal context prediction, frequency-temporal augmentations, and transformation consistency objectives. For example, self-supervised pretraining on electroencephalography signals using temporal context prediction has shown strong performance in low-data regimes on clinically relevant tasks such as sleep scoring [63]. Human activity recognition from wearable sensors similarly benefits from self-supervised pretraining that captures temporal dynamics without requiring expensive annotations. As noted in the predictive paradigm discussion, the inherent sequential structure of time series makes predictive pretext tasks particularly well-suited to this modality.

**Tabular Data.** Tabular data, ubiquitous in healthcare, finance, and scientific research, lacks the spatial or temporal structure that facilitates natural augmentation design. Self-supervised learning for tabular data has addressed this challenge by constructing multi-view learning problems through feature subsetting, as in the SubTab framework, which divides input features into multiple subsets and learns representations by reconstructing data from these partial views [70]. Surveys on SSL for non-sequential tabular data categorize approaches into predictive, contrastive, and hybrid learning paradigms, highlighting the difficulty of defining meaningful augmentations in the absence of explicit relational structure [71]. The hybrid paradigm, combining generative reconstruction with contrastive objectives, proves particularly valuable here precisely because the absence of strong inductive biases makes any single paradigm insufficient on its own.

**Multimodal Combinations.** Perhaps the most promising frontier for self-supervised learning lies in exploiting the natural co-occurrence and complementarity of multiple data modalities. Cross-modal self-supervised learning leverages correspondences between modalities—such as audio-visual synchrony in video, or image-text co-occurrence in web data—to provide rich and diverse supervisory signals that transcend what any single modality can offer. Frameworks such as self-supervised multimodal versatile networks learn joint representations across visual, audio, and language streams by leveraging unlabeled video data [72]. Large-scale multi-modal pretraining datasets such as M5Product, spanning image, text, table, video, and audio modalities, push the boundaries of what cross-modal self-supervised learning can achieve at scale [73]. Cross-modal clustering approaches, such as XDC, leverage unsupervised clustering in one modality as a supervisory signal for another, demonstrating that cross-modal supervision can yield superior representations compared to single-modality approaches [74]. Joint video-language models like VideoBERT learn bidirectional joint distributions over visual and linguistic tokens, enabling high-level semantic understanding without explicit supervision [75].

A central insight emerging from this cross-modal panorama is that while the fundamental principles of self-supervised learning—exploiting intrinsic data structure, constructing proxy supervision, and learning transferable representations—generalize broadly, successful deployment in each domain requires careful attention to modality-specific inductive biases. Effective pretext task design must respect the structural properties of the target modality: spatial coherence in images, temporal dynamics in video and audio, sequential context in text, relational topology in graphs, and feature correlations in tabular data. Crucially, the choice among generative, contrastive, predictive, and clustering-based paradigms is not merely a technical preference but is guided by these structural characteristics—a consideration that will resurface throughout the detailed methodological discussions that follow. Comprehensive reviews confirm that this combination of universal principles and modality-specific adaptation characterizes the state of self-supervised learning across diverse data domains [29; 14]. The development of domain-agnostic benchmarks such as DABS, which evaluates SSL algorithms across natural images, sensor data, English text, speech, multilingual text, chest X-rays, and image-text pairs, further underscores both the generality and the remaining challenges of building universal self-supervised learning systems [76]. These cross-domain insights set the stage for the subsequent discussion of how SSL relates to and interacts with other major learning paradigms in the broader machine learning ecosystem.

### 1.7 Relationship to Related Learning Paradigms

Self-supervised learning (SSL) does not exist in isolation—it intersects deeply with a rich ecosystem of related learning paradigms, and understanding these relationships clarifies both the unique contributions of SSL and the synergies it enables when combined with complementary approaches. Having surveyed the diverse data modalities to which SSL has been successfully applied, we now turn to examining how SSL relates to and interacts with other major learning paradigms, a perspective that is essential for situating SSL within the broader machine learning landscape and for appreciating the scope of the survey that follows.

**SSL and Unsupervised Learning.** SSL is often regarded as a special case of unsupervised learning, as both paradigms operate without human-provided labels. However, SSL is distinguished by its deliberate construction of supervisory signals from the intrinsic structure of the data itself—through pretext tasks, contrastive objectives, or masked prediction—rather than relying purely on density estimation or clustering. This design philosophy allows SSL to learn semantically rich, transferable representations that classical unsupervised methods such as PCA or k-means typically cannot match in downstream task performance [77].

**SSL and Semi-Supervised Learning.** Semi-supervised learning (SSL in the traditional sense) seeks to leverage both labeled and unlabeled data simultaneously, whereas self-supervised pretraining typically treats the unlabeled data independently before any labeled data is introduced. Nevertheless, the two paradigms are highly complementary. Self-supervised pretraining can serve as a powerful initialization for semi-supervised pipelines, providing strong feature representations that enable more effective pseudo-label generation and label propagation [78; 79]. Theoretical analyses further show that self-training—a classical semi-supervised technique—benefits significantly from representations initialized via self-supervised pretraining, as the resulting feature spaces reduce the risk of error propagation through pseudo-labels [80]. Unified frameworks like Self-Tuning explicitly combine transfer of self-supervised pretrained weights with semi-supervised exploration of unlabeled data, outperforming either approach in isolation [81].

**SSL and Transfer Learning.** Transfer learning and self-supervised pretraining are perhaps the most intertwined paradigms. In the standard SSL pipeline, a model is pretrained on a pretext task using unlabeled data and then fine-tuned on a labeled downstream task—a workflow that is structurally identical to transfer learning [49]. Comparative studies have systematically examined when SSL-based pretraining outperforms supervised transfer learning, finding that SSL is particularly advantageous when the source and target domains differ substantially, when labeled source data is imbalanced, or when domain-specific unlabeled data is abundant [82]. Crucially, SSL pretraining decouples representation learning from task-specific supervision, enabling more flexible and general-purpose feature transfer [77]. Empirical benchmarks across diverse domains—including medical imaging, satellite imagery, and skin lesion analysis—confirm that self-supervised pretraining consistently improves over purely supervised transfer, especially in data-scarce regimes [83; 84].

**SSL and Multi-Task Learning.** Multi-task learning (MTL) improves generalization by jointly optimizing multiple related objectives. SSL naturally fits this paradigm: self-supervised pretext tasks can serve as auxiliary objectives alongside supervised targets, providing additional learning signal without requiring extra annotations [33; 85]. Studies have shown that jointly training a primary dense prediction task with a self-supervised auxiliary task yields consistent improvements in performance and robustness, even outperforming jointly training with labeled auxiliary tasks [86]. Graph-based neural network models further benefit from multiple self-supervised auxiliary tasks in a multi-task fashion, improving representation quality on semi-supervised classification benchmarks [87]. The joint optimization of SSL and supervised tasks—sometimes referred to as composite or multi-task learning—represents a particularly practical paradigm, as it requires no additional annotation while enriching the supervisory signal available to the model.

**SSL and Meta-Learning.** Meta-learning aims to equip models with the ability to learn new tasks rapidly from limited examples, and SSL provides a natural source of diverse tasks and rich representations to support this goal [88]. SSL pretraining yields broadly transferable features that serve as strong initializations for meta-learning algorithms, reducing the reliance on large supervised meta-training sets [89]. Conversely, meta-learning techniques can be applied to improve SSL itself—for example, by constructing diverse distributions of self-supervised tasks for meta-training in NLP [90], or by employing bi-level optimization to make a self-supervised model its own teacher through bootstrapped meta-gradients [91]. Hybrid frameworks such as Meta-Contrastive Networks combine contrastive self-supervised learning with model-agnostic meta-learning to address the hard-positive problem in video representation learning, achieving state-of-the-art results on action recognition benchmarks [92]. The interaction between meta-learning and SSL is thus bidirectional: each paradigm strengthens the other, with SSL providing scalable task generation and meta-learning providing principled few-shot adaptation [93].

**SSL and Continual Learning.** Continual learning addresses the challenge of learning from non-stationary data streams without catastrophically forgetting previously acquired knowledge. Self-supervised representations have been shown to be more transferable and less susceptible to forgetting than their supervised counterparts, largely due to the role of the multi-layer perceptron projector in learning broadly generalizable features [94; 95]. Frameworks for continual SSL convert self-supervised loss functions into distillation mechanisms by adding predictor networks that anchor current representations to their past states, enabling effective continual visual representation learning across diverse architectures [94]. Task Agnostic Representation Consolidation (TARC) interleaves self-supervised and supervised training stages, using a task-agnostic auxiliary loss during supervised updates to preserve generalizable features learned via SSL [96]. In medical imaging, continual SSL frameworks like MedCoSS address multi-modal conflicts and catastrophic forgetting by staging pretraining across modalities with rehearsal-based strategies and feature distillation [97]. Semi-supervised continual learning frameworks further combine hypernetworks with SSL objectives to handle sequentially arriving partially labeled data [98]. Together, these works demonstrate that SSL is not only compatible with but actively beneficial for continual learning scenarios.

**SSL and Foundation Models.** At the broadest scale, self-supervised pretraining is the cornerstone of the foundation model paradigm, wherein large-scale models are pretrained on massive unlabeled corpora and subsequently adapted to a wide variety of downstream tasks. Contrastive and generative SSL objectives power the pretraining of foundation models across vision, language, and multimodal domains [99]. The success of these models rests on the ability of SSL to extract universally useful representations from unlabeled data at scale—representations that transfer effectively across diverse tasks and domains [77]. As the scale of pretraining grows, SSL increasingly serves as the unifying methodological foundation across learning paradigms, enabling models that are simultaneously capable of transfer learning, few-shot adaptation, continual updating, and multi-task generalization.

In summary, self-supervised learning is not a standalone paradigm but a foundational methodology that enriches and is enriched by unsupervised, semi-supervised, transfer, multi-task, meta-, and continual learning. Understanding these relationships is essential for appreciating both the current capabilities and the future potential of SSL as a universal approach to representation learning. This perspective also informs the organizational principles of the survey that follows, which is designed to reflect both the methodological diversity of SSL and its broad integration with the wider landscape of modern machine learning.

## 2 Core Methodological Frameworks and Objectives

### 2.1 Contrastive Learning Methods

## 2.1 Contrastive Learning Methods

Contrastive learning has emerged as one of the most influential paradigms in self-supervised representation learning, operating on the principle that representations should draw together semantically related (positive) pairs while pushing apart unrelated (negative) pairs in an embedding space. This section surveys the foundational frameworks, core objectives, and architectural innovations that define modern contrastive self-supervised learning, establishing the landscape against which the non-contrastive and redundancy-reduction methods discussed in Section 2.2 can be understood.

**Core Loss Functions: NT-Xent and InfoNCE**

At the heart of most contrastive learning methods lie two closely related objectives: the Noise-Contrastive Estimation (InfoNCE) loss and the Normalized Temperature-scaled Cross-Entropy (NT-Xent) loss. The InfoNCE objective frames the learning problem as identifying the correct positive sample among a set of negatives, effectively maximizing a lower bound on the mutual information between different views of the same instance. Given an anchor representation and its positive counterpart, the model is trained to assign higher similarity to the positive pair relative to all negative pairs in the batch or memory bank. NT-Xent, a variant popularized by SimCLR, applies L2 normalization to embeddings and uses a temperature hyperparameter to control the sharpness of the distribution over similarities, making training more stable and the learned representations more uniformly distributed on the hypersphere [1; 15].

**SimCLR: Simple Contrastive Learning of Representations**

SimCLR established a clean and highly effective framework for contrastive self-supervised learning. The approach generates two augmented views of each image in a minibatch and uses the NT-Xent loss to maximize agreement between representations of these views while treating all other samples in the batch as negatives. A key contribution of SimCLR was the systematic investigation of the role of data augmentation composition, demonstrating that the combination of random cropping, color jittering, and Gaussian blur defines particularly effective positive pairs. Equally important was the introduction of a non-linear projection head — a small multi-layer perceptron applied on top of the base encoder before computing the contrastive loss — which was empirically shown to substantially improve the quality of the learned representations used for downstream tasks [1; 14; 35]. SimCLR also highlighted the critical role of large batch sizes, as a large number of in-batch negatives is necessary for the contrastive objective to learn discriminative features.

**MoCo: Momentum Contrast**

A central limitation of SimCLR and related in-batch contrastive methods is their dependence on large batch sizes to provide a sufficient number of negatives. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) addressed this constraint by maintaining a dynamic dictionary — implemented as a queue — that stores representations from recent minibatches, enabling the use of a much larger and more diverse set of negatives without requiring prohibitively large batches [1; 14]. To ensure the consistency and stability of the stored representations in the queue, MoCo introduced a momentum encoder: a slowly-updated copy of the main encoder whose parameters are updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online encoder's parameters, rather than by direct gradient backpropagation. This design decouples the encoder used for the query from the encoder used to produce keys, allowing the key representations to evolve gradually and remain consistent throughout the queue. Subsequent versions of MoCo incorporated design improvements from SimCLR, including the projection head and stronger augmentation policies, demonstrating that the two frameworks are highly complementary [14].

**PIRL: Pretext-Invariant Representation Learning**

PIRL (Pretext-Invariant Representation Learning) extended the contrastive learning paradigm to incorporate pretext task-based transformations more directly. Rather than contrasting different augmented crops of the same image, PIRL trains the encoder to produce representations that are invariant to specific, predefined image transformations — such as jigsaw patch permutations — by contrasting the transformed image representation against that of the original image stored in a memory bank. This approach connects the contrastive framework with earlier pretext task-based self-supervised methods and demonstrates that contrastive objectives can be applied to a broader class of invariances beyond simple augmentation crops [1; 100]. PIRL's memory bank design, which stores representations of all training images computed across epochs, provides a large and stable pool of negatives, though at the cost of representation staleness as the encoder evolves during training.

**The Role of Negative Pairs in Preventing Representational Collapse**

A fundamental challenge in self-supervised learning is representational collapse — the degenerate solution where the encoder maps all inputs to the same constant representation, trivially satisfying any similarity-maximizing objective. In contrastive methods, negative pairs are the primary mechanism for preventing this collapse: by requiring the encoder to assign dissimilar representations to different images, the contrastive loss exerts an explicit repulsive force that counteracts the attractive force between positive pairs [14; 35]. The number, diversity, and quality of negatives therefore directly influence both the stability of training and the quality of the learned representation. Methods differ in how they source negatives — in-batch sampling (SimCLR), memory queues (MoCo, PIRL), or explicit negative sampling strategies — with each approach presenting distinct trade-offs between computational cost, negative diversity, and representation consistency. As discussed in Section 2.2, the dependence on negatives for collapse prevention constitutes one of the primary motivations for the development of non-contrastive approaches.

**ReSSL and Soft Contrastive Learning**

Building on the success of standard contrastive objectives, more recent methods have explored refinements to the treatment of negative pairs. ReSSL (Relational Self-Supervised Learning) introduced a relational perspective, arguing that the relative structure among samples — rather than strict instance-level discrimination — provides a richer supervisory signal. Instead of treating all negatives equally, ReSSL uses a weak augmentation to compute soft relational targets that capture the pairwise similarity structure, and trains the model to reproduce this structure under strong augmentations [14]. This adaptive soft contrastive approach mitigates the noise introduced by false negatives — samples that are semantically similar to the anchor but treated as negatives — and encourages the encoder to respect the broader geometric structure of the data manifold.

**Key Improvements: Decoupled Contrastive Loss, Additive Margins, and Beyond**

Several targeted modifications to the NT-Xent and InfoNCE objectives have been proposed to address known limitations. The decoupled contrastive loss removes the positive term from the denominator of the InfoNCE objective, arguing that coupling the positive pair's similarity into the normalization introduces a gradient conflict that harms learning efficiency. By decoupling these terms, the gradient dynamics become more favorable, improving representation quality particularly in settings with small batch sizes or limited negatives. Additive margin contrastive losses, inspired by metric learning literature, introduce a fixed or adaptive margin into the similarity computation to ensure a minimum separation between positive and negative pairs, leading to more geometrically structured embedding spaces [100]. Adaptive soft contrastive methods further replace the hard binary positive/negative assignment with soft labels derived from the similarity structure of the data, allowing the model to gracefully handle the ambiguity of near-duplicate and semantically related samples.

**Projection Heads and Architectural Considerations**

The projection head — typically a two or three-layer MLP appended to the backbone encoder — has proven to be a surprisingly important architectural component in contrastive frameworks. Empirically, representations extracted before the projection head generalize substantially better to downstream tasks than those at the projection head's output [1; 14]. Theoretical analyses have attributed this to the projection head absorbing augmentation-specific invariances that are useful for the pretext task but harmful for semantic downstream tasks, effectively shielding the encoder's representation from excessive invariance. Notably, this architectural insight — that the component used for loss computation need not be the component used for transfer — has become a universal design principle that extends beyond contrastive methods into the non-contrastive and redundancy-reduction frameworks discussed in Section 2.2.

**Summary**

Contrastive self-supervised learning methods have established a powerful and versatile paradigm for learning visual and multimodal representations from unlabeled data [1; 14; 15]. From the foundational frameworks of SimCLR and MoCo, through the pretext-invariant formulations of PIRL, to the relational and soft contrastive perspectives of ReSSL and related methods, the field has systematically addressed the core challenges of negative sampling efficiency, collapse prevention, and representation transferability. The architectural innovations of memory banks, momentum encoders, and projection heads introduced by these methods remain central design patterns in the broader landscape of self-supervised representation learning. Nevertheless, the persistent requirement for large numbers of negative pairs motivates an alternative family of approaches — non-contrastive and redundancy-reduction methods — which dispense with explicit negatives altogether, as examined in the following section.

### 2.2 Non-Contrastive and Redundancy-Reduction Methods

## 2.2 Non-Contrastive and Redundancy-Reduction Methods

As established in Section 2.1, contrastive self-supervised learning methods depend fundamentally on large numbers of negative pairs to prevent representational collapse, a requirement that often necessitates large batch sizes, memory banks, or momentum queues. A distinct family of methods addresses this limitation by eliminating explicit negative pairs entirely, instead relying on architectural asymmetries, regularization objectives, and redundancy-reduction principles to learn meaningful representations. These methods occupy a conceptual middle ground between the negative-pair-driven contrastive approaches of Section 2.1 and the reconstruction-based generative approaches of Section 2.3, offering a complementary perspective on how collapse can be prevented without either explicit repulsion or pixel-level reconstruction.

**Bootstrap Your Own Latent (BYOL)** introduced one of the most influential non-contrastive paradigms by employing a siamese network with an asymmetric design: an online network equipped with a trainable predictor head and a target network updated via an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online network's weights. The online branch is trained to predict the target branch's representations of differently augmented views of the same image, with a stop-gradient operation applied to the target network to prevent gradient flow through it. This asymmetric setup, combined with the momentum-based target network, was shown empirically to avoid representational collapse without any negative pairs, though the precise theoretical mechanism remained initially debated [15]. Notably, the EMA-based target network bears a structural resemblance to the momentum encoder introduced by MoCo, yet serves an entirely different purpose: rather than maintaining a consistent pool of negatives, it provides a slowly evolving regression target that stabilizes learning without requiring any contrastive signal.

**SimSiam** further simplified this paradigm by removing the momentum encoder entirely, relying solely on a predictor network and stop-gradient operations applied to one branch of a symmetric siamese network. The stop-gradient mechanism was identified as the critical component preventing collapse: without it, the network converges to degenerate constant representations. SimSiam demonstrated that neither large batches, nor momentum encoders, nor negative pairs are strictly necessary for learning useful representations, pointing to the stop-gradient as a form of implicit regularization [15; 26]. This result sharpened understanding of which components in contrastive and non-contrastive frameworks are truly essential versus incidental.

**Barlow Twins** pursued a conceptually distinct approach inspired by the neuroscientific principle of redundancy reduction. Rather than operating on the similarity between sample pairs, Barlow Twins directly acts on the cross-correlation matrix computed between the feature vectors produced by two augmented views of the same image. The objective encourages this cross-correlation matrix to be close to the identity matrix, simultaneously promoting invariance to augmentations (on-diagonal terms close to 1) and reducing redundancy between different feature dimensions (off-diagonal terms close to 0). This feature-decorrelation mechanism provides a natural defense against dimensional collapse by penalizing correlated output dimensions, offering a fundamentally different collapse-prevention strategy compared to the repulsive force of negative pairs in contrastive methods [26; 15].

**VICReg (Variance-Invariance-Covariance Regularization)** extended this family with an explicit multi-term objective composed of three complementary regularization terms: a variance term that maintains the standard deviation of embeddings above a threshold to prevent dimensional collapse, an invariance term that minimizes the mean squared distance between the embeddings of two views to encourage alignment, and a covariance term that penalizes off-diagonal elements of the feature covariance matrix to decorrelate dimensions. VICReg's explicit decomposition of the collapse-prevention and alignment objectives offers greater transparency and ease of hyperparameter tuning compared to Barlow Twins, while sharing the same underlying intuition that the feature space should be simultaneously well-spread and non-redundant [26].

**TiCo (Transformation Invariance and Covariance Contrast)** further contributed to this line of work by combining an alignment objective with a covariance contrast term that explicitly pushes the feature covariance matrix of one view away from that of another, providing an alternative mechanism for preventing collapse through covariance-level contrastive regularization rather than pair-level negatives.

A unified theoretical perspective has emerged to explain why these diverse non-contrastive methods avoid collapse. From an information-theoretic standpoint, both predictor-based methods like BYOL and SimSiam and redundancy-reduction methods like Barlow Twins and VICReg can be understood as maximizing the information content of representations while preventing dimensionality collapse through different mechanisms. For BYOL and SimSiam, the stop-gradient combined with the predictor effectively implements an implicit form of EM-style optimization that prevents trivial solutions [15]. For Barlow Twins and VICReg, the decorrelation and variance regularization terms directly enforce a full-rank embedding distribution, ensuring no directions in the representation space are collapsed. This theoretical convergence mirrors the architectural insight from Section 2.1 regarding projection heads: in both contrastive and non-contrastive settings, certain components serve to absorb or prevent degenerate solutions rather than directly producing the final representation.

Several hybrid and unified frameworks have built upon these foundations. The **RegCLR** framework, for instance, integrates enhanced Barlow Twins as a representative regularized method alongside masked autoencoders as a contrastive component, demonstrating that redundancy-reduction objectives can be productively combined with reconstruction-based objectives — foreshadowing the hybrid methods that will be examined in Section 2.3 [101]. Similarly, analyses such as UniGrad have revealed deep mathematical equivalences between contrastive, non-contrastive, and feature-decorrelation methods, suggesting these seemingly distinct approaches may be understood as different instances of a unified gradient-based framework [15; 26].

Overall, non-contrastive and redundancy-reduction methods represent a theoretically rich and practically effective family of self-supervised learning approaches. By replacing the requirement for explicit negative pairs with principled architectural and objective-level mechanisms, they offer greater flexibility in batch size selection, simpler implementation, and strong empirical performance across diverse benchmarks. Together with the contrastive methods of Section 2.1, they define the landscape of pairwise similarity-based self-supervised learning, against which the reconstruction-based and generative paradigms explored in Section 2.3 provide a complementary and increasingly integrated perspective [26; 25].

### 2.3 Masked and Generative Approaches

## 2.3 Masked and Generative Approaches

Masked and generative self-supervised learning methods constitute a foundational paradigm that frames representation learning as a reconstruction problem. In contrast to the contrastive and non-contrastive methods discussed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2, which rely on pairwise similarity objectives or redundancy-reduction principles, these approaches task a model with recovering corrupted, masked, or otherwise incomplete input signals, thereby forcing the encoder to internalize rich structural and semantic knowledge about the data distribution. This reconstruction-based perspective provides a complementary inductive bias that proves particularly valuable for dense prediction tasks and fine-grained understanding, laying the groundwork for the clustering-based methods explored in Section 2.4.

### Masked Image Modeling

The most influential instantiation of this paradigm in computer vision is Masked Autoencoder (MAE), which divides an image into non-overlapping patches, randomly masks a large fraction of them (typically 75%), and trains a Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder–decoder architecture to reconstruct the original pixel values of the masked patches. The asymmetric design—a heavy encoder operating only on visible patches and a lightweight decoder tasked with reconstruction—makes the approach computationally efficient while encouraging the encoder to develop holistic, context-aware representations. The success of MAE and related masked image modeling approaches demonstrated that high masking ratios push the model beyond learning low-level texture statistics, instead requiring understanding of global spatial relationships and semantic content.

More broadly, masked modeling traces its conceptual roots to denoising autoencoders and Masked Language Modeling (MLM) in NLP, where models such as BERT predict masked tokens given their surrounding context. Transferring this idea to vision requires careful consideration of the tokenization and masking strategy. Unlike discrete text tokens, raw image pixels are highly redundant at the local level; consequently, naive low-ratio masking leads to trivial reconstruction tasks solvable through local interpolation. High masking ratios and patch-level tokenization address this, making the task genuinely predictive and semantically meaningful.

Beyond MAE, the Masked Image Modeling family includes BEiT, which predicts discrete visual tokens from a pre-trained dVAE rather than raw pixels, and SimMIM, which employs a simpler direct pixel regression objective. These variants collectively explore the design space along several axes: the reconstruction target (raw pixels, discrete tokens, HOG features, or deep features from a momentum encoder), the masking strategy (random, block, or structured), and the decoder architecture (shallow MLP versus deeper Transformer).

### Generative Self-Supervised Methods

Generative approaches more broadly include Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and their self-supervised extensions. Methods such as S3VAE integrate structured latent variable models with self-supervised objectives, disentangling content from nuisance factors such as motion or viewpoint. These probabilistic generative models provide principled Bayesian grounding for representation learning, connecting self-supervised objectives to likelihood maximization and posterior inference. The encoder in such frameworks learns an approximate posterior over latent codes, while the decoder models the data likelihood conditioned on those codes, enabling explicit control over what information is encoded and what is discarded.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) also provide a generative self-supervised learning signal, though their training instability and mode-collapse issues make them less dominant for representation learning compared to reconstruction-based methods. Nonetheless, GAN-based pretraining has been explored for learning representations that capture high-frequency visual detail absent in pixel-MSE objectives.

### Complementarity of Generative and Contrastive Signals

A key insight motivating hybrid frameworks is that generative and contrastive objectives are complementary: reconstruction-based objectives encourage the model to retain fine-grained local detail about input structure, while contrastive objectives push the model to learn globally invariant, semantically discriminative representations [35]. This complementarity directly bridges the methods reviewed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 with the reconstruction-based approaches discussed here. Purely generative approaches can produce representations burdened with low-level pixel statistics irrelevant to semantic tasks, while purely contrastive approaches may discard structured local information necessary for dense prediction tasks such as segmentation and detection.

This complementarity motivates a growing class of hybrid frameworks. Contrastive Masked Autoencoder (CAN) and related methods like MACRL integrate masked autoencoding reconstruction loss with an instance-level or patch-level contrastive objective. In such frameworks, the encoder is jointly trained to reconstruct masked regions and to align representations of different augmented views of the same image. The reconstruction loss anchors the representation to spatially structured input content, while the contrastive loss promotes semantic invariance across transformations. Empirical results on benchmarks such as ImageNet demonstrate that combining both objectives yields representations that outperform either objective alone on both linear evaluation and fine-tuning protocols.

The integration strategies vary across hybrid methods. Some approaches apply contrastive loss at the CLS token level while applying reconstruction loss at the patch token level, exploiting the natural spatial granularity of transformer architectures. Others propose alternating optimization between generative and contrastive phases, or weighting the two objectives dynamically during training. The multi-objective nature of these frameworks also connects to multi-task self-supervised learning perspectives, where combining complementary pretext tasks improves representation quality and downstream generalization [102; 103].

### Reconstruction Targets and Architecture Design

An important design dimension in masked and generative SSL is the choice of reconstruction target. Reconstructing raw pixels with mean squared error provides a dense, spatially uniform training signal but may focus model capacity on perceptually unimportant high-frequency content. Alternative targets include normalized pixel values, discrete visual tokens from a pre-trained tokenizer, and deep feature representations from a momentum encoder (as in data2vec), the latter enabling target representations that are inherently semantic and contextual. This progression toward semantic reconstruction targets also foreshadows the prototype-based objectives employed by the clustering methods discussed in Section 2.4, where the supervisory signal similarly evolves from raw data statistics toward learned semantic structure.

The encoder architecture profoundly affects what is learned. Vision Transformers are particularly well-suited for masked modeling because their self-attention mechanism naturally handles variable-length sequences of visible patches, allowing the encoder to operate solely on unmasked tokens without requiring a mask token placeholder. Convolutional architectures, by contrast, require more careful handling of masked regions, though sparse convolution and masked convolution techniques have been explored. The decoder's depth and width also matter: a sufficiently expressive decoder can reconstruct masked content from minimal encoder features, potentially causing the encoder to underfit; a too-shallow decoder shifts the burden entirely to the encoder, which is the desirable inductive bias for pretraining a powerful representation.

### Summary

Masked and generative self-supervised approaches have become a central pillar of modern visual representation learning, occupying a distinct and complementary role alongside the contrastive and non-contrastive methods surveyed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2. Their reconstruction-based objectives provide rich self-supervisory signals without requiring negative pairs, expensive augmentation pipelines, or momentum encoders. Hybrid frameworks that combine masked autoencoders with contrastive objectives represent the current frontier, offering representations that are simultaneously locally structured and globally semantic [35]. The semantic structure implicit in reconstruction targets also connects naturally to the explicit prototype and cluster assignment mechanisms that characterize the clustering-based methods examined in the following section. Continued progress in this area is expected through improved reconstruction targets, more expressive decoder architectures, and principled integration of generative and contrastive signals.

### 2.4 Clustering-Based Methods

## 2.4 Clustering-Based Methods

Clustering-based self-supervised learning represents a distinct paradigm that bridges unsupervised clustering and representation learning by using cluster assignments as pseudo-labels to provide supervisory signals. Where the masked and generative methods discussed in Section 2.3 derive supervision from reconstruction objectives, and the contrastive methods of Sections 2.1 and 2.2 rely on explicit pairwise similarity comparisons, clustering-based approaches iteratively assign data points to learned prototypes or cluster centroids and train encoders to predict these assignments, effectively bootstrapping semantic structure directly from the data distribution. As will become clear in Section 2.5, this prototype-discovery perspective also provides natural foundations for the hybrid and unified frameworks that seek to integrate complementary SSL objectives.

### Core Prototype Assignment Mechanisms

The foundational insight of clustering-based SSL is that semantically similar examples should be assigned to the same prototype, and this assignment process can serve as a self-generated supervisory signal. SwAV (Swapping Assignments between Views) exemplifies this paradigm by jointly learning visual representations and a set of learnable prototype vectors. Rather than directly comparing feature vectors across views as in contrastive methods, SwAV computes soft assignments of each view to the prototypes and enforces consistency by predicting the assignment of one view from the representation of another view — a "swapped" prediction objective. This design eliminates the need for explicit negative pairs while still leveraging the structural contrast between multiple views of the same image, yielding competitive performance while being significantly more memory-efficient than large-batch contrastive methods.

The prototype assignment in SwAV is computed via an online variant of the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm, which solves an optimal transport problem to produce balanced, equipartitioned soft assignments across prototypes. This balancing constraint is critical to the method's success: without it, the model could trivially collapse by assigning all examples to a single prototype. The equipartition constraint ensures that each prototype is used approximately equally across a batch, thus promoting diverse and informative assignments that carry meaningful supervisory signal. This concern with collapse prevention echoes the dimensional collapse and representation degeneration problems addressed by the non-contrastive methods reviewed in Section 2.2, though clustering-based methods address it through assignment balancing rather than through architectural asymmetry or redundancy reduction.

### Online Clustering and Pseudo-Label Generation

A key challenge in clustering-based SSL is performing clustering in an online, efficient manner during training, avoiding the expensive offline clustering steps that characterized earlier methods. Online clustering approaches update prototype assignments simultaneously with representation learning, enabling end-to-end training without alternating optimization stages. The pseudo-labels generated by cluster assignments guide the encoder to produce representations where semantically related examples cluster together, inducing a form of semantic invariance that generalizes well to downstream tasks.

DINO (Self-Distillation with No Labels) and its successors, including DinoSR, build upon related principles by treating the clustering or prototype assignment problem through a self-distillation lens, where a student network is trained to match the output distribution of a momentum-updated teacher network. The teacher's outputs serve as soft pseudo-labels akin to cluster assignments, and enforcing consistency between student and teacher predictions across different views effectively implements an online clustering objective with implicit prototype discovery. This connection between self-distillation and clustering highlights the deep relationship between these two paradigms, and recalls the momentum encoder designs discussed in the context of non-contrastive methods in Section 2.2, where slowly evolving teacher networks similarly provide stable supervisory targets.

### Preventing Cluster Collapse

A central failure mode in clustering-based SSL is cluster collapse, where the model degenerates by assigning all or most examples to a single cluster, producing uninformative representations. Several strategies have been developed to counteract this phenomenon. The most direct approach is the equipartition or balanced assignment constraint employed by SwAV, which explicitly prevents any single prototype from dominating by enforcing a uniform marginal distribution over cluster assignments within each batch. This is implemented efficiently through the Sinkhorn-Knopp iterative algorithm, which converges rapidly in practice.

Beyond explicit balancing constraints, temperature scaling in the softmax computation of assignment probabilities plays an important role in controlling the sharpness of assignments. Sharp assignments (low temperature) encourage confident prototype association but risk instability early in training, while softer assignments (high temperature) provide more stable gradients but may yield less discriminative representations. Practical implementations typically employ a combination of a sharper teacher distribution and a softer student distribution, as popularized in self-distillation frameworks, creating an asymmetry that stabilizes training while preserving the discriminative capacity of learned prototypes.

Multi-crop augmentation strategies, where in addition to two standard-resolution views several additional low-resolution crops are generated, provide an efficient mechanism to increase the number of view pairs without proportionally increasing computational cost. These additional crops enrich the training signal and improve the diversity of assignments seen during each optimization step, further mitigating the risk of collapse by ensuring that prototypes must explain varied spatial and scale-level views of each image. The use of such structured augmentation policies draws a natural parallel to the augmentation design principles central to the contrastive methods reviewed in Section 2.1.

### Handling Permutation Invariance

A subtle but important challenge in clustering-based SSL concerns the permutation invariance of cluster assignments: the labeling of clusters is arbitrary and can permute across training iterations or between different mini-batches, making it difficult to enforce consistency. Prototype-based methods address this by maintaining a fixed set of prototype vectors that are updated via gradient descent alongside the encoder, anchoring the assignment space and preventing arbitrary permutations from disrupting training stability. The use of stop-gradient operations on certain components and the momentum-based update of teacher networks in self-distillation frameworks further stabilize the assignment space by slowing the evolution of the target distribution relative to the encoder being trained.

Additionally, online clustering methods typically normalize both feature representations and prototype vectors to lie on the unit hypersphere, ensuring that inner product-based assignment scores are well-conditioned and that the geometry of the prototype space remains stable throughout training. This hyperspherical normalization, combined with prototype re-normalization after each gradient update, prevents individual prototypes from growing disproportionately in magnitude and dominating the assignment distribution. The broader role of normalization and projection in shaping representation geometry is a recurring theme across SSL paradigms, connecting these design choices to the projection head analyses relevant to contrastive and non-contrastive methods alike.

### Bayesian and Energy-Based Unifications

Beyond the algorithmic perspective, clustering-based SSL methods admit elegant theoretical unifications through probabilistic and energy-based frameworks. From a generative latent variable model perspective, the prototype assignment problem can be interpreted as inferring the posterior distribution over discrete latent variables in a mixture model, where each prototype corresponds to a mixture component and the encoder learns to produce representations that maximize the likelihood of observed views under this mixture. This connection to generative models provides a principled explanation for why clustering-based objectives yield semantically meaningful representations: they are performing approximate maximum likelihood estimation in a structured latent variable model. Notably, this latent variable interpretation also connects back to the probabilistic generative frameworks discussed in Section 2.3, where VAE-based methods similarly frame representation learning as posterior inference.

Energy-based model (EBM) perspectives further enrich this theoretical picture by interpreting the contrastive and clustering objectives as defining an energy landscape over the representation space, where low energy is assigned to plausible data representations and high energy to unlikely ones. The Sinkhorn-based balanced assignment in SwAV can be viewed as computing a regularized optimal transport plan that minimizes an entropic transport cost between the empirical data distribution and the prototype distribution, connecting clustering-based SSL to the rich literature on optimal transport and entropic regularization. These energy-based and optimal transport perspectives, in turn, provide direct theoretical grounding for the Bayesian unification frameworks explored in the hybrid methods of Section 2.5.

Bayesian formulations of clustering SSL also provide natural extensions to hierarchical prototypes, variable numbers of clusters, and uncertainty-aware representations. By placing priors over prototype locations and assignment probabilities, Bayesian clustering SSL frameworks can automatically infer the appropriate number of semantic categories present in unlabeled data, rather than treating the number of prototypes as a fixed hyperparameter. These extensions connect clustering-based SSL to classical Bayesian nonparametric methods such as Dirichlet process mixture models, opening avenues for more principled and flexible self-supervised representation learning in settings with unknown or variable semantic complexity.

### Summary

Clustering-based self-supervised learning methods offer a compelling alternative to the purely contrastive methods of Sections 2.1–2.2 and the generative approaches of Section 2.3, by leveraging the semantic structure inherent in unlabeled data through prototype discovery and online pseudo-label generation. Key contributions of this paradigm include the development of efficient online clustering mechanisms, balanced assignment strategies that prevent cluster collapse, and theoretical connections to generative latent variable models and energy-based frameworks. These methods achieve strong downstream performance while being more memory-efficient than large-batch contrastive approaches, making them particularly attractive for large-scale pretraining scenarios where negative pair computation becomes a bottleneck. The prototype structures and Bayesian unifications introduced here also set the stage for the hybrid and unified frameworks examined in Section 2.5, where the complementary strengths of clustering-based, contrastive, and generative objectives are combined into more powerful and theoretically grounded SSL systems.

### 2.5 Hybrid and Unified Frameworks

## Hybrid and Unified Self-Supervised Frameworks

The preceding sections have examined self-supervised learning paradigms in relative isolation—contrastive methods that enforce semantic invariances through explicit positive and negative pair comparisons, generative and reconstruction-based objectives that preserve fine-grained structural fidelity, and clustering-based approaches that discover semantic prototypes through online pseudo-label generation. Each of these paradigms encodes a distinct inductive bias, and each exhibits characteristic strengths alongside well-documented limitations. Hybrid and unified self-supervised frameworks have emerged as a compelling research direction that seeks to overcome these individual limitations by combining complementary objectives, thereby improving the richness, generalizability, and robustness of learned representations.

A central motivation for hybrid frameworks is that distinct SSL objectives capture different and often complementary aspects of the data distribution. Contrastive objectives excel at enforcing global semantic invariances by pulling together similar views and pushing apart dissimilar ones, while generative and reconstruction-based objectives encourage the model to retain fine-grained local information about the input structure. When these two families are combined—as in frameworks pairing contrastive losses with reconstruction losses—the encoder is simultaneously encouraged to learn both coarse semantic similarity and detailed structural fidelity, a combination that individually specialized methods cannot achieve. This complementary nature is reflected in hybrid approaches such as ConRec and RegCLR, which augment standard contrastive objectives with reconstruction terms, and in work that systematically studies how combining generative and contrastive signals yields representations superior to those obtained from either signal alone [58].

Beyond simple additive combinations of losses, another class of hybrid methods focuses on cross-paradigm distillation. In these approaches, knowledge from one SSL paradigm is transferred to a model trained under a different paradigm, allowing the student to inherit desirable properties—such as rich semantic clustering from a contrastive teacher or detailed reconstruction fidelity from a generative teacher—that would otherwise be absent. This connection to distillation is particularly natural given the momentum-based teacher-student architectures discussed in the context of non-contrastive and clustering-based methods, where the teacher network already serves as a slowly evolving source of supervisory signal. The DMT framework extends this principle across paradigm boundaries by distilling knowledge from multiple self-supervised teachers representing different paradigms, demonstrating that leveraging the complementary strengths of contrastive and masked image modeling teachers consistently improves student representations on both classification and dense prediction benchmarks [104].

A particularly illuminating direction within unified frameworks involves gradient-level analyses that reveal deep theoretical equivalences among seemingly distinct SSL objectives. Unified gradient analyses such as UniGrad have demonstrated that contrastive, non-contrastive, and feature-decorrelation methods—despite their apparent algorithmic differences in how they handle negative samples, stop-gradients, and covariance penalties—often induce similar gradient update structures on the encoder. These findings suggest that the diversity of SSL paradigms may be understood as different instantiations of a common underlying optimization principle, paving the way for principled hybrid designs that combine objectives in theoretically grounded ways rather than through ad hoc engineering. This perspective echoes the architectural insights discussed previously, where projection heads, momentum encoders, and predictor networks were shown to interact in non-trivial ways across different SSL paradigms.

The Bayesian perspective also contributes substantially to unification efforts. By analyzing self-supervised objectives through probabilistic graphical models and likelihood-based frameworks, it becomes possible to derive unified lower bounds that integrate clustering-based and energy-based objectives—building directly on the connections to mixture models and optimal transport discussed in the context of clustering-based methods—enabling joint training without the asymmetric architectural components typically required to prevent collapse [65] [68]. Such principled unifications not only clarify the theoretical relationships between paradigms but also yield practical algorithms that outperform paradigm-specific baselines.

Similarly, combining contrastive self-supervised objectives with generative augmentation strategies has been explored to enrich the diversity of views and training signals, with generative models producing novel positive examples that complement traditional augmentation pipelines [35]. In the domain of sentence representation learning, hybrid generative-contrastive approaches have demonstrated that phrase-reconstruction objectives can match or surpass purely contrastive methods on semantic similarity benchmarks, challenging the assumption that contrastive methods are strictly superior for representation learning [105].

Across these varied instantiations, a consistent finding is that hybrid and unified frameworks tend to outperform single-paradigm methods, particularly on diverse downstream tasks that require both semantic and structural understanding. This observation connects naturally to the architectural considerations examined in the following section, where output dimensionality, sparsity regularization, and layer-wise self-distillation represent additional design levers that interact closely with the choice of pretraining objective. The trend toward hybridization reflects a maturing recognition in the SSL community that no single inductive bias—invariance to augmentation, reconstruction fidelity, or cluster assignment consistency—is universally optimal, and that principled combinations of complementary objectives constitute the most promising path toward universally transferable representations.

### 2.6 Architectural Components and Design Choices

## Architectural Components of Self-Supervised Learning Frameworks

Architectural design choices play a pivotal role in determining the effectiveness of self-supervised learning (SSL) pipelines. While the preceding sections have examined SSL paradigms primarily through the lens of their training objectives—contrastive losses, generative reconstruction, clustering-based assignments, and hybrid combinations thereof—the architectural components that instantiate these objectives are equally consequential. Understanding how individual components contribute to representation quality has been a central focus of empirical and theoretical investigations across the SSL literature, and many of the design decisions described here find their deepest justification in how they interact with and support the objectives discussed previously.

**Projection Heads.** One of the most influential architectural discoveries in modern SSL is the use of a projection head—a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) appended to the backbone encoder during pretraining and subsequently discarded at evaluation time. Originally introduced and analyzed in the context of contrastive frameworks, the projection head serves as a buffer that absorbs invariances imposed by the contrastive loss, thereby preventing the encoder itself from discarding useful information needed for downstream tasks. Empirical studies have shown that deeper and wider projection heads generally yield better representations, while the dimensionality of the projection output is a critical hyperparameter that balances the expressiveness of the contrastive objective against overfitting and dimensional collapse. The theoretical relationship between the projection head and dimensional collapse has been examined extensively, with findings suggesting that sparsity in the projection subspace correlates with richer encoder representations.

**Momentum Encoders and Exponential Moving Averages.** A key innovation in contrastive SSL is the momentum encoder, wherein a slowly evolving copy of the online encoder is maintained via an exponential moving average (EMA) of its parameters. This design stabilizes training by providing consistent target representations without requiring large batch sizes or explicit negative pairs. The momentum encoder decouples the representation update dynamics from the noisy gradient updates of the online network, effectively acting as a slowly moving teacher that guides the student network toward stable and informative representations. The momentum coefficient is a critical hyperparameter: too small a value introduces instability, while too large a value causes the target network to evolve too slowly to provide useful learning signal. As will be discussed in the following section on collapse prevention, this momentum-based stabilization also plays a direct role in maintaining well-structured representation spaces throughout training.

**Predictor Networks in Non-Contrastive Methods.** Non-contrastive SSL methods such as BYOL and SimSiam introduce an additional lightweight predictor network applied exclusively to the online branch of a siamese architecture. This asymmetric design, combined with a stop-gradient operation on the target branch, is central to avoiding representational collapse without negative pairs. The predictor network introduces an asymmetry in the optimization landscape that prevents trivial constant solutions from being stable fixed points of the learning dynamics. Theoretical analyses have shown that the predictor network implicitly performs an approximate expectation-maximization step, iteratively updating the representation toward a moving target, and that its architecture—particularly its depth and bottleneck structure—significantly influences the quality of the learned representations. The interplay between predictor networks and stop-gradient operations, which the following section examines in detail as a collapse prevention strategy, represents one of the most theoretically rich areas of current SSL research.

**Siamese and Asymmetric Network Structures.** The siamese network architecture, in which two branches share weights and process different augmented views of the same input, forms the backbone of many SSL frameworks. Variants include fully symmetric siamese networks used in contrastive methods, partially asymmetric architectures with a predictor on one branch, and fully asymmetric teacher-student designs with momentum updates. The choice between these structural variants involves trade-offs between training stability, sensitivity to batch size, memory requirements, and representation quality. Asymmetric designs have been found particularly effective in self-distillation settings, where representations from one part of a network are used to supervise representations in another part, as explored in transformer-based SSL frameworks that leverage self-distillation across layers to encourage hierarchically structured features [14]. These architectural asymmetries also connect intimately to the hybrid and unified frameworks discussed in the preceding section, where teacher-student distillation across paradigm boundaries exploits similar structural principles.

**Output Dimensionality and Sparsity Regularization.** The dimensionality of the representation space has a non-trivial effect on SSL performance. Too low a dimensionality encourages dimensional collapse, where the effective rank of the representation matrix degrades and all samples map to a low-dimensional manifold. Too high a dimensionality can lead to over-parameterization and poor generalization. Techniques such as variance-covariance regularization explicitly penalize dimensional collapse by encouraging the covariance matrix of representations to be diagonal with unit variance, effectively distributing information across all output dimensions. Sparsity regularization in the projection subspace has been explored as an alternative mechanism to promote well-distributed and informative representations. Both of these strategies—variance-covariance regularization and sparsity regularization—are examined more formally in the following section as components of the broader toolkit for preventing representational collapse.

**Self-Distillation Across Transformer Layers.** With the rise of Vision Transformers as backbone architectures in SSL, new design opportunities have emerged around self-distillation across transformer layers. Rather than treating the transformer as a monolithic encoder, these approaches exploit the hierarchical structure of transformer representations by using intermediate layer outputs as additional supervision signals. This multi-scale self-distillation encourages the network to encode information at multiple levels of abstraction and has been shown to improve the spatial and semantic quality of learned representations, particularly for dense prediction tasks such as segmentation and detection [14].

**Cross-Modal Architectural Adaptations.** In multimodal SSL settings, architectural design must additionally account for the heterogeneity of input modalities. Multimodal frameworks have explored shared encoder architectures, modality-specific encoders with a shared projection space, and fusion networks that integrate representations from different modalities [72; 73]. The design of the fusion mechanism—whether through concatenation, cross-attention, or dedicated fusion transformers—critically affects the quality of the learned joint representations and the ability to generalize across modalities.

In summary, the quality of self-supervised representations is highly sensitive to architectural choices spanning projection heads, momentum encoders, predictor networks, siamese structures, output dimensionality, regularization strategies, and layer-wise self-distillation. These components do not operate independently: they interact in non-trivial ways with one another and with the choice of pretraining objective, whether contrastive, generative, clustering-based, or hybrid. As the following section makes clear, many of these architectural decisions are most deeply understood through the lens of representational collapse prevention—a perspective that unifies seemingly disparate design choices under a common theoretical framework and underscores that principled SSL design requires careful co-consideration of network architecture, training objective, and optimization dynamics.

### 2.7 Strategies to Prevent Representational Collapse

## 2.7 Strategies to Prevent Representational Collapse

Building upon the architectural components discussed in the previous section—projection heads, momentum encoders, predictor networks, and variance-covariance regularization—it becomes clear that many of these design choices are not arbitrary but are specifically motivated by a fundamental challenge in self-supervised learning: representational collapse. This refers to the degenerate situation where an encoder maps all inputs to the same or nearly identical representations, thereby losing all discriminative information. Understanding and preventing this failure mode is central to the design of effective SSL frameworks, and the architectural strategies previously described find their deepest justification in how they address this problem. Three principal forms of collapse have been identified and studied in the literature: full representational collapse, dimensional collapse, and cluster collapse.

**Full Representational Collapse** occurs when the encoder produces a constant output regardless of the input, rendering the learned representation entirely uninformative. This mode of failure is most acute in symmetric frameworks without any mechanism to enforce diversity among representations. **Dimensional collapse** is a subtler but equally damaging phenomenon in which the representations span only a low-dimensional subspace of the embedding space, even when individual outputs differ—leading to representations that capture only a narrow facet of the data's variability. **Cluster collapse** arises in clustering-based and prototype-assignment methods, where the model learns to assign all inputs to a single cluster or prototype, destroying the structural organization of the representation space.

### Negative Sampling

The earliest and most direct strategy to counter collapse is the use of negative pairs in contrastive learning. Frameworks such as SimCLR, MoCo, and PIRL explicitly define positive pairs (different augmentations of the same instance) and negative pairs (augmentations of distinct instances), and optimize objectives such as InfoNCE or NT-Xent that push representations of negative pairs apart while pulling positive pairs together. By forcing the model to distinguish between different instances, negative sampling provides a direct gradient signal that prevents the model from collapsing to a constant solution. The number and quality of negatives are critical: too few negatives lead to insufficient repulsion, while false negatives (semantically similar instances treated as negatives) can harm representation quality. Strategies such as large batch sizes, memory banks, and momentum encoders have been proposed to maintain a large and diverse pool of negatives without requiring prohibitively large batch sizes during training. The memory bank approach, introduced in MoCo, stores encoded representations from past iterations and uses them as negatives, decoupling the batch size from the number of negatives available.

### Momentum-Based Updates and Memory Banks

Momentum encoders, as discussed in the context of architectural design in the previous section, serve a dual purpose: beyond stabilizing training, they play a direct role in collapse prevention. In MoCo-style frameworks, a slowly-evolving "key encoder" is maintained as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the "query encoder," ensuring that the stored representations in the memory bank are produced by a consistent encoder. This momentum-based approach addresses the consistency problem that arises when negative keys change rapidly during training, providing stable targets for the contrastive objective and preventing the training dynamics from becoming unstable. By maintaining well-structured negative representations throughout training, momentum encoders indirectly but effectively contribute to keeping the representation space from degenerating.

### Non-Contrastive Methods: Stop-Gradient and Predictor Networks

A major advance in collapse prevention came with the development of non-contrastive methods such as BYOL and SimSiam, which operate entirely without negative pairs yet avoid collapse through architectural asymmetry—specifically through the predictor networks and stop-gradient operations introduced in the previous section's discussion of siamese architectures. The key mechanisms are the use of a predictor network on one branch of the siamese architecture and the application of a stop-gradient operation on the other branch. In SimSiam, one branch's gradient is explicitly blocked, preventing the trivial solution where both branches converge to the same constant. BYOL additionally uses a momentum encoder (EMA target network), which provides a slowly changing target that the online network is trained to predict. Theoretical analyses have explored why these mechanisms succeed: the stop-gradient effectively creates an implicit form of self-distillation, and the predictor network introduces an asymmetry that breaks the symmetry of the collapse solution. These methods demonstrate that the repulsive force of negative pairs is not strictly necessary for collapse prevention if appropriate architectural constraints are imposed.

### Variance-Covariance Regularization

A complementary approach to collapse prevention operates directly on the statistical properties of the representation distribution, connecting closely to the output dimensionality and sparsity regularization concerns raised in the previous section. Barlow Twins introduces a cross-correlation matrix objective that encourages the representations of two augmented views to be maximally correlated along corresponding dimensions while being minimally correlated across different dimensions, effectively enforcing both alignment and decorrelation of the feature dimensions. VICReg (Variance-Invariance-Covariance Regularization) further decomposes this into explicit variance, invariance, and covariance terms: the variance term prevents dimensional collapse by enforcing that each dimension of the representation has sufficient variance across the batch; the invariance term pulls representations of positive pairs together; and the covariance term decorrelates different dimensions to prevent them from encoding redundant information. The TiCo method similarly uses a correlation matrix-based regularizer. These approaches directly address dimensional collapse by penalizing degenerate covariance structures, and do not require negative pairs or architectural asymmetry, making them conceptually clean and easy to analyze theoretically.

### Batch Normalization

Batch normalization plays a subtle but important role in collapse prevention, particularly in early contrastive frameworks such as SimCLR. By normalizing representations across the batch, batch normalization prevents any single dimension from dominating or collapsing, and introduces an implicit form of competition among samples that discourages constant-output solutions. It has been shown empirically and theoretically that batch normalization is a key factor in the success of certain SSL methods, with its removal leading to significant performance degradation or outright collapse. However, its reliance on batch statistics introduces dependencies across samples in a batch, which can be problematic in small-batch or distributed training settings, motivating alternatives such as layer normalization or group normalization.

### Clustering-Based Collapse Prevention

In clustering-based and prototype-assignment methods such as SwAV, cluster collapse—where all samples are assigned to a single cluster—is prevented through the use of equipartition constraints or Sinkhorn-Knopp-based optimal transport assignments. These mechanisms enforce that cluster assignments are distributed uniformly across all prototypes within a batch, preventing any single prototype from dominating. Online clustering with online pseudo-label generation further requires careful design of assignment strategies that are invariant to the permutation of prototypes and robust to noisy or redundant views.

### Orthogonality Constraints and Sparsity Regularization

Additional strategies for preventing dimensional collapse include enforcing orthogonality constraints on the weight matrices of the projection or prediction heads, which directly prevents the learned features from becoming linearly dependent. Sparsity regularization on projection subspaces has been proposed to encourage the use of a broad set of dimensions rather than concentrating information in a low-dimensional manifold—complementing the sparsity-based approaches to projection head design discussed in the previous section.

### Unified Theoretical Perspectives

Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to unify these diverse strategies under common principles. From an information-theoretic perspective [34], collapse can be understood as a failure to capture the mutual information between different views of the data, and all successful anti-collapse mechanisms can be seen as different ways of maximizing or lower-bounding this mutual information. Spectral and kernel-based analyses connect SSL objectives to classical spectral embedding methods, showing that dimensional collapse corresponds to the degenerate case where only a few eigenvectors of the kernel matrix are captured. Gradient-based analyses have shown that the combination of stop-gradient and predictor network in SimSiam-style methods can be interpreted as an alternating minimization procedure that avoids the trivial fixed point. More broadly, the landscape of collapse prevention strategies reflects a deep connection between representation geometry, optimization dynamics, and statistical properties of the data distribution—suggesting that principled SSL design requires careful consideration of all three aspects simultaneously.

In summary, preventing representational collapse is not a single problem but a family of related challenges, each requiring tailored solutions. The field has converged on a rich toolkit including negative sampling with memory banks and momentum encoders, architectural asymmetry with stop-gradient and predictor networks, direct variance-covariance regularization, batch normalization, equipartition constraints for clustering, and orthogonality-based regularization. These strategies are deeply intertwined with the architectural design choices surveyed in the previous section, underscoring the fact that effective SSL frameworks emerge from the careful co-design of network architecture and training objective. Theoretical frameworks continue to develop in parallel, aiming to provide unified and principled explanations for when and why each strategy succeeds, and to guide the design of next-generation SSL algorithms that are robust to collapse across diverse data regimes and architectures.

## 3 Data Augmentation, Multi-View Learning, and Input Modality Design

### 3.1 The Role of Data Augmentation in Self-Supervised Learning

Data augmentation lies at the heart of self-supervised learning (SSL) frameworks, serving as the primary mechanism through which supervisory signals are constructed without human-provided labels. As established in the preceding discussion of multi-view learning, the construction of multiple views from the same underlying datum is central to SSL, and data augmentation is the principal means by which these views are generated. By applying different transformations to the same underlying data sample, SSL methods generate multiple views that are treated as positive pairs, while samples derived from distinct inputs constitute negative pairs. The choice of augmentation strategy therefore directly determines what invariances the learned representations will encode, and which semantic properties will be preserved or discarded in the resulting embedding space.

In contrastive learning frameworks, the composition and strength of augmentations have a profound impact on representation quality. Augmentations that are too weak fail to provide sufficient diversity between positive pairs, causing the model to rely on trivial shortcuts rather than learning meaningful semantic features. Conversely, overly aggressive transformations may destroy task-relevant information, making it difficult for the encoder to recognize that two views originate from the same sample. This tension—which mirrors the broader challenge of balancing view diversity and semantic consistency discussed in the context of multi-view learning—motivates careful selection and calibration of augmentation pipelines, which typically combine multiple operations such as random cropping, color jittering, Gaussian blurring, grayscale conversion, horizontal flipping, and random resizing [14; 1].

A key insight established in the SSL literature is that different augmentation choices shape the downstream task performance in fundamentally different ways. Augmentations that induce invariance to color and texture, for example, bias the representation toward shape-based features, while spatial augmentations promote invariance to object position and scale. This means that the downstream task and the pretext augmentation strategy must be aligned: augmentations that destroy information relevant to the target task will result in representations that perform poorly when transferred [14]. For instance, in medical imaging applications such as breast cancer detection on mammography, domain-specific augmentation paradigms must be identified carefully to avoid discarding clinically meaningful visual patterns [8].

Beyond manually crafted augmentation pipelines, automatic augmentation policies have been explored to reduce the burden of human design and discover augmentation strategies that are optimally suited to a given dataset or task. These approaches search over a combinatorial space of augmentation operations and their magnitudes, either through reinforcement learning, evolutionary algorithms, or differentiable optimization, to find policies that maximize representation quality as measured by downstream performance. Such automation is particularly valuable in specialized domains where expert intuition about beneficial transformations is limited.

Generative model-based augmentation strategies represent another important direction, where data-driven models are used to synthesize diverse and realistic views beyond the reach of traditional hand-crafted transformations. By leveraging generative adversarial networks or similar frameworks to produce augmented samples, these approaches can capture complex distributional variations in the data, enriching the diversity of positive pairs and potentially exposing the encoder to out-of-distribution variations that improve generalization. For example, in remote sensing applications where data heterogeneity is high due to varying sensor characteristics, generative augmentation can help bridge domain gaps that fixed augmentation pipelines cannot address.

Expert-guided and domain-specific augmentation approaches are particularly critical in modalities such as medical imaging, audio, and sensor-based activity recognition, where generic image augmentations may be inappropriate or even harmful. In human activity recognition from wearable sensor data, for example, augmentations must preserve temporal structure and physiological plausibility to generate meaningful positive pairs [106; 16]. Similarly, in audio processing, augmentations must respect the spectro-temporal properties of the signal to avoid generating physically implausible views [6]. In medical imaging, transformations must be chosen to preserve anatomically meaningful information while providing sufficient view diversity to prevent the model from exploiting low-level artifacts [107; 18]. These domain-specific considerations echo the broader principle that view construction must be guided by the invariances and equivariances relevant to the target application, as emphasized in the multi-view learning framework.

The role of augmentation invariances in shaping downstream task performance extends beyond the choice of individual operations to their composition and ordering. Research has shown that combining augmentations in a carefully designed sequence can yield richer supervisory signals than applying them independently, and that augmentation strength must be tuned to balance the competing demands of view diversity and information preservation. In fine-grained recognition tasks, for instance, strong spatial augmentations that crop out discriminative regions can severely degrade downstream performance, motivating the development of targeted augmentation strategies that preserve local structure [108].

Overall, data augmentation is not merely a practical implementation detail in SSL frameworks but a principled design choice that encodes prior knowledge about invariances relevant to the target domain. The systematic study of augmentation strategies—encompassing manual design, automatic discovery, and generative synthesis—remains an active and critical area of SSL research, with strong implications for the quality, robustness, and transferability of learned representations across diverse data modalities and application domains [15; 35]. These considerations will continue to inform the design of loss functions and architectural choices explored in subsequent sections, as the interplay between view construction and learning objective is fundamental to the overall effectiveness of self-supervised representation learning systems.

### 3.2 Multi-View Learning Frameworks and View Quality

Multi-view learning constitutes a foundational principle in self-supervised representation learning, wherein multiple transformed or complementary perspectives of the same underlying datum are constructed and leveraged to learn rich, semantically meaningful representations. The central intuition is that a good encoder should capture information that is consistent and shared across different views of the same instance, while discarding view-specific noise and irrelevant details. This principle is closely connected to the mutual information maximization framework, which posits that maximizing the mutual information between representations of different views encourages the encoder to capture the underlying factors of variation that are invariant to the specific augmentations applied.

A critical question in multi-view self-supervised learning concerns what constitutes a "good" view. Empirical and theoretical analyses have shown that views should be sufficiently diverse to prevent the encoder from relying on trivial low-level shortcuts, yet sufficiently similar in semantic content to ensure that the shared information being maximized is task-relevant [23]. When views are too similar, the model fails to learn meaningful abstractions; when they are too dissimilar, the shared information may not correspond to the semantic structure useful for downstream tasks. This tension between diversity and semantic consistency is a central design challenge in multi-view frameworks, and it directly motivates the careful construction of data augmentation pipelines discussed in the following section [109].

Several prominent frameworks have been developed to exploit multi-view redundancy systematically. Contrastive learning methods such as SimCLR, MoCo, and their variants construct two views of each image via stochastic data augmentation and train the encoder to align their representations while pushing apart representations from different images [25]. The quality and composition of augmentations—including random cropping, color jitter, grayscale conversion, and blur—have been shown to significantly influence the quality of learned representations, with certain combinations being particularly beneficial for capturing semantic content [26].

Beyond instance-level contrastive methods, more sophisticated multi-view frameworks have been proposed to leverage richer sources of view diversity. Augmented Multiscale DIM (AMDIM) and related methods exploit multi-scale spatial views within a single image, contrasting local and global representations to encourage the encoder to capture both fine-grained and holistic semantic information. Such multi-scale approaches address limitations of single-resolution cropping by explicitly modeling spatial hierarchies in the representation space [15].

For video and temporal data, the notion of multi-view extends naturally to temporal dimensions. The BraVe framework (Broadening Views) exemplifies this by incorporating both narrow and broad temporal and spatial views, enabling the model to leverage longer temporal contexts and wider spatial extents as positive pairs [29]. This strategy improves the diversity of positive pairs without sacrificing semantic consistency, yielding representations that better capture temporal dynamics and are more transferable to downstream video understanding tasks such as action recognition and retrieval.

Multi-view learning has also been extended to the multimodal setting, where views from different modalities—such as audio and visual streams, or image and text—serve as natural positive pairs [30]. Cross-modal views provide particularly rich supervisory signals because the modalities are semantically aligned yet perceptually distinct, forcing the encoder to capture high-level semantic content rather than modality-specific low-level features. Frameworks exploiting such cross-modal correspondence have demonstrated strong transfer to a wide range of downstream tasks, underscoring the power of diverse view construction. These cross-modal considerations are examined in greater depth in subsequent sections dedicated to multimodal self-supervised learning.

Theoretical analyses complement these empirical observations by formalizing the conditions under which multi-view learning yields good representations. Information-theoretic perspectives establish that maximizing the mutual information between views captures the minimal sufficient statistics of the data with respect to downstream tasks, provided that augmentations are chosen to preserve task-relevant information while discarding nuisance factors [25]. These insights motivate principled view design, where domain knowledge guides the selection of augmentations that align with the invariances and equivariances relevant to target applications—a theme that recurs prominently in the following discussion of augmentation strategies and, later, in the treatment of invariance versus equivariance in representation learning.

Practical strategies for improving positive pair quality and diversity include asymmetric augmentation pipelines, where one view is subjected to stronger augmentations than the other [110], multi-crop strategies that generate several low-resolution views alongside high-resolution crops to increase the number of informative pairs per forward pass, and generative augmentation approaches that leverage learned generative models to synthesize semantically consistent but perceptually diverse views [111]. Together, these developments underscore that the design of multi-view learning frameworks—encompassing the construction, diversity, and quality of views—is as critical as the choice of loss function and architecture in determining the effectiveness of self-supervised representation learning, and they lay the conceptual groundwork for the augmentation strategies and invariance considerations explored in the sections that follow.

### 3.3 Invariant and Equivariant Representation Learning

A central tension in self-supervised representation learning lies between learning representations that are *invariant* to certain transformations and those that are *equivariant*, meaning they preserve or encode transformation-related information. Most contrastive and non-contrastive SSL frameworks are designed to maximize invariance: they train encoders to map differently augmented views of the same input to similar representations, thereby discarding information about the specific transformations applied. This invariance is beneficial when downstream tasks are themselves invariant to those transformations—for instance, object recognition is largely invariant to random cropping and color jittering. However, enforcing excessive invariance can discard information that is critical for other downstream tasks, such as pose estimation, depth prediction, or fine-grained action recognition in video, where the precise nature of transformations carries semantic meaning.

Building directly on the multi-view principles discussed in the previous section, the importance of augmentation choice for shaping invariances becomes particularly clear. As noted in analyses of multi-view learning, the selection of augmentations directly determines what information is retained or discarded in learned representations [34]. If augmentations are too aggressive, task-relevant information may be destroyed; if they are too mild, the SSL objective may be trivially solved without learning meaningful features. This motivates careful, domain-sensitive augmentation design, but also the development of equivariant methods that explicitly encode transformation information rather than discarding it.

Equivariant SSL methods address this limitation by designing objectives that encourage the encoder to represent not just the semantic content of an input but also the nature of the transformation applied. For video data, time-equivariant contrastive learning approaches exploit the temporal structure of video frames, encoding temporal displacements as part of the learned representation rather than treating all temporally nearby frames as equivalent positives. Such approaches are particularly well-suited for downstream tasks like action recognition and video retrieval, where temporal dynamics are central [3]. Similarly, frameworks like DUET and ESSL are designed to disentangle transformation-invariant content from transformation-equivariant factors, producing richer representations that can serve a broader range of downstream tasks.

The tension between invariance and equivariance has motivated the development of hybrid frameworks that jointly learn both types of features. Methods such as MT-SLVR and CIPER combine invariant SSL objectives—which enforce consistency across augmented views—with equivariant objectives that predict or encode the specific transformation parameters. This multi-task formulation has been shown to yield representations that transfer more broadly across diverse downstream tasks, as the model learns to capture both content and transformation structure simultaneously [47; 112]. The multi-task approach mirrors insights from supervised auxiliary task learning, where solving multiple complementary objectives simultaneously acts as a form of data-dependent regularization [33].

From the perspective of transfer learning, the choice between invariant and equivariant representations has significant implications. Representations that are aggressively invariant may perform well on standard benchmarks like ImageNet linear evaluation, where label information is largely invariant to common augmentations, but may fail to transfer to tasks that require spatial or temporal sensitivity. Equivariant representations, by contrast, encode richer structural information about the input, but may require more capacity and careful design to prevent the equivariant components from interfering with semantic content learning. These considerations connect directly to the cross-modal and multi-modal setting discussed in the following section, where the question of which modality-specific or modality-shared information to preserve becomes equally critical.

Empirical investigations have reinforced the importance of this balance. Studies examining the downstream performance of self-supervised models across diverse tasks have found that no single augmentation or invariance strategy dominates across all settings [82; 113]. This suggests that future SSL frameworks should adopt adaptive or task-conditioned approaches to invariance, potentially learning which transformations to be invariant to and which to encode equivariantly based on the structure of available data or downstream task signals. The joint modeling of invariant and equivariant features thus represents a promising and active frontier in self-supervised representation learning, one whose principles extend naturally into the multi-modal settings explored in the sections that follow.

### 3.4 Cross-Modal and Multi-Modal Self-Supervised Learning

## 3.4 Cross-Modal and Multi-Modal Self-Supervised Learning

Cross-modal and multi-modal self-supervised learning leverages the natural co-occurrence and correspondence between different data modalities to construct rich supervisory signals without human annotation. The core intuition is that semantically aligned data streams—such as audio and visual content in videos, or images and their textual descriptions—provide complementary and mutually informative views of the same underlying concept. By learning representations that align or are consistent across these modalities, models can acquire deep semantic understanding that would be difficult to obtain from any single modality alone. This perspective extends naturally from the invariance-equivariance considerations discussed in the preceding section: just as augmentation design determines which intra-modal information is preserved or discarded, the choice of cross-modal alignment objective determines which inter-modal semantic structure is captured in the resulting representations.

One of the most prominent directions in this space is audio-visual correspondence learning, where the natural synchrony between sound and visual events in video provides a powerful self-supervised signal. Models trained to determine whether a given audio clip corresponds to a specific visual frame learn rich representations of both streams, enabling transfer to diverse downstream recognition tasks. This audio-visual self-supervision exploits the temporal co-occurrence structure inherent in video, making it a highly scalable approach to representation learning—and one that connects directly to the temporally-structured self-supervised objectives explored in depth in the following section on video and temporal data.

Video-language joint models represent another major paradigm, exemplified by frameworks that jointly train on video and associated natural language descriptions. Such models learn to align visual and linguistic representations in a shared embedding space, enabling cross-modal retrieval, video captioning, and visual question answering. The linguistic stream provides high-level semantic abstraction that guides the visual encoder toward learning semantically meaningful features beyond low-level appearance statistics. Multi-task learning frameworks like [114] further demonstrate that combining self-supervised image-image contrastive learning with language-image pretraining (CLIP-style objectives) yields representations superior to either approach alone, achieving improvements of +8.1% linear accuracy over self-supervision and +5.2% zero-shot accuracy over language supervision on ImageNet and numerous additional benchmarks.

Cross-modal discrimination methods generalize the contrastive learning paradigm to operate across different modalities rather than across augmented views of the same modality. Here, a positive pair consists of aligned samples from different modalities (e.g., a video clip and its corresponding audio), while negative pairs are formed from misaligned cross-modal combinations. This formulation encourages the model to discover modality-invariant semantic content while discarding modality-specific noise, echoing the broader invariance-learning principles discussed in the previous section but applied across modal boundaries rather than augmentation boundaries. Versatile multimodal networks extend this idea to simultaneously span visual, audio, and language streams, learning unified representations that can be applied flexibly across all constituent modalities.

Multi-modal data augmentation has also emerged as an important technique in cross-modal self-supervised learning. Rather than applying augmentations independently to each modality, frameworks such as LeMDA propose augmentation strategies that operate jointly across modalities, generating correlated perturbations that preserve cross-modal alignment while increasing view diversity. Such coupled augmentation policies help prevent models from exploiting modality-specific shortcuts and encourage the learning of genuinely shared semantic content.

Large-scale multi-modal pretraining datasets and frameworks are critical enablers of cross-modal self-supervised learning. Frameworks like M5Product and SCALE provide large-scale multi-modal datasets with rich annotations spanning product imagery, text, and other signals, enabling pretraining of versatile models capable of understanding complex real-world multi-modal data. These large-scale pretraining efforts highlight the importance of data curation, alignment quality, and scale for the success of cross-modal self-supervised methods, as demonstrated in studies showing that pre-training data source significantly impacts few-shot and full fine-tuning transfer performance [50].

Unified multi-modal frameworks and related approaches demonstrate that a single self-supervised objective—such as predicting masked representations in a latent space—can be applied uniformly across speech, vision, and language modalities, paving the way toward truly modality-agnostic representation learning. The multi-task learning framework in [115] similarly illustrates that learning both invariant and variant features across audio domains enables stronger and more flexible representations for diverse downstream tasks, reinforcing the broader principle that jointly modeling invariant and equivariant structure yields richer, more transferable representations.

Overall, cross-modal self-supervised learning demonstrates that the richest supervisory signal often arises at the intersection of modalities: the semantic content shared between vision, language, audio, and other streams provides a natural, annotation-free training objective that scales with data availability and generalizes broadly. The ongoing development of large-scale multi-modal datasets, improved cross-modal alignment objectives, and unified architectures capable of processing heterogeneous inputs continues to drive rapid progress in this area. These principles of multi-modal alignment and temporal correspondence set the stage for the following section, which examines how self-supervised learning can be specifically tailored to exploit the sequential and temporal structure of video and other time-series data.

### 3.5 Self-Supervised Learning for Video and Temporal Data

## 3.5 Self-Supervised Learning for Video and Temporal Data

Video and temporal data present unique opportunities for self-supervised learning by virtue of their inherent sequential and temporal structure. Unlike static images, videos contain rich temporal dynamics—motion patterns, object trajectories, scene transitions, and causal relationships—that can serve as powerful sources of self-supervisory signals without any manual annotation. Building upon the cross-modal and multi-modal self-supervised learning principles discussed in the previous section, this section surveys the principal strategies for designing pretext tasks and contrastive objectives tailored to exploit these temporal properties, before transitioning to the domain-specific challenges of graph-structured and geometric data addressed in the following section.

### Pretext Tasks Exploiting Temporal Structure

Early approaches to self-supervised video representation learning relied heavily on handcrafted pretext tasks that exploit the temporal dimension. Tasks such as predicting the correct temporal order of shuffled video frames, identifying the playback speed or direction of video clips, and forecasting future frames all leverage the natural structure of video sequences. These tasks encourage encoders to capture motion dynamics and temporal coherence, yielding representations that transfer well to action recognition and video retrieval benchmarks such as UCF101, HMDB51, and Kinetics.

Temporal ordering prediction tasks require the model to distinguish between correctly ordered and shuffled frame sequences, thereby learning that semantically coherent actions unfold in a consistent temporal direction. Similarly, video playback rate prediction—where the model must identify whether a clip is played at normal, fast, or slow speed—forces the encoder to be sensitive to the rate of change in visual content over time. Contrastive predictive coding approaches extend these ideas by predicting future latent representations from past context, making the encoder aware of long-range temporal dependencies.

### Contrastive Learning with Temporal Augmentations

Contrastive learning frameworks have been extended to video by defining positive pairs as different temporal or spatiotemporal crops of the same video clip. The challenge lies in constructing augmentations that preserve the semantic content of the action while introducing sufficient variability to prevent trivial solutions. Spatiotemporal augmentations, including random temporal cropping, frame subsampling, temporal jittering, and spatial transformations such as random resizing and color jitter applied consistently across frames, have emerged as essential tools for generating meaningful views of video clips.

A key design question is how to encode the information about which augmentation was applied in order to build augmentation-equivariant representations. Composable Augmentation Encoding (CATE) addresses this by explicitly encoding the composition of applied augmentations alongside the visual content, enabling the model to disentangle the underlying scene representation from the transformation applied. This augmentation encoding strategy allows the contrastive objective to be applied not only to semantic content but also to the transformation space, improving the richness and generalizability of learned representations.

### Integrating Temporal Information via TaCo and Related Approaches

Beyond spatial contrastive objectives, methods such as TaCo (Temporal Contrastive) integrate temporal consistency constraints directly into the contrastive learning framework. Rather than treating different temporal crops as independent views, TaCo enforces that clips sampled from nearby temporal positions in the same video should yield similar representations, while temporally distant clips from different videos serve as negative pairs. This encourages the encoder to capture smooth temporal dynamics and long-range dependencies, leading to stronger representations for temporal reasoning tasks.

Temporal information has also been integrated into contrastive frameworks through multi-resolution temporal sampling, where clips at different temporal scales—short clips capturing fine-grained motion and longer clips capturing high-level activity—are contrasted together. This multi-scale temporal approach reflects the hierarchical nature of human activities, which exhibit meaningful structure at multiple time scales simultaneously.

### Audiovisual and Spatiotemporal Self-Supervised Learning

Videos naturally pair visual content with accompanying audio streams, making audiovisual correspondence a powerful source of self-supervision that connects directly to the broader cross-modal learning paradigm discussed in the preceding section. By training models to associate audio segments with their corresponding visual frames—and to distinguish mismatched audio-visual pairs as negatives—self-supervised frameworks can learn rich multimodal representations without labels. The spatiotemporal augmentation strategies in audiovisual learning must account for both the visual and audio streams simultaneously, ensuring that positive pairs remain semantically consistent across both modalities while negative pairs capture genuine semantic dissimilarity.

Methods such as BraVe (Broadly Relevant Video Augmentations) explicitly broaden the range of temporal and spatial views used in audiovisual contrastive learning. By sampling views that may come from significantly different temporal positions or spatial extents within the same video, BraVe encourages the encoder to capture more broadly relevant features that remain predictive over a wider range of contexts. This contrasts with narrower augmentation strategies that may lead to representations that are overly sensitive to local spatiotemporal patterns.

### Self-Supervised Learning for Time Series Beyond Video

The principles developed for video self-supervised learning generalize naturally to other forms of temporal data, including electroencephalography (EEG) signals, physiological time series, and event sequences. For instance, predicting whether two time windows are sampled from the same temporal context provides a straightforward yet powerful self-supervisory signal for learning representations of multivariate time series [63]. This relative positioning pretext task encourages the encoder to capture temporal continuity and context, and has been shown to outperform purely supervised approaches in low-data regimes on clinically relevant tasks such as sleep stage scoring [63].

More broadly, the taxonomy of self-supervised methods for time series—encompassing generative, contrastive, and adversarial-based approaches—mirrors the landscape of video SSL but requires careful adaptation of augmentation strategies to preserve the meaningful temporal and frequency properties of the signal [60]. Comparative studies between contrastive and generative SSL for time series reveal complementary strengths: generative approaches such as masked autoencoders tend to excel at reconstruction and forecasting tasks, while contrastive approaches demonstrate stronger performance on classification tasks requiring discriminative features [59].

Non-contrastive self-distillation frameworks have also been adapted for time series, applying the data2vec self-distillation paradigm to temporal data by predicting latent representations of masked views of the same sequence [64]. This approach avoids the need for modality-specific contrastive pair construction while demonstrating competitive performance on both classification and forecasting benchmarks from the UCR and UEA archives [64].

Event sequences—a distinct form of temporal data arising in banking, e-commerce, and healthcare—present additional challenges for SSL, as they are characterized by irregular sampling, discrete event types, and variable lengths. Comparative studies of generative and contrastive SSL approaches for event sequences suggest that no single approach dominates uniformly, motivating hybrid methods that align generative and contrastive embeddings as complementary views of the same sequential data [58]. This challenge of handling irregular, non-Euclidean data foreshadows the structural complexities encountered in graph-structured and point cloud domains, which are addressed in the following section.

### Benchmarks and Evaluation

The evaluation of self-supervised video representations has converged around a set of standard benchmarks. Action recognition datasets such as UCF101, HMDB51, and Kinetics are used to evaluate the quality of learned temporal and motion representations, typically through linear evaluation or fine-tuning protocols that mirror those used in image SSL. Video retrieval tasks assess the ability of self-supervised representations to retrieve semantically related clips from a large gallery, probing the global semantic content of the representations. These benchmarks collectively reveal that methods exploiting richer temporal augmentations and multi-scale temporal views tend to achieve stronger transfer performance, particularly on tasks requiring fine-grained temporal discrimination.

In summary, self-supervised learning for video and temporal data has matured into a rich subfield that leverages the inherent sequential structure of temporal data through diverse pretext tasks, contrastive objectives with temporally aware augmentation strategies, and audiovisual correspondence signals. The field continues to evolve toward methods that capture both fine-grained temporal dynamics and high-level semantic temporal structure, with promising directions including composable augmentation encodings, multi-resolution temporal contrasting, and hybrid generative-contrastive frameworks for diverse temporal modalities. As we move beyond temporal data, the next section examines how these SSL principles must be further adapted to accommodate the irregular topologies of graph-structured data and the unordered geometry of 3D point clouds.

### 3.6 Self-Supervised Learning for Graphs and Point Clouds

## 3.6 Self-Supervised Learning for Graphs and Point Clouds

Self-supervised representation learning for graph-structured data and 3D point clouds presents unique challenges compared to the image, video, and temporal domains discussed in the preceding section. Unlike Euclidean data such as video frames or time series signals, graphs and point clouds lack regular grid structures, making the design of meaningful augmentations and positive/negative pair construction significantly more complex. The absence of spatial regularity means that standard augmentation strategies such as random cropping or color jittering cannot be directly applied, necessitating domain-specific approaches that respect the underlying topology and geometry of the data. These structural considerations parallel the temporal-domain challenges encountered in video SSL, but require fundamentally different solutions owing to the non-Euclidean nature of the data.

For graph-structured data, a variety of augmentation strategies have been explored to generate contrastive views. Node-level augmentations include feature masking and perturbation, while edge-level augmentations involve dropping, adding, or rewiring edges to create structurally diverse views. These operations must be carefully designed to preserve semantically meaningful graph properties while introducing sufficient variability to encourage robust representation learning. Multi-scale subgraph contrastive learning approaches exploit the hierarchical nature of graphs by contrasting representations at different levels of granularity, from individual nodes to subgraph structures to the entire graph level. This multi-scale perspective allows models to capture both local structural patterns and global topological properties simultaneously, mirroring the multi-resolution temporal sampling strategies employed in video SSL.

Adversarial pooling strategies have also been explored for multi-scale view generation in graph SSL, where a differentiable pooling operation learns to identify informative subgraph regions that serve as challenging positive or negative pairs. Such adversarial approaches dynamically adapt the difficulty of the contrastive task during training, potentially yielding more discriminative representations. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA)-inspired methods, such as CCA-SSG, draw on classical statistical techniques to learn representations that maximize the cross-correlation between two augmented views of a graph, offering a non-contrastive alternative that avoids the need for explicit negative sampling and the associated computational overhead of large batch sizes or memory banks.

For 3D point cloud data, the challenge of constructing meaningful views is compounded by the unordered and irregular nature of point sets. Self-supervised methods for point clouds have explored pretext tasks such as point cloud reconstruction, normal estimation, relative position prediction, and completion of partially occluded scenes. Contrastive approaches for RGB-D scene understanding, such as P4Contrast, leverage the complementary nature of visual appearance (RGB) and geometric structure (depth) by contrasting point-pixel pairs across these two modalities. In P4Contrast, corresponding point cloud regions and image patches extracted from the same 3D location serve as positive pairs, while those from different locations form negatives, enabling the model to align appearance and geometry representations without manual annotation.

The unique challenges of constructing meaningful views for non-Euclidean data extend beyond augmentation design to encompass the definition of similarity and dissimilarity. In graphs, two augmented views of the same graph are natural positives, but the definition of negative samples is less straightforward since graphs from different classes may share structural motifs, and those from the same class may appear structurally distinct. Graph partition and community detection-based negatives, as well as cross-graph contrasting strategies, have been proposed to address this issue. Similarly, for point clouds, occlusion patterns and scanning artifacts introduce natural variability that can be exploited as self-supervised signal, but also create noise that may corrupt learned representations if not handled carefully.

Beyond unimodal graph and point cloud SSL, multimodal approaches that combine graph topology with node features or additional sensor streams have shown promise. For instance, in multivariate time series settings, graph-based contrastive learning exploits spatial relationships between sensors by constructing correlation or proximity graphs, with node and edge augmentations designed to preserve temporal dynamics while introducing structural variation [29]. This graph-aware contrasting enables the model to capture both temporal patterns within individual sensor channels and spatial dependencies across channels, forming a natural bridge to the time series SSL methods discussed in the following section.

The broader landscape of self-supervised learning across diverse data modalities underscores the need for principled augmentation strategies that respect the inherent structure of each data type [14]. For graphs and point clouds, this means going beyond naive perturbation toward augmentations that preserve semantic content while introducing sufficient invariance-promoting transformations. As will be seen in the following section, many of these same principles—domain-aware augmentation design, multi-scale consistency objectives, and careful construction of positive and negative pairs—resurface in the context of time series SSL, underscoring their generality across non-Euclidean and non-visual data modalities. Progress in this direction will be critical for extending the remarkable success of SSL from vision and language to graph-structured and geometric domains, with applications spanning molecular property prediction, social network analysis, 3D object recognition, and autonomous driving scene understanding.

### 3.7 Self-Supervised Learning for Time Series Data

## 3.7 Self-Supervised Learning for Time Series

Time series data presents unique challenges for self-supervised representation learning due to its inherent temporal dependencies, variable lengths, non-stationarity, and the need to preserve both local and global temporal structure when constructing augmented views. Much like the domain-specific augmentation strategies required for graphs and point clouds discussed in the previous subsection, defining semantically meaningful augmentations for time series requires careful consideration of the signal's temporal and frequency characteristics, rather than simply borrowing transformations from image or text domains.

A central challenge in designing self-supervised frameworks for time series is the construction of effective positive pairs that preserve task-relevant information while introducing sufficient diversity to drive representation learning. Naive augmentations such as random cropping, jittering, or scaling may inadvertently destroy important temporal patterns or introduce false positives that mislead contrastive objectives. This has motivated the development of domain-aware augmentation strategies that respect the structural properties of time series signals, echoing the need for principled, structure-preserving augmentations identified across all non-Euclidean and non-visual data modalities.

Multi-task contrastive frameworks have emerged as a promising paradigm for time series SSL, combining multiple consistency objectives to capture complementary aspects of temporal data. By jointly optimizing contextual consistency, which encourages representations of nearby temporal segments to be similar, temporal consistency, which aligns representations across different temporal scales, and transformation consistency, which enforces invariance to specific signal-level augmentations, these frameworks produce richer and more transferable representations than any single objective alone.

For multivariate time series, the spatial relationships among different sensor channels or variables constitute an additional source of structure that can be exploited for self-supervised learning. Graph-aware contrastive approaches model inter-variable dependencies using graph neural networks, constructing positive pairs that preserve both temporal coherence and spatial correlations across channels. This connection between graph-based SSL and time series SSL is natural: the graph structures described in Section 3.6 can serve as a principled scaffold for capturing inter-sensor dependencies in multivariate settings. Such approaches are particularly relevant for applications such as human activity recognition and physiological signal analysis, where the relationships between sensors or body locations carry important semantic information [63].

Neural process-based augmentation offers another direction for time series SSL, leveraging probabilistic generative models to produce diverse yet semantically consistent augmented views. By treating time series as realizations of underlying stochastic processes, neural process frameworks can generate augmented samples that respect the distributional properties of the original signal, providing richer supervision signals than deterministic transformations alone.

The downstream tasks targeted by time series SSL methods are considerably more diverse than those in vision or language, spanning classification, forecasting, and anomaly detection, each of which places different demands on the learned representations. Classification tasks benefit from representations that capture discriminative patterns across the entire time series, while forecasting requires representations that encode temporal dynamics and future-predictive structure. Anomaly detection, in contrast, depends on representations that accurately model the normal data manifold so that deviations can be reliably identified. This diversity of downstream tasks stands in contrast to graph and point cloud SSL, where node classification, graph classification, and 3D object recognition are the primary targets, and underscores the importance of designing pretext tasks that are aligned with specific application requirements.

Constructing hard negative pairs is particularly challenging for time series, as temporally adjacent segments are often highly correlated and may represent the same underlying state or event. Techniques such as double Universum-based hard negative construction and feature-aware negative sampling have been proposed to address this issue, ensuring that negative pairs are semantically distinct without being trivially dissimilar. The evaluation of self-supervised time series representations often reveals that methods that perform well on classification do not necessarily transfer to forecasting or anomaly detection, highlighting the importance of designing pretext tasks and augmentation strategies that are aligned with the specific requirements of the target downstream application.

Overall, the field of self-supervised learning for time series is rapidly maturing, with growing recognition that domain-specific augmentation design, multi-task consistency objectives, and careful attention to the structure of positive and negative pairs are essential ingredients for learning representations that generalize across the diverse range of real-world time series applications. These themes resonate strongly with the broader principles observed across graph, point cloud, text, audio, and tabular domains, and collectively motivate the development of unified SSL frameworks capable of operating effectively across heterogeneous data modalities, as discussed in the following subsection.

### 3.8 Self-Supervised Learning for Text, Audio, and Tabular Data

## 3.8 Self-Supervised Learning for Text, Audio, and Tabular Data

Self-supervised representation learning has been successfully extended beyond visual domains to encompass text, audio, and tabular data, each presenting unique challenges and opportunities for designing effective pretext tasks and multi-view learning frameworks. Just as the previous subsection highlighted the need for domain-aware augmentation strategies tailored to the temporal and structural properties of time series, the modalities discussed here similarly demand careful adaptation of view construction and consistency objectives to their respective structural characteristics.

**Text Representations.** In the natural language domain, self-supervised learning has a rich history rooted in masked language modeling and next-token prediction objectives. Beyond these foundational approaches, multi-view sentence representation learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm that exploits distributional and architectural asymmetry to construct diverse views of textual data. By treating different sentence encodings—obtained through dropout masks, token deletion, or paraphrasing—as positive pairs, contrastive objectives can be applied to learn semantically rich sentence embeddings without any labeled data. The diversity of views is critical: views that are too similar fail to provide meaningful learning signal, while views that are too dissimilar risk losing semantic coherence. Distributional asymmetry, where one view is drawn from a broader context and another from a narrower representation, has been shown to improve the discriminability and generalization of the resulting embeddings across downstream natural language understanding tasks. These considerations directly echo the challenges of constructing informative positive pairs discussed in the context of time series and graphs, where semantic consistency must be carefully balanced against augmentation diversity.

**Audio Representations.** Self-supervised learning for audio and music poses distinct challenges compared to vision and text, as temporal structure, frequency content, and acoustic characteristics must all be preserved in meaningful representations. Contrastive learning frameworks adapted for audio leverage frame-level masking strategies, where positive pairs are constructed from temporally proximate frames of the same audio clip and negative pairs from frames belonging to different recordings or distant temporal positions. This positive-negative frame masking approach encourages the model to learn representations that are locally consistent while remaining discriminative across different audio sources. For music representation in particular, such methods must capture both short-term timbral characteristics and longer-term structural patterns, motivating the use of multi-scale temporal contrasting—a theme closely related to the multi-scale temporal consistency objectives explored for time series data in the previous subsection. Self-supervised audio models have demonstrated strong transferability to downstream tasks including music genre classification, instrument recognition, and speaker identification, often matching or surpassing supervised baselines with limited labeled data.

**Tabular Data Representations.** Tabular data presents a fundamentally different challenge for self-supervised learning due to the heterogeneous nature of features, the absence of spatial or temporal locality, and the difficulty of defining semantically meaningful augmentations. Unlike time series or graph data, where the topology and temporal ordering provide natural structure for augmentation design, tabular data lacks an inherent relational or sequential structure. The SubTab framework addresses these challenges by decomposing the feature space into multiple subsets, thereby creating a multi-view learning problem from tabular data without requiring domain-specific knowledge. By training an encoder to produce consistent representations across different feature subsets of the same sample, SubTab encourages the model to learn robust, complementary representations that capture the underlying data manifold. This feature subsetting strategy serves as a general-purpose augmentation mechanism for tabular data, avoiding the pitfalls of naive noise injection or feature masking that may destroy important statistical relationships between features.

**Generic SSL Frameworks Across Modalities.** A broader class of self-supervised methods seeks to transcend modality-specific design choices by employing self-transformation mechanisms that are applicable across diverse data types. Rather than relying on hand-crafted domain-specific augmentations, these frameworks learn or parameterize the transformation process itself, enabling the discovery of invariances directly from data. Such generic approaches are particularly valuable for heterogeneous and scientific data modalities where expert-designed augmentations are unavailable or costly to specify. By combining self-transformation with contrastive or predictive objectives, these frameworks provide a unified pathway toward modality-agnostic self-supervised pretraining, supporting transfer to a wide range of downstream tasks across text, audio, tabular, and other specialized domains.

Together, these developments across text, audio, and tabular modalities demonstrate that the core principles of multi-view learning and self-supervised pretraining are broadly applicable, provided that the view construction and augmentation strategies are carefully adapted to the structural properties and semantic requirements of each data type. This adaptability of SSL principles across such heterogeneous modalities naturally raises the question of how positive and negative sample pairs can be constructed most effectively across all these settings—a fundamental challenge that cuts across every domain discussed so far, and which is examined in depth in the following subsection.

### 3.9 Constructing Effective Positive and Negative Pairs Across Modalities

Constructing effective positive and negative sample pairs is a foundational challenge in self-supervised contrastive learning, as the quality and semantic consistency of these pairs directly determines the richness of learned representations. Building upon the multi-view learning principles discussed in the context of text, audio, and tabular data, researchers have developed increasingly sophisticated pair construction strategies that go beyond naive augmentation-based approaches to address issues such as false negatives, semantic ambiguity, feature suppression, and modality-specific structural properties.

**Positive Pair Construction and Semantic Consistency**

The most basic approach to constructing positive pairs involves applying different stochastic augmentations to the same data instance and treating the resulting views as semantically equivalent. However, this assumption can be violated when augmentations introduce significant semantic distortion. For example, random cropping in visual data may produce two crops with entirely distinct semantic content, leading to degraded representation learning. Frameworks such as LeOCLR address this by enforcing that the shared region between positive crops is semantically correct, using an adapted loss function to ensure semantic consistency in positive pairs [116]. Similarly, in the context of sentence representation learning, standard augmentation techniques frequently introduce semantic margin between ostensibly positive pairs, and methods like IFTCL propose a Twins Loss that explicitly preserves the innate semantic margin during training rather than blindly maximizing similarity between all positive pairs [117].

**Hard Negative Construction and Mining**

While positive pair quality is critical, the construction of informative negative samples is equally important for learning discriminative representations. Contrastive learning frameworks rely on negatives to provide the contrasting signal that prevents collapse, and harder negatives—those semantically close to the anchor but from distinct categories—have been shown to yield stronger representations. Several strategies have been proposed to systematically construct or mine such hard negatives. Synthetic hard negative generation, for instance, involves mixing negative samples at the feature level to create more challenging contrasts, combined with debiasing mechanisms to reduce the effect of false negatives that may arise from mixing semantically similar samples [118]. Max-margin contrastive learning takes inspiration from support vector machines, selecting negatives as sparse support vectors obtained through a quadratic optimization problem so that contrastiveness is enforced by maximizing the decision margin, leading to better convergence properties with fewer negatives [119].

**Feature-Aware and Stage-Wise Negative Sampling**

A particularly important limitation of standard contrastive learning is feature suppression, whereby the trained encoder captures only a limited subset of the input's information while ignoring other potentially valuable content. This leads to indistinguishable representations for visually similar but semantically different inputs. The Multistage Contrastive Learning (MCL) framework directly addresses this by employing feature-aware negative sampling, where at each successive stage, negatives are exclusively selected from the cluster to which the anchor was assigned in preceding stages [120]. This progressive strategy ensures that previously unlearned features are progressively captured across stages, while cross-stage representation integration preserves already-learned features. Such a multi-stage negative sampling approach demonstrates that the choice of which samples serve as negatives can be made dynamically and adaptively, guided by the current state of the learned representation space.

**False Negative Mitigation**

A persistent challenge in contrastive learning is the presence of false negatives—samples from the same semantic class as the anchor that are incorrectly treated as negatives, thereby introducing conflicting gradients into the training signal. This problem is particularly acute in large-scale datasets where the probability of sampling semantically related instances as negatives is non-trivial. In time-series contrastive learning, theoretical analyses have revealed that standard InfoNCE-based frameworks suffer from both false negatives and class imbalance, and addressing these issues via instance graphs that facilitate interactive learning among instances can significantly mitigate their impact [121]. Positive-unlabeled contrastive learning (PUCL) offers a principled approach by treating generated negative samples as unlabeled samples and using information from positive samples to correct bias in the contrastive loss, proving that this correction incurs only negligible bias compared to an unbiased loss [122]. From a different angle, the Siamese Prototypical Contrastive Learning framework mitigates false negatives by employing siamese-style metric loss to match intra-prototype features while increasing distance between inter-prototype features, reducing the harmful effect of semantically similar negatives being pushed apart [123].

**Adaptive and Kernel-Based Pair Weighting**

Beyond binary categorization of samples as positives or negatives, several works have explored adaptive weighting schemes that account for the varying degree of semantic similarity or augmentation strength across pairs. The CACR framework proposes a doubly contrastive strategy that separately compares positive and negative samples within their own groups before contrasting between groups, making the query attract more distant positives with greater force while repelling closer negatives more strongly [124]. Score-matching functions have been used to measure how much a sample has changed from the original through augmentation, allowing every pair to be weighted adaptively by the magnitude of the augmentation-induced change, with empirical demonstrations of consistent improvements across multiple contrastive learning methods [125]. In multi-head contrastive learning, adaptive temperatures are assigned per projection head and regularized to prevent degenerate solutions, allowing the model to handle varying intra-sample similarity from different augmentation strategies more effectively [126].

**Modality-Specific Pair Construction Challenges**

Constructing meaningful positive and negative pairs becomes considerably more complex when moving beyond visual data to modalities such as audio, speech, text, graphs, and time series—echoing the unique structural challenges encountered in the domain-specific self-supervised frameworks discussed previously. In speaker verification, for instance, the assumption that positive pairs differ only in speaker identity is violated because speech signals also contain linguistic content, and frameworks incorporating sequential disentanglement via variational autoencoders address this by removing linguistic content before constructing the contrastive objective [127]. For multi-label text classification, the standard binary positive-negative dichotomy is insufficient because different label combinations imply varying degrees of semantic relatedness, motivating the development of specialized contrastive losses such as Jaccard Similarity Contrastive Loss that account for partial label overlap when defining pair relationships [128]. In image-text retrieval, cross-modal pair construction is complicated by the need to align representations across fundamentally different modalities, and frameworks like CODER introduce dynamic dictionaries for both modalities with diversity-sensitive contrastive learning that achieves adaptive negative pair weighting to account for the varying informativeness of different cross-modal negative pairs [129].

**Decoupling Negative Sample Size from Batch Size**

A practical constraint in many contrastive frameworks is that the number of negative samples is tightly coupled to the mini-batch size, limiting the contrast available in memory-efficient settings. Momentum-based queue dictionaries as in MoCo partially alleviate this by maintaining a large pool of encoded representations across batches. Fine-grained analysis of queue size and consistency in MoCo reveals that the InfoNCE loss implicitly attracts anchors to positives with varying penalty strengths depending on the difficulty of other anchors in the batch, a property termed inter-anchor hardness-awareness, which motivates the use of dual temperature schemes to achieve competitive performance without large dictionaries [130]. Decoupled contrastive learning (DCL) identifies a negative-positive-coupling (NPC) effect in the standard InfoNCE loss that leads to unsuitable learning efficiency with respect to batch size, and removes the positive term from the denominator, achieving competitive performance with much smaller batches [131]. Similarly, the EqCo framework establishes equivalent rules for contrastive learning by adaptively scaling the margin term according to the number of negative pairs, enabling training with as few as 16 negatives per query on large-scale datasets without significant accuracy degradation [132].

**Incremental and Non-Stationary Settings**

When contrastive learning is deployed in incremental or continual settings where new data arrives sequentially, the distribution of negative samples changes over time, creating bias in standard InfoNCE-based objectives. Incremental contrastive learning frameworks address this by estimating the change in noise distribution for old data and incorporating this estimate into a modified InfoNCE loss that remains statistically equivalent to full retraining, enabling unbiased incremental updates with significant computational savings [133].

**Theoretical Perspectives on Pair Construction**

From a theoretical standpoint, the relationship between the quality of positive and negative pair construction and downstream classification performance has been formalized through surrogate gap analyses. Studies have established that the contrastive loss serves as a surrogate objective for the downstream classification loss, and that larger negative sample sizes improve downstream performance because the surrogate gap between the contrastive and supervised losses decays with more negatives [134]. This theoretical foundation provides principled guidance for the design of negative sampling strategies, suggesting that the diversity and hardness of negatives, rather than sheer quantity alone, are the key factors determining downstream generalization. Furthermore, analyses of neural collapse geometry reveal that hard negative sampling in both supervised and unsupervised contrastive learning drives representations toward the equiangular tight frame structure associated with neural collapse, and that incorporating hard negatives alongside feature normalization is critical for avoiding dimensional collapse [135].

In summary, the construction of effective positive and negative pairs is a multifaceted problem that intersects data augmentation design, semantic consistency enforcement, false negative correction, adaptive weighting, cross-modal alignment, and theoretical analysis of surrogate objectives. As the field continues to extend self-supervised contrastive learning to increasingly diverse and complex data modalities, progress across all these dimensions remains essential for advancing the quality and generalizability of learned representations.

## 4 Theoretical Understanding and Analysis of Self-Supervised Learning

### 4.1 Information-Theoretic Frameworks and Mutual Information Maximization

Information-theoretic frameworks provide a principled foundation for understanding why self-supervised learning (SSL) methods work and how to design more effective objectives. At the core of these frameworks lies the principle of mutual information (MI) maximization: by maximizing the mutual information between representations derived from different views or augmentations of the same underlying data, a model is encouraged to capture the shared, semantically meaningful content while discarding view-specific noise and irrelevant variation. This information-theoretic perspective complements the probabilistic and generative viewpoints discussed later, and together they form a cohesive theoretical foundation for modern SSL.

**Mutual Information Maximization.** The mutual information maximization perspective posits that a good representation should retain as much task-relevant information as possible from the input while compressing away nuisances. Formally, given two views $v_1$ and $v_2$ generated from a common source $x$ through stochastic augmentation, the representation learning objective can be written as maximizing $I(z_1; z_2)$, the mutual information between the encoded views. This principle underlies many contrastive methods: the InfoNCE loss, used in frameworks such as SimCLR and MoCo, can be interpreted as a lower bound on the mutual information between paired representations [14]. By optimizing this bound, contrastive methods effectively learn to align representations of positive pairs (augmented views of the same instance) while separating those of negative pairs, thereby maximizing a tractable surrogate for MI.

**Multi-View Redundancy and Task-Relevant Information.** A central concept in the information-theoretic analysis of SSL is the role of multi-view redundancy. When multiple views of an input are constructed—whether through spatial cropping, color jitter, temporal sampling in video, or cross-modal pairing—the shared information across views tends to correspond to high-level semantic content that is invariant to the specific augmentation applied [14; 3]. The principle is that by training a model to predict one view from another, or to produce consistent representations across views, it is implicitly forced to extract the redundant (shared) information between them. Theoretical analyses show that when augmentations are chosen such that the shared information between views corresponds closely to the information relevant for downstream tasks, the representations learned by MI maximization will generalize well to those tasks [15].

**Partial Information Decomposition.** A more refined information-theoretic lens is provided by partial information decomposition (PID), which decomposes the total information that a set of views provides about a target variable into components: redundant information (carried by all views), unique information (carried by only one view), and synergistic information (only available when all views are considered jointly). In the context of SSL, the redundant component corresponds to what contrastive objectives capture—the information shared across all constructed views. This decomposition clarifies an important limitation of standard multi-view contrastive learning: it may fail to capture synergistic information that is only accessible by jointly considering all views, and may discard unique information present in individual views that is nonetheless relevant for downstream tasks. This motivates the design of objectives that explicitly balance the trade-off between capturing redundant and synergistic components, a direction with implications for multi-modal self-supervised learning [14].

**Unifying Contrastive and Predictive Objectives.** The information-theoretic perspective offers a powerful lens for unifying seemingly disparate SSL objectives. Contrastive methods, which maximize agreement between positive pairs and minimize it for negative pairs, and predictive methods, which train a model to forecast masked or missing portions of the input, can both be cast as instances of MI maximization between different portions or views of the data [1; 15]. Predictive coding objectives, for example, maximize the MI between a context representation and future observations; masked language modeling maximizes the MI between context tokens and the masked token distribution. By framing both families under the common umbrella of maximizing lower bounds on MI, the theory provides not only a unified conceptual account of why these methods work, but also suggests principled criteria for evaluating and improving pretext task design: a pretext task is informative to the extent that the MI it maximizes aligns with the MI relevant for the downstream task. This unification sets the stage for the subsequent discussion of how generative and probabilistic latent variable models further bridge these paradigms under a common theoretical language.

**Implications for Augmentation Design.** The information-theoretic framework also illuminates the critical role of data augmentation. The choice of augmentation directly determines the shared information between views and therefore what the model learns to capture. If augmentations are too weak, the two views remain nearly identical and the MI objective is trivially satisfied without learning meaningful abstractions. If augmentations are too aggressive, the shared information may no longer include task-relevant semantics, leading to poor downstream performance [14; 35]. This analysis explains empirical findings that the precise choice and composition of augmentation strategies profoundly influences the quality of learned representations, and motivates information-theoretic criteria for augmentation selection.

**Limitations and Extensions.** Despite its elegance, the MI maximization framework faces practical challenges. Direct computation of MI in high-dimensional spaces is intractable, necessitating the use of variational bounds (e.g., InfoNCE) that may be loose or require large batch sizes with many negatives. Moreover, maximizing MI alone does not guarantee useful representations if the views share spurious correlations or if the downstream task requires information that is not captured by any pair of views [14]. Extensions of the framework, including minimum sufficient statistics perspectives, rate-distortion theory, and the information bottleneck principle, have been proposed to address these limitations by explicitly regularizing the amount of information retained in the representation and focusing it on task-relevant content. These limitations and extensions naturally motivate a complementary probabilistic treatment, wherein SSL objectives are grounded in explicit generative models and approximate inference procedures—a perspective developed in the following subsection. Together, these information-theoretic foundations continue to guide both the theoretical understanding and practical design of self-supervised representation learning systems.

### 4.2 Probabilistic and Generative Latent Variable Models

Probabilistic and generative latent variable models offer a principled theoretical lens through which the mechanisms of self-supervised learning can be understood from first principles. Rather than treating SSL objectives as heuristically motivated engineering choices, these frameworks interpret representation learning as approximate inference in a generative model, where the encoder learns to infer a compact latent code that explains the observed data. Building directly upon the information-theoretic foundations established in the previous section—where SSL was characterized through mutual information maximization and view redundancy—the probabilistic perspective deepens this understanding by grounding diverse SSL paradigms within a unified language of probabilistic inference and generative modeling. This perspective connects contrastive, clustering-based, and masked modeling methods, setting the stage for the kernel-based and spectral analyses that follow by establishing the structural properties of the latent spaces these methods produce.

**Variational Autoencoders and Generative Foundations**

The variational autoencoder (VAE) framework provides a natural starting point for understanding generative approaches to representation learning. By optimizing the evidence lower bound (ELBO), VAEs learn a probabilistic encoder that maps inputs to distributions over latent variables, and a decoder that reconstructs the input from samples of those latent variables. This generative perspective naturally motivates many self-supervised objectives: masked autoencoders, for instance, can be understood as learning to complete partially observed data under a generative model, where the masked patches serve as missing variables to be inferred from visible context [24]. The connection to denoising autoencoders is particularly illuminating, as denoising objectives can be derived from the score function of the data distribution, providing theoretical grounding for reconstruction-based SSL methods.

**Latent Variable Models for Contrastive Learning**

Contrastive learning methods have been interpreted through the lens of latent variable models, where the data augmentation process defines a generative process over views. Under this framework, two augmented views of the same image are treated as conditionally independent observations of a shared latent semantic variable. The contrastive objective then approximates maximum likelihood estimation in this generative model, encouraging the encoder to recover the shared latent factor while discarding view-specific nuisances. This formulation connects contrastive SSL to classical latent variable models such as factor analysis and probabilistic PCA, and provides theoretical grounding for why contrastive representations transfer well to downstream tasks: the learned latent code captures the semantically invariant structure shared across views [25].

The probabilistic interpretation also sheds light on the role of negative samples. In the generative view, negative pairs correspond to samples from different latent classes, and the contrastive loss approximates the log-likelihood ratio between the positive and negative generative models. This connects the InfoNCE objective to importance-weighted bounds on mutual information and to energy-based models, where the unnormalized density is parameterized by the dot product between query and key representations. Crucially, this probabilistic interpretation complements the information-theoretic characterization of InfoNCE as a lower bound on mutual information discussed in the previous section, offering a generative model perspective on the same objective.

**Clustering-Based Methods and Mixture Models**

Clustering-based SSL methods such as prototype assignment and online clustering find natural probabilistic interpretations as Expectation-Maximization (EM) in Gaussian mixture models or categorical latent variable models. The cluster assignment step corresponds to the E-step, inferring which prototype a data point belongs to, while the representation update corresponds to the M-step, optimizing the model parameters to maximize the expected log-likelihood. This connection motivates Bayesian unifications that treat cluster assignments as latent variables marginalized over during training, providing theoretical justification for strategies that prevent cluster collapse and ensure balanced assignments.

**Probabilistic Frameworks for Multi-View Learning**

Multi-view generative models provide a principled framework for understanding how information is shared and transformed across different views or modalities. Under a shared latent variable model, each view is generated by passing the shared latent code through a view-specific decoder. The SSL objective then corresponds to maximizing the marginal likelihood of observed views, which can be lower bounded by objectives that encourage alignment between view-specific encoders. This perspective has been formalized through multi-view extensions of VAEs and through probabilistic canonical correlation analysis, which decompose the latent space into shared and view-specific components [29]. Such decompositions motivate factorized representation learning, where the encoder is encouraged to separately capture information shared across modalities and information unique to each modality. Notably, this shared-versus-unique decomposition of the latent space mirrors the redundant-versus-unique distinction from the partial information decomposition framework discussed in the previous section, illustrating the deep complementarity between information-theoretic and probabilistic perspectives.

**Generative Models and Augmentation**

Recent work has explored how generative models can be integrated into the SSL pipeline not only as theoretical tools but also as practical augmentation mechanisms. By conditioning generative models on source image representations, it becomes possible to sample diverse augmentations that maintain semantic consistency with the original image, effectively expanding the set of positive pairs available for contrastive training [111]. This approach is grounded in the generative view of augmentation: the augmentation process defines a probabilistic graphical model where the latent semantic variable causes both the original image and its augmented variants, and learning to be invariant to augmentations is equivalent to inferring the causal latent variable. This generative characterization of augmentation provides a probabilistic counterpart to the information-theoretic analysis of augmentation design discussed previously, reinforcing the principle that effective augmentations are those that preserve task-relevant semantic information in the shared latent structure.

**Connections to Speech and Discrete Latent Models**

In the speech domain, probabilistic latent variable models have a long history through hidden Markov models and Gaussian mixture model-based acoustic models. Modern self-supervised speech representation learning methods can be understood as learning continuous relaxations of these discrete latent structures, where the quantization step in models like VQ-VAE or wav2vec 2.0 approximates the discrete latent variable inference step [27; 136]. The connection to discrete latent variable models motivates the use of commitment losses and codebook learning as mechanisms for stable representation learning, and provides theoretical grounding for why discrete bottlenecks can improve downstream task performance by enforcing a compressed, information-theoretically compact representation.

**Theoretical Implications for Downstream Performance**

A key contribution of probabilistic generative frameworks is the theoretical grounding they provide for the relationship between pretraining objectives and downstream task performance. Under the latent variable model view, a well-trained SSL encoder recovers the posterior distribution over the shared semantic latent variable, which is precisely the information needed for any downstream task that depends on that variable. This provides a theoretical basis for the widely observed empirical phenomenon that better contrastive or generative pretraining leads to better linear separability and transfer accuracy. Furthermore, probabilistic frameworks naturally accommodate uncertainty quantification, enabling SSL encoders to express calibrated uncertainty about their representations, which is valuable in safety-critical downstream applications.

Overall, probabilistic and generative latent variable models provide a theoretically rigorous and conceptually unifying framework for understanding self-supervised learning. By grounding diverse SSL objectives in the language of probabilistic inference and generative modeling, these frameworks illuminate the common structure underlying contrastive, clustering-based, and masked modeling methods, and provide principled guidance for the design of new SSL algorithms with provable properties. The latent spaces they characterize—with their shared, factorized, and discrete structures—also provide the geometric objects that kernel-based and spectral analyses in the following section will examine through the complementary lens of functional analysis and spectral decomposition.

### 4.3 Kernel-Based Analyses and Spectral Perspectives

Kernel-based analyses and spectral perspectives provide powerful mathematical lenses through which the behavior and theoretical guarantees of self-supervised learning methods can be rigorously understood. Building naturally upon the probabilistic and generative frameworks discussed in the previous section, these approaches draw on classical results in functional analysis, spectral graph theory, and kernel methods to illuminate why SSL objectives produce useful representations and under what conditions they achieve near-optimal downstream performance. Where probabilistic frameworks interpret SSL through the language of latent variable inference, kernel-based and spectral frameworks characterize the geometric and algebraic structure of the learned representation space itself.

A central line of work connects contrastive learning objectives to classical spectral embedding techniques. The NT-Xent and InfoNCE losses, when analyzed in the kernel regime, can be interpreted as learning a kernel function that captures the similarity structure induced by the augmentation distribution. This connection reveals that contrastive methods implicitly perform a form of spectral decomposition on a data similarity graph, where positive pairs define edges and the learned representations correspond to the leading eigenvectors of the associated graph Laplacian. Such connections link SSL directly to classical manifold learning methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps and Multidimensional Scaling, providing a unified theoretical language for understanding both families of methods.

The multi-view perspective on SSL further enriches the kernel-based analysis. When each data point is associated with two views generated by stochastic augmentations, the kernel defined by the expected inner product between view representations captures the statistical dependence structure across views. Theoretical results in this setting establish that linear functions of the learned representations achieve near-optimal downstream performance on prediction tasks whenever the two views provide redundant information about the label [36]. This result, derived in the linear and kernel regimes, provides a formal justification for the empirical success of contrastive SSL and clarifies the role of augmentation design in shaping the downstream-relevant structure of the representation space.

Representer theorems offer another angle on kernel-based SSL analysis. In the kernel regime, the optimal SSL encoder can be shown to lie in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) determined by the architecture and the pretext task loss, analogously to the classical representer theorem in supervised kernel methods. This perspective enables the derivation of closed-form solutions for the optimal representation in simplified settings, yielding analytical insights into the geometry of the learned embedding space and the influence of regularization on its structure.

The spectral perspective also illuminates the collapse problem in SSL—a theoretical concern that will be examined in greater depth in the following section. Dimensional collapse, in which the learned representations span only a low-dimensional subspace of the embedding space, corresponds to the dominance of a small number of eigenmodes in the learned kernel matrix. Variance-covariance regularization methods such as Barlow Twins and VICReg can be interpreted as enforcing that the eigenspectrum of the feature covariance matrix remains flat, preventing the concentration of representational capacity in a few directions. This spectral interpretation connects non-contrastive SSL objectives to classical whitening and decorrelation procedures, providing a unified framework for understanding how different SSL methods avoid degenerate solutions—a theme that the next section will revisit from the perspective of loss landscape geometry and training dynamics.

Information-theoretic analyses complement the spectral perspective by connecting the mutual information maximization principle underlying many SSL methods to the spectral properties of the data distribution. The mutual information between two views of a data point can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues of a cross-covariance operator between the view-conditional distributions, and maximizing this quantity over representations corresponds to retaining the leading eigenfunctions of this operator. This connection rigorously grounds the multi-view SSL framework in spectral functional analysis and yields conditions under which the learned representations are sufficient statistics for downstream prediction.

Taken together, kernel-based and spectral analyses provide a mathematically grounded foundation for understanding self-supervised representation learning. They reveal deep connections between modern SSL objectives and classical methods in manifold learning and spectral graph theory, establish formal optimality guarantees for learned representations in tractable regimes, and offer principled explanations for the effectiveness of various design choices including augmentation strategies, regularization objectives, and architectural components. These theoretical insights lay the groundwork for the subsequent analysis of learning dynamics and representational collapse, where the spectral properties of the embedding space become directly relevant to understanding training stability and convergence behavior.

### 4.4 Learning Dynamics and Representation Collapse Theory

## 4.4 Learning Dynamics and Representation Collapse Theory

One of the most fundamental challenges in self-supervised learning is understanding and preventing representational collapse—the degenerate phenomenon where the encoder maps all inputs to identical or near-identical representations, rendering the learned features useless for downstream tasks. Building upon the spectral and kernel-based perspectives developed in the previous section, which established how dimensional collapse corresponds to the dominance of a small number of eigenmodes in the learned kernel matrix, this section examines the complementary perspective of loss landscape geometry, gradient dynamics, normalization mechanisms, and architectural constraints that together govern the stability and expressivity of learned representations. The theoretical insights developed here will in turn directly inform the generalization and transfer analysis that follows, where collapse avoidance is shown to be a prerequisite for the representational richness that underpins downstream performance.

**Complete and Dimensional Collapse**

Representational collapse manifests in two principal forms. *Complete collapse* occurs when the encoder produces a constant output regardless of input, typically because a trivial solution satisfies the self-supervised objective with zero loss. *Dimensional collapse*, a subtler and more insidious failure mode, occurs when the learned representations span only a low-dimensional subspace of the embedding space, even if individual samples are not mapped to identical points. Dimensional collapse implies that the effective rank of the representation matrix is far lower than the ambient dimensionality, resulting in redundant and poorly discriminative features. As foreshadowed in the spectral analysis of the previous section, this failure mode corresponds precisely to an ill-conditioned eigenspectrum of the feature covariance matrix—a connection that motivates many of the collapse-prevention mechanisms examined below.

In contrastive learning frameworks such as SimCLR and MoCo, complete collapse is explicitly prevented by the use of negative pairs: the InfoNCE and NT-Xent objectives penalize representations that are too similar across non-augmented views of distinct samples. The presence of negative samples effectively introduces a repulsive force in the embedding space, preventing all representations from collapsing to a single point. However, even with negatives, dimensional collapse can persist if the objective does not sufficiently encourage utilization of all embedding dimensions.

Non-contrastive methods such as BYOL and SimSiam, which operate entirely without negative pairs, face a particularly severe risk of complete collapse. These methods employ asymmetric architectural designs—predictor networks and stop-gradient operations—to prevent collapse without negative samples. The theoretical understanding of why these mechanisms work remains an active area of inquiry. Several analyses suggest that the predictor network introduces an implicit regularization that biases the optimization toward non-collapsed solutions, while the stop-gradient operation breaks the symmetry that would otherwise drive both networks to identical degenerate outputs. The interplay between predictor network capacity, learning rate schedules, and the exponential moving average (EMA) update in momentum-based encoders determines whether the training dynamics converge to a useful or degenerate fixed point.

**Loss Landscape Geometry and Optimization Dynamics**

The geometry of the loss landscape plays a critical role in determining the learning dynamics of SSL models. For contrastive objectives, the loss landscape contains a structured set of saddle points corresponding to partially collapsed solutions, where some but not all dimensions of the embedding space are utilized. Gradient descent dynamics on this landscape can be analyzed through the lens of spectral properties of the Gram matrix of representations: convergence to a full-rank solution corresponds to gradient flow toward a global minimum that enforces uniform spreading of representations across the hypersphere.

Redundancy-reduction methods such as Barlow Twins and VICReg directly operate on the cross-correlation or covariance matrix of representations, penalizing off-diagonal entries to decorrelate features and penalizing variance collapse to ensure each dimension carries informative signal. As noted in the previous section, these objectives are closely related to classical whitening and decorrelation procedures; here, the loss landscape perspective provides a complementary explanation for why variance-covariance regularization prevents dimensional collapse. The loss landscape of Barlow Twins, when analyzed in the linear regime, admits closed-form solutions corresponding to orthonormal projections of the data, with the global minimum achieving perfect decorrelation and uniform variance across all embedding dimensions—precisely the flat eigenspectrum condition identified through the spectral lens.

**The Role of Normalization and Bias**

Batch normalization (BN) plays a subtle but critical role in preventing collapse in many SSL frameworks. In SimCLR, the application of BN within the projection head prevents representations from saturating at extreme values, which would otherwise cause gradient vanishing and impede optimization. More importantly, BN introduces a form of implicit contrastive signal by normalizing representations within a batch: because BN computes statistics across the batch, the normalized representations of different samples within a batch are implicitly compared, preventing all samples from converging to the same pre-normalization value. This observation suggests that BN functions as a soft negative sampling mechanism, explaining in part why methods using BN are more robust to collapse than those without.

The role of bias terms and weight initialization in shaping early training dynamics is also relevant. In the initial phases of training, the loss landscape is largely determined by the random initialization, and the gradient directions are strongly influenced by the spectral structure of the initial weight matrices. Orthogonal initialization of weight matrices has been shown to provide better coverage of the embedding space in early training, reducing the risk of early-stage dimensional collapse before the SSL objective has had sufficient opportunity to shape the representations.

**Orthogonality Constraints and Manifold Dynamics**

Several theoretical analyses frame the collapse problem in terms of optimization on manifolds, specifically the Stiefel manifold of matrices with orthonormal columns. Methods that explicitly enforce orthogonality constraints on weight matrices or representation matrices can be understood as performing gradient descent on this manifold, with the SSL objective defining the objective function and the orthogonality constraint defining the feasible set. The geometry of the Stiefel manifold—including its curvature, geodesics, and retraction operations—determines the efficiency and convergence behavior of these constrained optimization procedures.

From a different perspective, the SSL training dynamics can be analyzed through the lens of neural tangent kernel (NTK) theory in the infinite-width limit. Under the NTK regime, the representations evolve according to a linear differential equation determined by the initial kernel, and the conditions for collapse can be expressed as spectral conditions on this kernel. While the NTK approximation may not hold exactly in practical finite-width networks, the insights derived from this analysis—particularly regarding the relationship between network depth, width, and collapse stability—provide useful theoretical guidance for architecture design, and connect naturally to the kernel-based theoretical framework developed in the preceding section.

**Theoretical Explanations for Collapse Avoidance**

Different SSL paradigms employ distinct mechanisms to avoid collapse, and a unified theoretical account of these mechanisms remains an open challenge. For contrastive methods, the repulsion provided by negative pairs can be formalized as a log-sum-exp regularizer on the similarity matrix, which has a well-characterized effect on the optimization landscape. For clustering-based methods such as SwAV, the use of online cluster assignments as pseudo-labels introduces a discrete bottleneck that prevents continuous collapse: if all representations collapsed to a single point, all samples would receive the same cluster assignment, which would be penalized by the uniform assignment constraint enforced via the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm.

For self-distillation methods like DINO, the teacher-student dynamics introduce a form of momentum-based regularization that stabilizes training: the slowly-evolving teacher provides a stable target that prevents the student from collapsing, while the student's updates are absorbed into the teacher through EMA, gradually improving the quality of the target representations. The stability of this feedback loop depends critically on the EMA decay rate and the learning rate schedule, with theoretical analyses identifying phase transitions between stable learning and collapse as these hyperparameters are varied.

The projection head also plays a nontrivial role in collapse dynamics [40]. Theoretical analysis reveals that the projection head, by learning to project representations into a lower-dimensional space, absorbs the burden of satisfying the SSL invariance objective, thereby protecting the encoder from collapsing to low-dimensional solutions [41]. The progressive feature weighting induced by training dynamics in deeper layers of the encoder—where features become increasingly unequal—means that the encoder's lower layers retain more normalized and less specialized representations, which are more robust to collapse [40]. This theoretical understanding motivates the empirical practice of discarding the projection head after pretraining and using the encoder representations for downstream tasks [42], a practice whose implications for transfer performance will be examined in the following section.

**Connections to Classical Stability Theory**

The collapse problem in SSL can also be analyzed through the lens of classical dynamical systems theory. The training process can be modeled as a continuous-time dynamical system on the space of network parameters, with fixed points corresponding to potential solutions—both collapsed and non-collapsed. The stability of these fixed points under gradient flow determines which solutions are reachable from typical initializations. Non-collapsed solutions correspond to saddle points or local minima with positive curvature in all relevant directions, while collapsed solutions may correspond to global minima of certain trivial objectives or saddle points with negative curvature in collapse directions.

Understanding the transition between stable learning and collapse as a function of architecture, objective, and hyperparameters is essential for principled SSL design, and has direct consequences for the generalization and transfer properties examined in the next section. The representations that survive collapse avoidance—those that span the full embedding space with well-conditioned geometry—are precisely the representations that transfer most effectively to downstream tasks. Progress on this front requires combining tools from random matrix theory, dynamical systems, and optimization theory, and represents one of the most mathematically rich and practically important open problems in self-supervised representation learning [41] [40].

### 4.5 Generalization, Transferability, and Downstream Task Theory

Understanding the generalization and transfer capabilities of self-supervised representations is fundamental to explaining their empirical success across diverse downstream tasks. Rooted in the theoretical foundations developed for collapse avoidance and representation geometry discussed previously, this body of work seeks to characterize when and why representations learned without labels can support accurate prediction after fine-tuning or linear probing, and what properties of the pretraining objective, data distribution, and architecture drive transferability.

**Multi-View Redundancy and Downstream Guarantees**

A central line of work formalizes the conditions under which multi-view or contrastive pretraining guarantees near-optimal downstream performance. The foundational insight is that when two views of a datum provide redundant information about the downstream label, linear functions of the learned representations can achieve near-optimal prediction accuracy [36]. This result provides a theoretical justification for the widely observed empirical phenomenon that contrastive SSL representations, despite being learned without access to labels, transfer remarkably well to supervised classification tasks via simple linear probes. The key theoretical condition—view redundancy with respect to the label—connects augmentation design directly to downstream generalization, establishing that augmentations should preserve label-relevant information while discarding nuisance variation. This connection between augmentation policy and generalization directly complements the geometric perspective established earlier: the augmentation-induced invariances that shape embedding manifold structure are precisely the invariances that determine transferability.

**Generalization Bounds and False Negative Bias**

Generalization error bounds for contrastive methods have been studied to quantify the gap between empirical training objectives and true downstream risk. Such bounds typically depend on the size of the unlabeled pretraining corpus, the complexity of the encoder function class, and the quality of the positive pair construction. The debiased contrastive learning framework further demonstrates that sampling-induced bias from false negatives—unlabeled points sharing the true class of the anchor—can degrade both training-time objectives and downstream generalization, and proposes corrected objectives that provably improve generalization bounds for downstream classification [61]. This work highlights that the theoretical guarantees of contrastive SSL are sensitive to the statistical properties of the negative sampling procedure, and that correcting for false negatives yields tighter bounds and better empirical performance across vision, language, and reinforcement learning benchmarks.

**Information-Theoretic Foundations of Transfer**

From an information-theoretic perspective, multi-view redundancy serves as the theoretical bridge between pretraining and downstream tasks [36]. When the two views used for pretraining share sufficient mutual information with the downstream target, the learned representation is provably sufficient for accurate linear classification. This framework unifies a variety of contrastive and predictive objectives under the common principle of maximizing shared information between views, providing a principled basis for understanding transfer learning performance. Notably, this information-theoretic view is consistent with the geometric understanding that representations exhibiting better alignment and uniformity tend to transfer more effectively—both perspectives converge on the importance of capturing the shared semantic structure across views.

**Probabilistic Latent Variable Perspectives**

Probabilistic latent variable models offer complementary theoretical grounding for transfer. By positing a generative model of the data in which views are independent given a shared latent variable, one can show that discriminative SSL objectives—including contrastive methods such as SimCLR, CLIP, and VICReg—approximately recover the posterior distribution over the latent structure [137]. This generative perspective explains why learned representations transfer well: they capture the underlying causal or semantic structure shared across views, which is precisely the information relevant to diverse downstream tasks. The analysis also theoretically justifies the use of a projection head during pretraining, showing that discarding it at transfer time is consistent with the generative model structure [137]. This result reinforces the architectural insight from the previous section that the projection head absorbs task-specific invariances during pretraining, leaving the encoder backbone with representations of broader geometric richness.

**Architecture, Inductive Bias, and Representation Geometry**

The interplay between inductive bias, network architecture, and generalization is another important dimension of transfer theory. Architectural choices such as the depth and width of the encoder, the presence of batch normalization, and the design of projection heads all influence the geometry of the representation space and, consequently, downstream accuracy. Theoretical analyses suggest that projection heads play a critical role in preventing dimensional collapse—where the representation space degenerates into a low-dimensional subspace—which would severely limit the expressiveness available for downstream tasks. By separating the collapse-preventing function of the projection head from the representation learned by the encoder backbone, SSL methods achieve representations with better geometric properties for transfer. This architectural separation thus serves a dual purpose: it protects against the collapse dynamics analyzed in the preceding section while simultaneously promoting the representational richness that underpins downstream generalization.

**Augmentation Design and Generalization Alignment**

The impact of data augmentation choice on downstream generalization has also received substantial theoretical attention. Augmentation defines the notion of invariance encoded in the representation: strong augmentations enforce greater invariance, potentially discarding task-relevant information, while weak augmentations may not sufficiently abstract away nuisance factors. Theoretical conditions for augmentation design establish that augmentations should be semantically label-preserving—i.e., they should not change the class of the sample—to ensure that the pretraining objective aligns with the downstream task. When this condition is violated, generalization guarantees weaken, providing theoretical motivation for careful domain-specific augmentation design. These theoretical conditions directly echo the geometric observation that augmentation policies act as regulators of embedding manifold structure, determining which directions in input space are collapsed or preserved.

**Empirical Validation and Broader Transfer Evaluation**

Transfer learning evaluation studies provide empirical validation of these theoretical predictions. Domain-agnostic benchmarks and large-scale multi-task evaluations reveal that SSL methods with stronger theoretical foundations—such as those explicitly designed to maximize view redundancy or minimize false negative bias—consistently achieve better transfer performance across diverse domains including natural images, satellite imagery, biological data, and fine-grained recognition tasks [36; 61]. Together, these theoretical and empirical investigations establish a growing body of principled understanding that guides the design of SSL algorithms with reliable and predictable generalization properties, forming the foundation upon which the geometric analyses of the following section are built.

### 4.6 Geometric and Structural Properties of Learned Representations

The geometric and structural properties of representations learned through self-supervised learning have become an important lens through which researchers evaluate, compare, and improve SSL methods. Building upon the theoretical transfer learning guarantees and augmentation design principles established in the preceding discussion, understanding how the embedding space is organized—its curvature, alignment with semantic structure, and sensitivity to architectural choices—provides valuable insights into why certain SSL approaches transfer more effectively to downstream tasks than others. These geometric considerations also set the stage for the architectural investigations of projection heads and related components that follow.

**Manifold Structure and Representation Geometry**

A central observation in the analysis of SSL representations is that the learned embedding space tends to organize data along low-dimensional manifolds corresponding to semantic categories. The geometry of this manifold—including its intrinsic dimensionality, curvature, and local neighborhood structure—directly influences how well representations support downstream classification, retrieval, and transfer tasks. Empirical studies have shown that SSL objectives differ substantially in the geometric organization they induce: contrastive methods such as SimCLR and MoCo tend to produce more uniformly distributed embeddings on a hypersphere, while non-contrastive methods like BYOL and Barlow Twins may produce representations with different spectral properties and cluster geometries. Methods that explicitly promote dimensional decorrelation, such as Barlow Twins and VICReg, encourage representations to span a high-dimensional space more uniformly, potentially avoiding the dimensional collapse where only a few dimensions carry meaningful information. This geometric perspective thus provides a concrete and measurable counterpart to the theoretical collapse avoidance conditions discussed previously.

**Role of Encoder and Projection Head Geometry**

A particularly important architectural dimension is the interplay between the encoder backbone and the projection head appended during pretraining. It has been empirically and theoretically observed that while the projection head improves optimization during pretraining, the representations immediately before the projection head (i.e., those of the encoder) tend to be more transferable and geometrically richer for downstream tasks. The projection head appears to induce a form of geometric transformation that discards information not needed for the pretext task but potentially useful for diverse downstream problems. This observation motivates the common practice of discarding the projection head after pretraining and using only encoder representations for transfer. The geometry induced at the encoder level is thus shaped both by the SSL objective and by the implicit regularization imposed by the projection head's architecture and dimensionality. The precise mechanisms underlying this architectural separation are examined in greater depth in the following section on projection head theory.

**Augmentation Strength and Embedding Space Organization**

The choice and strength of data augmentations play a fundamental role in shaping the geometry of the learned embedding space. Stronger augmentations encourage the encoder to learn more invariant representations, collapsing together views that differ significantly in appearance but share semantic content. However, excessively strong augmentations can destroy fine-grained geometric structure, leading to representations that are too coarse for tasks requiring sensitivity to subtle visual differences. The augmentation policy thus acts as a geometric regularizer, determining which directions in input space are collapsed together in the embedding manifold and which are preserved. This directly extends the theoretical augmentation design conditions discussed earlier—where augmentations were required to be semantically label-preserving—to the geometric level: augmentations that satisfy this condition produce well-organized manifolds while those that violate it distort the embedding geometry in ways that limit transferability. This has direct implications for transfer: representations learned with augmentations that align well with the invariances relevant to a downstream task will naturally produce better-organized manifolds for that task [14].

**Alignment with Semantic Class Hierarchies**

Beyond simple cluster formation, theoretical and empirical analyses have investigated whether SSL representations align with the hierarchical structure of semantic classes—for instance, whether coarse-grained groupings (e.g., animals vs. vehicles) are more clearly separated than fine-grained ones (e.g., cat vs. dog). It has been observed that large-scale SSL pretraining tends to produce representations that respect such hierarchies, with the geometry of the embedding space reflecting the taxonomic relationships among classes. This property is particularly valuable for few-shot and zero-shot transfer, where the geometric structure of the representation space must generalize beyond the specific categories seen during pretraining, and is consistent with the theoretical prediction that view redundancy with respect to the label hierarchy drives downstream generalization. Cross-modal SSL methods that align representations across vision, language, and audio modalities further enrich this geometric structure by grounding visual embeddings in the semantic space of language [72; 75].

**Representation Geometry and Transfer Learning Capability**

The relationship between the geometric properties of SSL representations and their transfer learning capability is a subject of active investigation. Representations that exhibit better alignment (similar samples are close together) and uniformity (the embedding space is evenly covered) tend to transfer more effectively across diverse downstream tasks [14]. These geometric properties connect directly to the information-theoretic and probabilistic frameworks discussed in the previous section: alignment corresponds to the encoder capturing shared information across views, while uniformity corresponds to the full utilization of the representation space predicted by probabilistic latent variable models. Manifold graph metrics, which measure the topology of the learned representation space by constructing neighborhood graphs, have been proposed as label-free tools for comparing SSL models and predicting their downstream performance without requiring labeled evaluation data. Such metrics capture properties like connectivity, cluster compactness, and inter-class separation at a geometric level, providing a principled way to assess representation quality beyond traditional linear probing accuracy.

Furthermore, the spectral properties of the feature covariance matrix—specifically whether eigenvalues follow a power-law decay or collapse to a few dominant dimensions—have been identified as indicators of representation quality and generalization capability. Representations with richer spectral structure, where information is distributed across many dimensions, tend to support broader transfer. This connects to the theoretical understanding of dimensional collapse as a geometric failure mode, where the effective dimensionality of the representation space collapses to a low-dimensional subspace, severely limiting transferability. As discussed in the following section, understanding this spectral perspective on dimensional collapse is central to explaining why the design of the projection head has such outsized influence on the quality of encoder-level representations.

Overall, the geometric and structural analysis of self-supervised representations provides a unifying framework that connects architectural choices, augmentation policies, and learning objectives to the downstream utility of learned features. As SSL methods scale to larger models and more diverse data [73; 76], understanding and controlling the geometry of learned representations becomes increasingly important for building universal and transferable SSL systems, and motivates the detailed architectural investigation of projection heads and asymmetric designs that constitutes the focus of the next section.

### 4.7 Projection Head Theory and Architectural Analysis

The projection head—a small neural network appended to the encoder during self-supervised pretraining and discarded at transfer time—has emerged as one of the most intriguing and theoretically rich components in modern self-supervised learning pipelines. Building on the geometric and structural properties of SSL representations discussed in the previous section, understanding the role of the projection head and related architectural components provides a deeper theoretical basis for why certain design choices produce more transferable and well-organized embedding spaces. Despite its apparent simplicity, the projection head plays a surprisingly critical role in shaping the quality of representations learned by the encoder, and understanding why it should be discarded after training has motivated a substantial body of theoretical investigation.

A central question is why representations extracted from the encoder backbone, rather than from the output of the projection head, consistently yield superior performance on downstream tasks. One influential line of reasoning holds that the projection head absorbs information that is useful for solving the pretext task but potentially harmful for downstream generalization—for instance, invariances to augmentations that destroy semantically meaningful variation. By concentrating this task-specific invariance in the projection subspace, the backbone encoder is freed to retain a richer, more general representation of the input. This interpretation frames the projection head as a form of information bottleneck that decouples pretext-task optimization from representation quality, directly connecting to the earlier observation that encoder-level representations tend to exhibit greater geometric richness and transferability than projector-level features.

Related theoretical analyses have investigated the connection between projection heads and the phenomenon of dimensional collapse, wherein the learned representations span only a low-dimensional subspace of the full embedding space despite the high dimensionality of the encoder output. It has been shown that the projection head can induce or exacerbate dimensional collapse in the representation space, as the contrastive or redundancy-reduction objective applied at the projector output need not encourage full-rank representations at the encoder level. Understanding this relationship has motivated architectural choices such as output dimensionality selection, regularization on the projection subspace, and the use of sparsity-inducing penalties to control which dimensions of the projected space are actively used. This theoretical link between projection head design and dimensional collapse connects directly to the spectral properties and effective dimensionality of learned representations discussed in the context of representation geometry.

The geometry of the projection subspace has also been studied empirically and theoretically. When the projection head is linear, the representations it produces lie in a strict subspace of the encoder output, and analyses have shown that sparsity in the projection subspace—the tendency for only a few directions of the encoder's representation space to be mapped to meaningful directions in the projection space—may partially explain the degraded quality of projector-level features for transfer. Nonlinear projection heads, typically realized as multi-layer perceptrons with batch normalization, mitigate some of these geometric pathologies by allowing more flexible mappings, but they also introduce additional complexity that complicates theoretical analysis.

Asymmetric architectures represent another major axis of architectural investigation. In non-contrastive methods such as BYOL and SimSiam, an additional predictor network is appended to one branch of the siamese network while the other branch uses a stop-gradient or a momentum-updated encoder. Theoretical justifications for these asymmetric designs have argued that the predictor network, combined with stop-gradient operations, prevents representational collapse by effectively implementing an implicit negative sampling mechanism or by enforcing an implicit expectation-maximization-like optimization dynamic. The momentum encoder, updated via an exponential moving average of the online encoder's parameters rather than through direct gradient descent, provides a slowly evolving target that stabilizes training and prevents the two branches from converging to the same trivial solution.

Alternative optimization schemes for the InfoNCE objective have also been theoretically examined. It has been noted that standard gradient-based optimization of InfoNCE does not guarantee full-rank representations, and proposals for regularized or constrained optimization—including orthogonality constraints on encoder weight matrices—have been theoretically motivated as mechanisms for maintaining representational diversity. These connections between optimization dynamics and representation geometry underscore the importance of principled architectural and algorithmic choices in self-supervised learning system design.

Taken together, theoretical investigations into the projection head and related architectural components reveal that the seemingly minor design decisions in self-supervised pipelines—the depth and width of the projector, the use of batch normalization, the presence of predictor networks, and the choice of momentum update rates—have profound consequences for the geometric and statistical properties of learned representations, their susceptibility to collapse, and their ultimate utility for diverse downstream tasks. These architectural considerations complement the geometric analysis of embedding spaces and set the stage for understanding deeper theoretical questions around memorization, robustness, and dataset imbalance, which further characterize how SSL representations behave under challenging and heterogeneous data conditions. The theoretical understanding developed in this area has begun to provide principled guidelines that complement the largely empirical progress that characterized earlier SSL research [34].

### 4.8 Memorization, Robustness, and Dataset Imbalance in SSL Theory

## Memorization, Robustness, and Dataset Imbalance in SSL Theory

Building upon the theoretical analyses of projection heads, architectural components, and the geometric properties of self-supervised embedding spaces discussed in the previous section, understanding how memorization, robustness, and dataset imbalance shape SSL representations represents a critical frontier in representation learning research. Unlike supervised learning, where memorization of training labels is well-studied, SSL encoders exhibit distinct memorization behaviors tied to the structure of pretext tasks and augmentation strategies rather than explicit label assignments. These theoretical considerations directly inform the empirical probing and interpretability investigations explored in the following section.

**Memorization in Self-Supervised Encoders**

Memorization in SSL manifests differently from its supervised counterpart. In supervised settings, memorization refers to a model fitting individual training labels without generalizing to unseen data. In SSL, however, the analogous phenomenon involves the encoder capturing instance-specific features—such as unique textures, backgrounds, or artifacts—that do not generalize across the data distribution. Theoretical analyses suggest that contrastive objectives, by encouraging representations of augmented views of the same instance to be similar, inherently bias encoders toward learning transformation-invariant features rather than instance-specific memorization. This invariance mechanism, while generally beneficial, can interact non-trivially with data heterogeneity, leading to selective memorization of underrepresented patterns.

The relationship between memorization and downstream generalization in SSL is nuanced. Representations that encode overly fine-grained instance details may perform well on retrieval tasks but poorly on classification, as the memorized features may not align with semantic categories. Conversely, representations that are too invariant may discard discriminative information necessary for fine-grained recognition. Empirical investigations have sought to characterize this trade-off by probing learned representations for memorized versus generalized content, finding that the degree of memorization is modulated by the strength and diversity of data augmentations, the size of the negative sample set in contrastive methods, and the architecture of the projection head—connecting directly to the architectural considerations examined in the preceding section.

**Robustness to Dataset Imbalance**

A particularly significant theoretical question concerns why SSL representations tend to be more robust to dataset imbalance and long-tailed distributions compared to supervised representations. In supervised learning, class imbalance directly distorts the loss landscape, causing the model to be dominated by majority class gradients and leading to poor feature learning for minority classes. SSL objectives, by contrast, do not directly optimize class-conditional distributions; instead, they operate over instance-level or view-level relationships, which are less susceptible to class frequency biases in their gradient dynamics.

Theoretical explanations for this robustness center on the geometry of the learned embedding space—a theme that connects tightly to the spectral and geometric properties of SSL representations discussed earlier. Contrastive and non-contrastive SSL methods encourage a uniform distribution of representations across the embedding hypersphere—a property known as uniformity—alongside alignment of representations of semantically similar inputs. This uniformity objective counteracts the tendency of supervised models to allocate disproportionate representational capacity to majority classes. Under long-tailed distributions, SSL objectives effectively prevent minority class representations from collapsing into high-density regions dominated by frequent categories, thereby preserving discriminative structure for tail classes.

However, this robustness is not absolute. Several studies have observed that when self-supervised pretraining is followed by supervised fine-tuning on imbalanced datasets, the final model performance on tail classes still degrades, suggesting that the robustness advantage of SSL manifests primarily in the pretrained representation quality rather than in the fine-tuning stage. Geometric harmonization techniques and re-weighted regularization strategies have been proposed to further improve SSL representation quality under severely skewed distributions, operating on the loss landscape to promote more balanced gradient contributions from samples across the frequency spectrum.

**Regularization and Loss Landscape Shaping**

The role of regularization in shaping the SSL loss landscape is theoretically intertwined with both memorization and robustness, and connects closely to the architectural mechanisms examined in the previous section. Variance-covariance regularization, as employed in methods like VICReg and Barlow Twins, explicitly penalizes dimensional collapse and encourages decorrelated representations, which has the secondary effect of regularizing against memorization by preventing the encoder from concentrating information in a small number of dimensions that might be tied to instance-specific features. Stop-gradient operations in non-contrastive methods like BYOL and SimSiam introduce implicit regularization through asymmetric information flow, and theoretical analyses have connected these mechanisms to shaping the loss landscape in ways that favor broad, flat minima associated with better generalization.

Batch normalization plays a particularly subtle regularization role in SSL, as it introduces implicit data augmentation effects during training by making representations dependent on the batch statistics rather than individual instances alone. This statistical coupling across samples within a batch effectively prevents the encoder from memorizing individual training instances, instead encouraging it to learn features that are consistent across the batch distribution. The interplay between batch normalization, projection head design, and memorization has been identified as a key factor determining the quality of SSL representations—reinforcing the central importance of projection head design choices analyzed in the preceding section.

**SSL under Non-IID and Non-Stationary Data Distributions**

Theoretical insights into SSL behavior under non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) and non-stationary data distributions are particularly relevant for real-world deployment scenarios, where data arrives in streams with evolving statistics. Standard SSL theoretical analyses assume that training data is drawn i.i.d. from a fixed distribution, but this assumption breaks down in federated learning, online learning, and continual learning settings.

Under non-IID conditions, contrastive learning faces the challenge that the negative samples encountered within a mini-batch may not be representative of the global data distribution, leading to biased gradient estimates and suboptimal representations. Theoretical analyses of this phenomenon have characterized the bias in contrastive loss gradients as a function of the deviation between the local batch distribution and the global data distribution, and have proposed corrective strategies such as memory banks and momentum encoders that maintain a more globally representative pool of negative samples—directly extending the momentum encoder mechanisms discussed in the context of asymmetric architectures.

Non-stationary distributions introduce the additional challenge of distributional drift, where the optimal representation for the current data distribution may conflict with representations learned from earlier data. Theoretical frameworks from continual learning, including Bayesian perspectives such as elastic weight consolidation, have been adapted to analyze SSL in non-stationary settings, characterizing the stability-plasticity trade-off in terms of the curvature of the SSL loss landscape with respect to model parameters. Parameters associated with flat loss landscape curvature can be updated aggressively to accommodate new data, while parameters at sharp curvature points encode information critical to past distributions and should be updated conservatively.

The behavior of SSL under distribution shift—where the test distribution differs systematically from the training distribution—has also received theoretical attention. SSL representations have been empirically observed to exhibit greater robustness to common corruptions and domain shifts compared to supervised representations trained on the same data, and theoretical explanations have been offered in terms of the alignment between SSL augmentation invariances and the structure of common distributional perturbations. When the augmentation policy used during pretraining encodes transformations that are similar in character to the distribution shifts encountered at test time, SSL representations naturally generalize across the shift. This theoretical insight motivates the design of domain-informed augmentation strategies that explicitly model anticipated distribution shifts during SSL pretraining, providing a principled path toward more robust and transferable representations for real-world applications.

Overall, the theoretical understanding of memorization, robustness, and dataset imbalance in SSL remains an active and important research area. Progress in this direction is essential for deploying SSL systems reliably in the heterogeneous, imbalanced, and non-stationary data environments characteristic of practical applications, and for developing principled design guidelines that go beyond empirical observation to provide theoretical guarantees on representation quality under challenging data conditions. These theoretical considerations set the stage for the empirical probing, geometric analysis, and interpretability investigations explored in the following section, which provide complementary experimental evidence for the behavioral properties of SSL representations characterized here.

### 4.9 Probing and Empirical Investigations of Representation Properties

## Empirical Analysis of Self-Supervised Representations: Probing, Geometry, and Interpretability

Building upon the theoretical foundations of memorization, robustness, and dataset imbalance explored in the previous section, empirical investigation of what information is encoded in self-supervised representations has become an essential complement to formal analyses, providing insights that guide the design of better objectives, architectures, and training protocols. A central methodology in this line of work is the use of **probing classifiers**: lightweight linear or shallow nonlinear models trained on frozen self-supervised representations to assess whether specific semantic, syntactic, or structural properties are linearly decodable from the learned feature space. Probing studies have revealed that different SSL objectives encode qualitatively different information — contrastive methods such as SimCLR and MoCo tend to produce representations with strong global semantic clustering, whereas masked reconstruction-based methods tend to encode richer local structural detail, a distinction with direct implications for downstream task selection.

**Representational similarity analysis (RSA)** and centered kernel alignment (CKA) have been widely employed to compare the internal geometry of SSL feature spaces against supervised counterparts and against each other. Such analyses have demonstrated that despite differences in objective functions, many SSL methods converge to broadly similar representational geometries at the final encoder layer, though intermediate layers can differ substantially. This finding suggests that the choice of pretext task shapes the trajectory of representation formation during training even when terminal representations appear similar, motivating layer-wise investigations rather than relying solely on final-layer evaluations.

**Saliency-based and attention-based explanations** offer another empirical window into representation quality. In vision transformers trained with self-supervised objectives, attention maps have been shown to spontaneously segment semantically meaningful regions without any localization supervision, providing evidence that SSL objectives encourage spatially structured representations beneficial for dense prediction tasks such as detection and segmentation. These qualitative observations have been quantified through unsupervised saliency scoring methods and localization metrics that assess whether the most informative image regions according to the encoder align with ground-truth object boundaries.

Investigations into how **augmentation choices** shape the representational geometry have revealed a fundamental trade-off: stronger augmentations encourage greater invariance and improved semantic clustering, but may discard fine-grained information necessary for tasks requiring sensitivity to low-level variations. Empirical studies on this trade-off have shown that the augmentation strength modulates the effective dimensionality of the learned representation, with overly aggressive augmentations leading to dimensional collapse in which the representational subspace degenerates to a low-dimensional manifold. This connection between augmentation, geometry, and collapse has been examined through the lens of projection head behavior [138], revealing that contrastive loss induces expansion or shrinkage effects on signal directions in projection space, and that these effects propagate back to shape the encoder representations in ways measurable through downstream linear probing accuracy.

The role of the **projection head** has been empirically demonstrated to be critical: representations extracted before the projector consistently outperform those taken after it on linear evaluation benchmarks, even when the projector is itself linear [138]. Empirical ablations across architectures and datasets have confirmed this phenomenon and led to investigations into why projectors suppress useful information — findings pointing to the interaction between the contrastive objective and the projector's tendency to discard task-relevant variance in favor of augmentation-invariant directions. These observations connect directly to the theoretical regularization mechanisms discussed previously, wherein projection head design interacts with batch normalization and stop-gradient operations to shape the overall loss landscape.

**Cross-modal probing studies** spanning vision, speech, and sensor modalities have examined how well SSL representations encode semantically meaningful properties across domains. For speech, probing classifiers trained on frozen SSL representations have been used to assess the degree to which phonemic, lexical, and speaker identity information is encoded at different network depths, revealing a hierarchical organization in which lower layers encode acoustic features and upper layers encode more abstract linguistic content. For sensor-based activity recognition, analogous probing experiments have examined the degree to which time-frequency patterns and activity-specific rhythms are captured by SSL encoders, complementing linear evaluation benchmarks with more interpretable diagnostic tools.

Empirical investigations into **disentanglement** in SSL representations have found that most contrastive and non-contrastive objectives do not explicitly encourage factorial or disentangled representations, yet partial disentanglement of semantic factors can emerge as a byproduct of multi-view learning. Methods that explicitly incorporate sequential or modality-specific disentanglement objectives — such as frameworks that separate speaker identity from linguistic content [127] — have shown that targeted architectural interventions can meaningfully increase the interpretability and transferability of learned representations compared to vanilla contrastive baselines.

The **impact of hyperparameters** on representation properties has been studied empirically through systematic ablations of batch size, temperature, training duration, and learning rate schedules. Temperature in the InfoNCE loss has been shown to regulate the hardness-awareness of the contrastive objective [130], with lower temperatures producing more discriminative but potentially overfit representations and higher temperatures yielding more uniform embedding spaces. These findings have motivated adaptive temperature mechanisms and dual-temperature formulations that decouple the roles of temperature in attracting positive pairs and repelling negative pairs, as empirically validated through probing and downstream fine-tuning experiments. Such hyperparameter sensitivity also has important implications for robustness under distribution shift and data imbalance, reinforcing the connection between empirical analysis and the theoretical concerns raised in the preceding section.

Finally, **power-law analyses** of feature covariance spectra have emerged as a promising label-free diagnostic tool for assessing representation quality without requiring downstream evaluation. The observation that well-trained SSL representations exhibit characteristic power-law decay in their singular value spectra — analogous to findings in supervised models — provides a quantitative indicator of the richness and effective dimensionality of the learned feature space, connecting representational geometry to generalization capacity. Together, these empirical methodologies form a growing toolkit for interpretable analysis of SSL representations that complements formal theory and guides the principled design of future self-supervised learning systems, setting the stage for the architectural and training innovations examined in the sections that follow.

## 5 Applications, Transfer Learning, and Domain-Specific Advances

### 5.1 Image Classification and Visual Recognition

## 5.1 Image Classification and Visual Recognition

Image classification and visual recognition represent the most extensively studied downstream applications for evaluating the quality of self-supervised representations. The standard evaluation paradigm involves two complementary protocols: linear evaluation, where a linear classifier is trained on top of frozen self-supervised features, and full fine-tuning, where the entire network is updated on the labeled downstream dataset. These protocols have become the de facto benchmarks for comparing SSL methods on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet.

Several landmark self-supervised methods have demonstrated remarkable performance on these benchmarks. Contrastive frameworks such as SimCLR and MoCo demonstrated that representations learned through instance discrimination and momentum-contrast mechanisms could achieve competitive or superior performance compared to supervised pretraining under linear evaluation settings [14]. Masked image modeling approaches have also shown strong transfer performance on classification benchmarks, with methods combining reconstruction and contrastive objectives yielding particularly rich representations that perform well under both linear probing and fine-tuning conditions.

Beyond standard ImageNet benchmarks, a critical dimension of self-supervised representation learning is generalization to domain-specific imagery, where labeled data is particularly scarce or expensive to obtain. In the medical imaging domain, self-supervised pretraining has proven highly beneficial, with SSL models substantially outperforming baselines trained with limited labeled data [8]. For example, in breast cancer detection on screening mammography, strong augmentation-based SSL techniques achieved improved data efficiency of nearly four-fold compared to supervised counterparts, and the learned representations transferred effectively across different dataset distributions [8]. Similarly, hierarchical self-supervised pretraining on aggregated multi-domain medical datasets has been shown to yield better initialization for downstream segmentation and classification tasks, demonstrating that multi-domain SSL pretraining significantly outperforms single-domain pretraining [18].

In the remote sensing and satellite imagery domain, self-supervised methods have been applied to address the scarcity of labeled annotations for land cover mapping and object recognition [9]. Clustering-augmented self-supervised learning has been demonstrated to produce discriminative feature representations from unlabeled remote sensing images without any human annotations, enabling effective downstream classification and segmentation [139]. The learned cluster structure additionally supports active sampling strategies that improve classification accuracy under limited annotation budgets.

For fine-grained visual recognition tasks, where subtle inter-class differences require highly discriminative representations, standard SSL methods pretrained on general-purpose datasets may not capture the domain-specific nuances required. To address this, coreset sampling strategies have been proposed to select informative subsets from large-scale open-set unlabeled data whose distribution aligns closely with fine-grained target datasets, enabling SSL pretraining that better captures fine-grained characteristics [108]. This approach demonstrates significant improvements across eleven fine-grained datasets and diverse downstream tasks, highlighting the importance of data distribution alignment between pretraining and target domains.

The scalability of SSL methods to large-scale unlabeled data has also been a key factor in their success for visual recognition. Scaling self-supervised approaches to 100 million images has been shown to approach or match the performance of supervised pretraining on tasks such as object detection, surface normal estimation, and visual navigation, while also revealing important insights about the limitations of current SSL objectives in learning high-level semantic representations at scale [140]. Comprehensive benchmarking across nine datasets has further highlighted the importance of standardized evaluation protocols to meaningfully compare SSL methods and identify their strengths and limitations for universal visual representation learning.

Small-dataset scenarios present a particularly challenging setting where self-supervised pretraining can offer substantial benefits. Hybrid architectures combining self-supervised pretraining with Vision Transformers have demonstrated significant performance improvements on small-scale datasets such as STL-10, where the low-level features derived from self-supervised architectures provide better initialization than raw image inputs [19]. This suggests that self-supervised pretraining is especially valuable in data-limited regimes where supervised training from scratch tends to overfit or underfit.

In summary, self-supervised representation learning has established itself as a powerful pretraining strategy for image classification and visual recognition across a broad spectrum of data types, scales, and domains. The combination of strong contrastive, non-contrastive, and masked modeling objectives with appropriate augmentation strategies enables the learning of transferable representations that generalize well from large-scale pretraining to diverse downstream classification tasks, including challenging domain-specific applications in medical imaging, remote sensing, and fine-grained recognition [15]. Having examined classification and global recognition, we next consider how self-supervised representations transfer to object detection and dense prediction tasks, where spatially precise, pixel-level feature quality becomes the primary criterion for evaluation.

### 5.2 Object Detection and Dense Prediction

## 5.2 Object Detection and Dense Prediction

The transfer of self-supervised representations to object detection and dense prediction tasks represents a critical test of whether SSL-learned features encode not merely global semantic content but also fine-grained spatial and structural information. Building directly on the classification foundations discussed in the previous subsection, dense prediction tasks such as object detection, semantic segmentation, and instance segmentation require spatially precise, pixel-level feature maps that preserve localization cues and object boundaries—properties that stand in stark contrast to the coarse, holistic representations sufficient for image-level recognition. This distinction poses unique challenges for self-supervised pretraining, as many contrastive objectives are explicitly designed to be invariant to spatial transformations, potentially discarding the very spatial information needed for localization.

Early self-supervised methods demonstrated that representations pretrained on ImageNet could transfer reasonably well to detection benchmarks such as PASCAL VOC and COCO when used as backbone initializations, providing a compelling alternative to fully supervised pretraining. However, a persistent gap remained between SSL-pretrained and supervised-pretrained models on tasks demanding localization precision. Addressing this gap has motivated significant research into dense contrastive pretraining, region-level learning objectives, and spatially aware augmentation strategies—all of which move beyond the global objectives that proved sufficient for the classification tasks examined in the previous subsection.

Multi-scale cropping strategies have proven particularly effective for dense prediction, as they encourage representations to be informative across different spatial resolutions and scales. By constructing positive pairs from crops at varying scales and positions, SSL methods can learn features that generalize across object sizes and spatial configurations. This approach closely mirrors the multi-scale processing requirements of modern detection architectures such as Feature Pyramid Networks. The introduction of region-level contrastive objectives, where individual object regions or superpixel segments are contrasted rather than entire images, further bridges the gap between global contrastive pretraining and pixel-level downstream tasks. Methods exploiting superpixel decomposition for dense learning have demonstrated improvements in high-resolution feature map quality, with experiments showing that region-based contrasting extends the applicability of contrastive learning to high-resolution images and improves overclustering performance on benchmarks such as Cityscapes and COCO [141].

Flow-equivariant learning represents another important direction for dense prediction, where pretraining objectives explicitly preserve equivariance to spatial transformations such as optical flow fields. Rather than encouraging invariance to spatial perturbations as in standard contrastive learning, equivariant approaches retain transformation-related information in the learned representations. This distinction is especially valuable for tasks such as object detection and semantic segmentation, where understanding the spatial relationship between image regions is fundamental. By learning to predict or encode the transformation between views, flow-equivariant methods produce features that are better aligned with the spatial structure of visual scenes, leading to improved localization performance.

Dense semantic contrast methods further advance this direction by operating at the feature map level rather than the global embedding level, computing contrastive losses over corresponding spatial locations across augmented views. This encourages the model to learn per-pixel discriminative features while maintaining global semantic consistency. Evaluation on segmentation benchmarks has shown that dense contrastive pretraining yields substantial improvements over both random initialization and standard global contrastive pretraining, particularly in low-data regimes where labeled segmentation masks are scarce.

The interplay between pretraining resolution, patch size, and downstream dense prediction performance has also received considerable attention. Masked image modeling approaches such as MAE, with their patch-based reconstruction objectives operating at fine spatial granularity, have demonstrated competitive performance on dense prediction tasks, suggesting that reconstruction-based pretraining naturally encourages spatially structured representations [24]. The complementary strengths of contrastive and generative objectives for dense prediction have motivated hybrid frameworks that combine masked autoencoding with contrastive learning, such as CAN and MACRL, which leverage both the spatial reconstruction signals of masked autoencoders and the semantic discrimination of contrastive objectives [142; 143].

Object-level representation learning from complex scene images has emerged as a particularly important challenge. Unlike object-centric datasets such as ImageNet, scene images contain multiple objects with complex spatial relationships, making it difficult to define meaningful positive pairs at the image level. Methods addressing this challenge leverage image-level SSL pretraining as a prior to discover object-level semantic correspondences, enabling self-supervised learning to scale to complex scene imagery and approach or surpass supervised ImageNet pretraining on several detection and segmentation downstream tasks on COCO [144]. This object-level perspective naturally anticipates the instance segmentation paradigm explored in the following subsection, where precise delineation of individual object instances requires both semantic and spatial precision.

Domain-specific applications of SSL for dense prediction have also been explored, including security inspection X-ray image analysis where dense prediction tasks such as prohibited item localization present unique challenges distinct from natural image detection. Approaches integrating contrastive pretraining with region-level objectives and false-negative mitigation strategies have demonstrated improvements over random initialization and competitive performance relative to supervised baselines in such specialized settings [145].

Overall, the trajectory of SSL for dense prediction has evolved from simple transfer of globally pretrained features toward specialized pretraining objectives that explicitly encode spatial, structural, and multi-scale information. The combination of region-level contrastive learning, equivariant objectives, multi-scale cropping, and hybrid generative-contrastive frameworks has substantially narrowed the gap between SSL and supervised pretraining on challenging benchmarks, establishing self-supervised dense prediction as a mature and practically valuable research direction. As we move from detection to segmentation in the following subsection, these spatial representation properties become even more critical, with the demand for fine-grained boundary delineation and instance-level discrimination pushing SSL objectives to their highest spatial resolution.

### 5.3 Instance and Semantic Segmentation

## 5.3 Instance and Semantic Segmentation

Instance and semantic segmentation represent challenging dense prediction tasks that require spatially structured representations, and self-supervised learning has emerged as a powerful pretraining strategy for these problems. Building on the dense prediction foundations discussed in the previous subsection, segmentation further demands that learned features encode fine-grained spatial correspondence, object boundaries, and part-level semantics—properties that differ substantially from globally pooled representations and that stress-test the spatial fidelity of self-supervised pretraining in especially demanding ways.

Vision Transformer-based self-supervised methods have demonstrated particularly strong capabilities for segmentation. Features learned through objectives like DINO exhibit emergent segmentation properties, where self-attention maps naturally highlight object boundaries and instance-level regions without any explicit supervision. This property arises because transformer architectures attending over patch tokens implicitly learn spatial grouping, making them well-suited for unsupervised object-part discovery and instance-aware representation learning. Similarly, Masked Autoencoder (MAE) pretraining encourages the encoder to develop holistic spatial understanding by reconstructing missing patches, leading to representations that capture local texture and global structure simultaneously, both of which are critical for segmentation. As noted in the preceding discussion of object detection, the patch-based reconstruction objective of MAE operates at fine spatial granularity, and this characteristic translates naturally into strong segmentation performance, particularly when labeled mask annotations are scarce.

Dense semantic contrast methods constitute another major family of SSL approaches tailored for segmentation. Rather than contrasting globally aggregated embeddings, these methods define positive and negative pairs at the pixel or patch level, encouraging semantically similar spatial locations to cluster together in the feature space while separating dissimilar regions. Such pixel-level contrastive objectives directly optimize for the spatial coherence required by segmentation tasks, and they complement the region-level contrastive strategies discussed in the context of object detection by pushing spatial discrimination to even finer granularities.

The complementarity between generative and contrastive pretraining objectives is also highly relevant for segmentation. Generative methods like MAE tend to produce representations rich in low-level textural detail useful for boundary detection, while contrastive methods tend to encode higher-level semantic groupings. Hybrid frameworks that combine both objectives can therefore yield representations that simultaneously capture the fine-grained spatial detail and semantic discrimination needed for strong segmentation performance, echoing the motivation behind the hybrid generative-contrastive frameworks such as CAN and MACRL introduced in the dense prediction context.

Evaluation across standard benchmarks such as COCO and PASCAL VOC has consistently shown that self-supervised pretraining with spatially aware objectives substantially reduces the performance gap with fully supervised counterparts, particularly in semi-supervised settings where only a fraction of pixel-level annotations are available. The broader insight from this body of work is that the choice of SSL objective significantly shapes the spatial structure of learned representations [35], and that objectives explicitly designed to preserve or exploit local spatial relationships yield the most transferable features for dense prediction tasks like instance and semantic segmentation. These insights naturally extend to the temporal domain, where analogous questions about the relationship between pretraining objectives and the structure of learned representations arise in the context of video understanding, as explored in the following subsection.

### 5.4 Video Understanding and Temporal Representation Learning

## 5.4 Video Understanding and Temporal Representation Learning

Video understanding presents unique challenges for self-supervised representation learning compared to static image domains, as models must capture both spatial appearance and temporal dynamics across frames. Building naturally on the spatial representation challenges addressed in instance and semantic segmentation, video understanding extends these concerns into the temporal dimension, requiring pretext tasks and architectures that explicitly exploit the sequential and dynamic structure of video data. A rich body of work has emerged that designs such pretext tasks specifically exploiting the temporal structure inherent in video, leading to substantial progress on benchmarks such as UCF101, HMDB51, and Kinetics.

Early self-supervised video methods focused on temporal ordering as a supervisory signal, training networks to predict whether frame sequences were presented in the correct temporal order. This family of approaches leverages the natural temporal coherence of video as a free source of supervision, encouraging encoders to develop internal representations sensitive to motion and action dynamics—a direct analog to the way spatially aware objectives in segmentation encourage sensitivity to local spatial structure. Building on these foundations, predictive coding approaches such as Dense Predictive Coding (DPC) extended the paradigm by training models to predict future spatiotemporal representations in a recurrent latent space, thereby learning to encode motion trajectories and temporal context useful for action recognition.

Contrastive learning has proven particularly effective for video representation learning. Methods such as VDIM (Video Deep InfoMax) and BraVe adapt contrastive objectives to the video domain by constructing positive pairs from different temporal or spatial views of the same video clip, encouraging the encoder to learn view-invariant features that nonetheless capture action-relevant content. This mirrors the dense contrastive strategies discussed for segmentation, but operates over temporal as well as spatial dimensions. BraVe specifically broadens the range of temporal and spatial views used as positive pairs, improving the diversity of the learned representations and their transferability to downstream recognition tasks. These contrastive video frameworks demonstrate competitive performance on standard action recognition benchmarks, often surpassing prior self-supervised video approaches by significant margins.

Multi-transformation classification provides another avenue for video self-supervision, where models are trained to identify which combination of spatial and temporal transformations have been applied to a video clip. This approach encourages representations to be sensitive to the rich variety of spatio-temporal transformations that characterize different actions, yielding features that transfer well to fine-grained action recognition tasks.

Language-based supervision has opened new directions for video representation learning. Joint video-language models such as VideoBERT learn from the natural co-occurrence of video frames and textual descriptions, employing masked modeling objectives over both visual tokens and language tokens simultaneously. By grounding visual representations in semantic language concepts, these models acquire action concept spaces that generalize across diverse video understanding tasks including action recognition, video captioning, and video question answering. The success of VideoBERT inspired a broader family of video-language pretraining approaches that exploit the rich semantic supervision available from transcribed speech, subtitles, and web-scraped text paired with video content, foreshadowing the fully integrated multimodal frameworks explored in subsequent sections.

Multi-task self-supervised frameworks have also been explored for video, where combining multiple temporal and spatial pretext objectives encourages the learning of both transformation-invariant and transformation-sensitive representations. Frameworks like [115] demonstrate that jointly learning invariant and equivariant features in a parameter-efficient multi-task manner yields strong and flexible representations that benefit diverse downstream tasks, including those requiring sensitivity to temporal transformations present in video.

The integration of audio with visual modalities provides additional temporal supervision, as natural video contains synchronized audio streams whose correspondence to visual content can serve as a powerful self-supervised signal. Audiovisual contrastive methods leverage cross-modal temporal alignment to learn richer representations than those obtainable from visual data alone, with the learned features demonstrating strong transfer to both visual and audio downstream tasks. This cross-modal temporal alignment principle forms a direct conceptual bridge to the speech and audio representation learning methods discussed in the following section, where temporal structure similarly serves as a central source of self-supervised signal.

Overall, self-supervised video representation learning has achieved remarkable progress by exploiting the temporal, spatial, and cross-modal structure inherent in video data. The central insight echoing across both spatial dense prediction and temporal video understanding is that pretraining objectives explicitly aligned with the structural properties of the target domain—whether spatial correspondence for segmentation or temporal coherence for action recognition—yield the most transferable and effective representations. State-of-the-art methods approach or exceed supervised baselines on UCF101 and HMDB51 for action recognition, while large-scale video-language models demonstrate compelling zero-shot and few-shot transfer capabilities on Kinetics and beyond. Remaining challenges include efficient pretraining on long-form video, handling complex temporal reasoning, and scaling to diverse action vocabularies across real-world video domains.

### 5.5 Speech and Audio Processing

## 5.5 Speech and Audio Representation Learning

Self-supervised representation learning has emerged as a transformative paradigm for speech and audio processing, addressing the fundamental challenge that large-scale labeled speech corpora are expensive and time-consuming to produce. By leveraging the intrinsic temporal and spectral structure of audio signals, SSL methods have achieved remarkable progress across automatic speech recognition (ASR), speaker identification, speaker verification, and general audio classification benchmarks. This progression closely mirrors the success of self-supervised methods in video understanding described in the previous section, with temporal structure again serving as a central source of supervisory signal, while also anticipating the multimodal extensions discussed in the following section on language and multimodal learning.

**Generative, Contrastive, and Predictive Pre-training Approaches**

The landscape of SSL for speech encompasses three broad families of methods. Generative approaches, such as autoregressive and masked prediction models, learn to reconstruct masked or future segments of audio, capitalizing on the sequential nature of speech to derive rich acoustic representations. Predictive approaches, exemplified by contrastive predictive coding (CPC), train encoders to predict future latent representations from past context, effectively capturing long-range temporal dependencies inherent in spoken language. Contrastive methods adapt the instance discrimination principle from visual SSL to the audio domain, constructing positive pairs from temporally or augmentation-related audio segments while contrasting against negative samples to learn discriminative embeddings.

A key challenge in audio SSL is the design of meaningful augmentations and positive pair construction. Unlike images, where spatial cropping and color jitter are well-established, audio requires careful consideration of augmentations that preserve linguistic or acoustic content while introducing sufficient variability. Strategies such as time-frequency masking, pitch shifting, speed perturbation, and additive noise have been explored, with empirical evidence suggesting that combining multiple augmentation types improves representation robustness. The selection of appropriate temporal windows for positive pairs is particularly critical, as pairs drawn from nearby temporal contexts tend to share phonemic and speaker-level content, facilitating the learning of semantically meaningful features.

**Efficiency-Oriented Methods**

Given the high computational cost of pretraining large speech models on thousands of hours of unlabeled audio, efficiency-oriented SSL methods have received growing attention. Approaches such as distillation-based compression seek to transfer the representational capacity of large self-supervised speech models to lightweight student architectures suitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices. Knowledge distillation for speech SSL presents unique challenges compared to visual domains, as speech representations must preserve both fine-grained acoustic detail for ASR and higher-level speaker and prosodic information for other downstream tasks. Task-agnostic distillation methods that compress self-supervised speech encoders without access to labeled data are particularly valuable, enabling the benefits of large-scale pretraining to be realized in practical deployment scenarios. Multi-teacher distillation strategies that aggregate complementary representations from multiple self-supervised models have shown promise in bridging the gap between model efficiency and representation quality [104].

**Audio-Visual Self-Supervised Learning**

Audio-visual correspondence provides a naturally occurring supervisory signal, as video recordings contain synchronized visual and acoustic streams whose co-occurrence can be exploited for cross-modal SSL. This cross-modal perspective directly complements the audiovisual contrastive methods discussed in the video understanding section, extending the same principle of temporal alignment to richer joint audio-visual representations. Audio-visual instance discrimination methods train models to associate audio segments with their corresponding video frames while discriminating against temporally misaligned or semantically unrelated audio-visual pairs. This cross-modal contrastive objective encourages the model to learn representations that capture the shared semantic content between visual and acoustic modalities, such as the correspondence between lip movements and speech sounds or between environmental sounds and their visual sources. Such representations generalize effectively to downstream tasks in both modalities, demonstrating the richness of the supervisory signal provided by natural audio-visual co-occurrence.

The robustness of audio-visual SSL models to noise and background interference has been identified as a critical property for real-world deployment. Methods that explicitly account for the presence of background sounds and visual distractors during pretraining tend to produce more robust representations, as the model must learn to focus on the relevant audio-visual correspondence signal amidst confounding information. Architectures that process audio and visual streams through separate but jointly trained encoders with cross-modal fusion mechanisms have proven effective, with the contrastive objective aligning the two representation spaces while preserving modality-specific information.

**Transferability to Few-Shot Audio Classification**

A compelling advantage of large-scale self-supervised audio pretraining is the ability to transfer learned representations to novel acoustic categories with very few labeled examples. Few-shot audio classification, where the model must recognize new sound categories from as few as one or five labeled examples per class, provides a stringent test of representation quality and generalization. SSL-pretrained encoders consistently outperform randomly initialized models in this setting, as the pretraining phase instills broad acoustic knowledge that can be rapidly adapted to new categories. The transferability of self-supervised audio representations depends critically on the diversity and scale of the pretraining corpus, with models pretrained on heterogeneous audio collections exhibiting better generalization to unseen sound categories than those pretrained on domain-specific data.

The connection between SSL for audio and broader multi-modal frameworks highlights a promising direction that anticipates the fully integrated multimodal systems discussed in the following section: unified models that learn representations spanning speech, environmental sound, and music can leverage complementary supervisory signals across audio sub-domains. Frameworks that treat different audio types as distinct views of a common acoustic reality, drawing inspiration from multi-view contrastive learning principles [36], offer a principled approach to learning universal audio representations.

**Benchmarks and Evaluation**

Standard benchmarks for evaluating SSL speech representations include LibriSpeech for ASR, VoxCeleb for speaker verification and identification, and AudioSet and ESC-50 for audio event classification. Linear evaluation protocols, where a linear classifier is trained on top of frozen SSL representations, serve as a standard measure of representation quality without the confounding effects of fine-tuning. Full fine-tuning evaluations, particularly in low-resource settings with limited labeled data, demonstrate the practical value of SSL pretraining for speech applications. The consistent improvements achieved by SSL methods over supervised baselines in low-resource ASR and few-shot audio classification highlight the potential of self-supervised audio pretraining to democratize speech technology for languages and domains where large labeled datasets are unavailable.

In summary, self-supervised representation learning has fundamentally advanced the state of speech and audio processing by enabling the extraction of rich acoustic representations from unlabeled data. The convergence of generative, contrastive, and predictive pre-training paradigms, combined with audio-visual self-supervision and efficiency-oriented distillation, positions SSL as a cornerstone methodology for building robust and generalizable speech and audio systems across diverse real-world applications. These foundations in unimodal and cross-modal audio-visual learning set the stage for the fully integrated language and multimodal self-supervised frameworks explored in the following section.

### 5.6 Natural Language Processing and Multimodal Learning

## Language and Multimodal Self-Supervised Learning

Self-supervised learning has played a transformative role in natural language processing (NLP), with objectives such as masked language modeling and next-token prediction establishing the foundation for large-scale pretraining. These objectives allow models to learn rich contextual representations from vast corpora of unlabeled text, enabling strong transfer to downstream tasks including text classification, question answering, natural language inference, and sentiment analysis. Building naturally on the audio-visual cross-modal principles explored in the preceding section on speech and audio representation learning, self-supervised learning has increasingly been extended to fully multimodal settings that jointly model language alongside visual, audio, and other sensory streams, forming the integrated frameworks that also underpin the sensor-based activity recognition methods discussed in the following section.

A prominent example of joint video-language pretraining is VideoBERT [75], which builds upon the BERT masked language modeling paradigm to learn bidirectional joint distributions over sequences of visual and linguistic tokens derived from video and automatic speech recognition outputs. VideoBERT demonstrates that large-scale cross-modal pretraining leads to high-level semantic representations capable of supporting open-vocabulary action classification and video captioning, with performance improvements attributed to the scale of training data and the richness of cross-modal supervision.

Multimodal self-supervised frameworks have further expanded to incorporate three or more modalities simultaneously. For instance, versatile multimodal networks trained via self-supervision on visual, audio, and language streams offer a unified representation that supports downstream tasks across all constituent modalities [72]. Such networks achieve state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks including UCF101, HMDB51, Kinetics600, AudioSet, and ESC-50 by leveraging complementary information across modalities during pretraining. Similarly, frameworks like i-Code [146] introduce flexible architectures that dynamically process single, dual, or triple-modality inputs from vision, speech, and language, using masked modality unit modeling and cross-modality contrastive objectives to learn unified general-purpose representations that outperform state-of-the-art methods on video understanding and NLP benchmarks.

Cross-modal contrastive learning provides another powerful paradigm for multimodal NLP. Methods such as TVDIM [147] extend mutual information maximization across text and image modalities, optimizing both intra-modality and inter-modality feature consistency through ranking-based contrastive learning to produce visual representations that significantly outperform prior visual self-supervised methods on image classification benchmarks. In a complementary direction, MACD [148] demonstrates that decoupled multimodal contrastive pretraining can inject visual knowledge into text encoders, enabling competitive performance on natural language inference tasks such as SNLI and STS-B without any labeled inference data.

Text-enhanced self-supervision has also been leveraged to learn semantic visual features by training convolutional networks to predict textual topic embeddings associated with images, yielding strong performance on image classification, object detection, and multimodal retrieval tasks [149]. More recent large-scale multimodal pretraining efforts, such as the M5Product framework and its SCALE method [73], extend these ideas to five heterogeneous modalities, demonstrating the importance of dataset scale and diversity for multimodal representation learning across diverse downstream tasks.

Domain-agnostic benchmarks such as DABS [76] provide a comprehensive evaluation infrastructure spanning natural images, English text, speech, multilingual text, and paired image-text domains, revealing that while specialized SSL algorithms achieve strong performance within individual domains, developing truly universal self-supervised methods that generalize across all modalities remains an open and important challenge. This aspiration for domain-agnostic generalization echoes directly in the following section, where SSL methods for wearable sensor and physiological signal data similarly strive for representations that transfer broadly across heterogeneous sensing modalities and deployment scenarios. Collectively, these efforts highlight that self-supervised learning has become the central paradigm for representation learning in NLP and multimodal settings alike, with masked modeling, contrastive objectives, and cross-modal alignment forming the principal methodological pillars driving progress across language understanding, image captioning, and cross-modal retrieval benchmarks.

### 5.7 Human Activity Recognition and Physiological Signal Analysis

## Human Activity Recognition and Physiological Signal Analysis

Human activity recognition (HAR) from wearable sensors and physiological signal analysis represent important application domains where labeled data is particularly scarce and expensive to obtain, making self-supervised learning an attractive solution. Just as the multimodal and NLP domains covered in the preceding subsection benefit from leveraging large amounts of unlabeled data through self-supervised objectives, sensors such as accelerometers, gyroscopes, and electroencephalography (EEG) devices generate abundant unlabeled temporal data that SSL methods can exploit to learn generalizable representations transferable to downstream recognition tasks. These learned representations lay important groundwork for the embodied and interactive settings discussed in the following subsection on reinforcement learning and robotics.

A prominent line of work focuses on leveraging multi-task self-supervised pretraining for wearable sensor data. By combining multiple pretext tasks such as transformation recognition, temporal ordering, and signal reconstruction within temporal convolutional network architectures, models can capture diverse aspects of the underlying activity patterns without requiring manual annotations. These multi-task frameworks learn richer representations than single-objective pretraining, demonstrating consistent improvements in semi-supervised settings where only small fractions of labeled data are available for fine-tuning.

Teacher-student frameworks have also proven effective for HAR. SelfHAR exemplifies this approach by employing a self-training strategy in which a teacher model generates pseudo-labels for unlabeled sensor data, which are then used to supervise a student model alongside the original labeled examples. This iterative self-training process progressively refines pseudo-label quality, leading to notable accuracy improvements over purely supervised baselines, particularly when labeled examples are severely limited. Such approaches bridge self-supervised representation learning with semi-supervised learning principles, exploiting the abundant unlabeled sensor streams commonly available in real-world deployments [79].

Momentum contrast mechanisms, originally developed for visual representation learning, have been successfully adapted for physiological time series through frameworks such as TS-MoCo. By maintaining a dynamic queue of encoded physiological signal representations and updating the key encoder via exponential moving average, TS-MoCo constructs a large and consistent pool of negative samples without requiring large batch sizes. This adaptation to temporal physiological modalities enables effective contrastive pretraining on EEG, electrocardiography, and other biosignal data, with learned representations transferring well to downstream tasks such as sleep stage scoring and arrhythmia detection [63].

Generalized sensing frameworks such as Sense and Learn aim to develop universal self-supervised pretraining strategies applicable across diverse sensor modalities and deployment scenarios. Rather than designing modality-specific pretext tasks, these frameworks define generic transformation-based objectives that can operate over heterogeneous sensing streams, enabling a single pretrained model to be fine-tuned across different activity recognition datasets and sensor configurations. This generalization capability is particularly valuable in cross-subject and cross-dataset transfer scenarios where the distribution of sensor data varies substantially, echoing the domain-agnostic aspirations seen in multimodal SSL benchmarks such as DABS discussed in the preceding subsection.

Beyond classification accuracy, recent studies highlight that self-supervised pretraining can substantially improve fairness in sensor-based human-centric applications. When compared to supervised counterparts, SSL-pretrained models exhibit more balanced performance across different demographic subgroups, suggesting that representations learned without explicit labels tend to encode less biased features [150]. This property is especially valuable in high-stakes healthcare applications such as disease monitoring and patient stratification, where equitable model performance across populations is essential.

Overall, self-supervised learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for HAR and physiological signal analysis, addressing the annotation bottleneck inherent to sensor data collection while producing representations that generalize broadly across tasks, subjects, and sensor modalities, with further benefits in terms of model fairness and robustness under limited supervision. The temporal structure and sequential nature of sensor data exploited by these SSL methods share conceptual foundations with the state representation learning challenges encountered in reinforcement learning and robotics, to which we turn in the following subsection.

### 5.8 Reinforcement Learning and Robotics

## Reinforcement Learning and Robotics

Self-supervised representation learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for improving sample efficiency and reducing the reliance on environment-specific labels in reinforcement learning (RL) and robotic control. Just as sensor-based HAR and physiological signal analysis exploit temporal structure through SSL to learn generalizable representations with minimal supervision, RL agents operating in complex environments face an analogous challenge: they must simultaneously learn to perceive their environment and optimize long-horizon behavioral policies, a dual challenge that typically demands enormous quantities of environment interactions. Self-supervised pretraining decouples representation learning from policy optimization, enabling agents to develop rich, transferable state encoders before or alongside policy learning, and producing foundations that echo the sequential and embodied nature of the activity recognition settings discussed in the preceding subsection.

A central application of SSL in RL involves learning compact, task-agnostic state representations from raw sensory observations such as image pixels. Contrastive predictive coding (CPC) has been applied to sequential decision-making problems, where temporal prediction objectives train encoders to capture the latent dynamics of environment transitions. By learning to predict future latent states from current observations, CPC-trained encoders provide structured representations that facilitate credit assignment and generalization across diverse tasks, substantially improving data efficiency on standard benchmarks including Atari games and continuous control environments.

Pretrained encoders derived from SSL objectives have demonstrated strong transferability to downstream RL tasks. In the Atari domain, encoders pretrained with contrastive or predictive objectives on offline interaction data yield representations that, when used as frozen feature extractors or fine-tuned with small amounts of online experience, achieve performance competitive with or exceeding models trained from scratch with far more data. This is particularly valuable in sample-constrained regimes, such as few-shot adaptation to new games or transfer across related environments, directly paralleling the limited-label scenarios central to the few-shot and semi-supervised learning paradigms explored in the following subsection.

In continuous control settings relevant to robotics, SSL-based state encoders have been integrated into actor-critic and model-based RL frameworks. Representations learned through augmentation-invariant contrastive objectives, applied to proprioceptive and visual observations, enable agents to learn locomotion and manipulation policies with significantly fewer environment interactions than pixel-based baselines. The invariance properties induced by augmentation-based SSL align well with the requirement that control policies should generalize across minor visual and physical variations in the environment.

Self-supervised pretraining on expert or offline trajectory data offers additional benefits for behavioral cloning and offline RL. By pretraining encoders on large collections of logged interactions using masked prediction, temporal contrastive, or forward/inverse dynamics objectives, agents can extract knowledge about environment structure and action consequences without online exploration. These pretrained representations serve as strong initializations for imitation learning or offline policy optimization, reducing the distributional shift challenges that plague standard offline RL algorithms.

In robotic manipulation and navigation, SSL methods have been used to learn visual representations from egocentric video streams, robot camera feeds, and paired sensorimotor data. Pretext tasks such as temporal order prediction, future frame prediction, and cross-view consistency learning exploit the rich temporal and spatial structure inherent in robotic interaction data, producing representations that capture object affordances, spatial layouts, and action-relevant features. These representations transfer effectively to downstream grasping, pushing, and navigation tasks, enabling robots to acquire new skills with minimal additional supervision.

The integration of SSL with model-based RL is another productive direction, where self-supervised world models learn to predict latent state transitions and rewards from compact representations. By combining reconstruction, contrastive, and predictive objectives within a unified latent space, such models support efficient planning and policy search without requiring dense reward signals. Overall, self-supervised representation learning substantially reduces the annotation and interaction burden in RL and robotics, opening pathways toward more generalizable and sample-efficient autonomous systems. This theme of learning effectively under label and data scarcity carries directly into the following subsection on few-shot and semi-supervised learning, where SSL pretraining similarly serves as the critical enabler for strong performance with minimal labeled examples.

### 5.9 Few-Shot and Semi-Supervised Learning

## Few-Shot and Semi-Supervised Learning

Self-supervised pre-training has emerged as a powerful enabler for few-shot and semi-supervised learning scenarios, where the availability of labeled examples is severely limited. By learning rich, transferable representations from large pools of unlabeled data during the pretraining phase, SSL methods dramatically reduce the dependence on costly human annotation while still achieving competitive downstream performance. This capacity for effective learning under label scarcity connects naturally to broader themes in the survey, including the robustness of SSL representations across diverse conditions and their utility as stable foundations for adaptation to new tasks.

A central theme in this area is the combination of self-supervised objectives with limited labeled data through semi-supervised frameworks. Contrastive learning methods such as SimCLR [151] naturally support semi-supervised extensions: after pretraining on unlabeled data, fine-tuning on as little as 1% of labeled ImageNet examples yields substantial improvements over purely supervised baselines trained from scratch, demonstrating that self-supervised representations encode generalizable semantic structure that is highly sample-efficient to adapt. MoCo-style frameworks similarly benefit from the quality and breadth of representations learned during contrastive pretraining, which provide strong initialization points that require far fewer labeled examples to fine-tune effectively.

Multi-task SSL frameworks extend this paradigm by simultaneously optimizing multiple self-supervised objectives alongside classification losses, enabling the model to extract complementary features that benefit downstream recognition under data-scarce conditions. The use of auxiliary pretext tasks—such as rotation prediction, patch ordering, or contrastive instance discrimination—as additional training signals alongside the primary classification objective helps regularize representations and improve generalization in the few-shot regime, since the pretext tasks effectively serve as implicit data augmentation at the feature level.

Streaming self-training approaches further extend the semi-supervised SSL paradigm to scenarios where domain-agnostic unlabeled data is continuously available. In such frameworks, pseudo-labels generated by the current model on unlabeled examples are iteratively incorporated into training, with self-supervised objectives providing a stabilizing signal that prevents representational drift and reduces the accumulation of noisy pseudo-labels. This combination of SSL pretraining with iterative self-training has demonstrated significant accuracy gains on benchmark classification tasks with very limited labeled examples, particularly when the unlabeled data distribution partially overlaps with the target domain.

For small-scale dataset regimes where even large-scale SSL pretraining may be infeasible, frameworks specifically designed for data-scarce settings exploit SSL objectives to maximize the utility of available samples. Methods like SiT leverage self-supervised pretext tasks tailored for small datasets, using careful augmentation strategies and architectural choices to prevent overfitting while still learning discriminative representations. Notably, Trip-ROMA [152] addresses the small data regime by arguing that a single negative per positive pair can suffice, and introduces random mapping strategies to mitigate overfitting—demonstrating that even minimal contrastive supervision combined with appropriate regularization yields strong few-shot and semi-supervised performance on multiple small benchmark datasets.

The treatment of false negatives—unlabeled samples from the same class mistakenly used as negatives during contrastive pretraining—is particularly critical in few-shot scenarios where the ratio of labeled to unlabeled data is small and class co-occurrence within mini-batches is frequent. Contrastive learning with negative sampling correction [122] addresses this issue by treating generated negatives as unlabeled and correcting the resulting bias, which is especially beneficial in semi-supervised settings where class imbalance and label scarcity make false negatives more prevalent. Similarly, time-series contrastive approaches that leverage instance graphs to emulate supervised contrastive learning through multiple-instance discrimination [121] have demonstrated meaningful improvements in semi-supervised classification on real-world datasets by explicitly mitigating the harmful effects of false negatives alongside class imbalance.

Collectively, these developments underscore the unique synergy between self-supervised representation learning and few-shot or semi-supervised paradigms: by leveraging the vast structure of unlabeled data through carefully designed pretext tasks and contrastive objectives, SSL frameworks provide powerful initializations and regularizers that enable effective learning even when labeled examples number in the tens or hundreds per class. This substantially closes the gap with fully supervised methods across diverse benchmarks and application domains, and points toward the broader utility of SSL as a foundation for robust generalization—a theme that carries forward into the discussion of continual learning and domain generalization in the sections that follow.

### 5.10 Continual Learning and Domain Generalization

## Continual Learning and Domain Generalization

Self-supervised representation learning has demonstrated considerable promise in both continual learning and domain generalization settings, where the ability to maintain robust, transferable features across changing data distributions is paramount. Building directly on the observation that SSL representations provide powerful initializations and regularizers for label-scarce settings, these same properties prove equally valuable when the challenge shifts from data scarcity to distribution shift and sequential task learning. Unlike supervised models that tend to overfit to the statistical regularities of a particular labeled dataset, SSL-derived representations encode more general structural properties of the data, making them inherently better suited to scenarios involving evolving data distributions or sequential task learning.

In the context of continual learning, a persistent challenge is the stability-plasticity dilemma: models must integrate new knowledge without catastrophically forgetting previously acquired representations. Self-supervised pretraining provides a strong initialization that can mitigate forgetting, as the learned features are not tied to any specific class label space. Notably, [153] demonstrates that shifting to a continual learning setting acts as a regularizer that prevents representational collapse in non-contrastive methods such as SimSiam, and that a hybrid between continual and multi-epoch training can improve linear probe accuracy by as many as 18 percentage points using ResNet-18 on ImageNet. This finding underscores the synergistic relationship between continual learning strategies and self-supervised objectives, suggesting that sequential data exposure not only helps avoid collapse but also yields richer representations.

From a Bayesian perspective, continual learning regularization techniques such as Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) provide a principled mechanism for preserving the knowledge encoded in SSL-pretrained models during downstream fine-tuning. [154] demonstrates that applying EWC regularization during fine-tuning of SSL backbones such as DINO-pretrained ViT-B/16 effectively constrains the learned posterior to remain close to the initial SSL model, improving worst sub-group performance on challenging benchmarks including Waterbirds and CelebA. This reinterpretation of SSL fine-tuning under the lens of Bayesian continual learning offers both theoretical grounding and practical gains, particularly in settings where fine-tuning data may exhibit label imbalance or spurious correlations.

Domain generalization—the ability to perform well on unseen target domains without access to target data during training—is another frontier where SSL representations have shown considerable advantage. Because SSL objectives encourage learning augmentation-invariant and structurally meaningful features, the resulting representations tend to capture domain-agnostic semantic content rather than domain-specific surface statistics. This property makes SSL-pretrained models more robust to distribution shift compared to their fully supervised counterparts, which can latch onto domain-specific shortcuts present in labeled training data. The same augmentation-invariant features that facilitate few-shot generalization thus also serve as a shield against spurious correlations when domains shift.

Across these use cases, a consistent theme emerges: SSL representations serve as a stable, domain-agnostic foundation that can be adapted to new tasks and environments with minimal degradation of previously acquired knowledge. The interplay between continual learning regularization, non-contrastive SSL objectives, and Bayesian fine-tuning frameworks represents a particularly fruitful direction for building models that generalize reliably across the diverse and evolving data distributions encountered in real-world deployment. This notion of SSL as a universal, stable foundation carries forward naturally into the following discussion of cross-domain benchmarking, where the transferability of SSL representations is systematically evaluated across a broad spectrum of modalities and downstream tasks.

### 5.11 Transfer Learning Evaluation and Benchmarking Across Domains

Evaluating the transferability of self-supervised representations across diverse downstream tasks and domains is critical for understanding the practical utility of SSL methods and guiding future development. Building on the observation that SSL representations serve as a stable, domain-agnostic foundation—as established in the context of continual learning and domain generalization—a comprehensive benchmarking effort must examine how pre-trained encoders generalize beyond the domains on which they were trained, spanning natural images, medical imagery, satellite data, audio, and beyond.

A central challenge in SSL benchmarking is the design of fair, standardized evaluation protocols. Linear probing, where a linear classifier is trained on frozen features, and full fine-tuning, where all model parameters are updated, serve as the two primary evaluation paradigms. These protocols reveal complementary properties: linear probing tests the intrinsic separability of learned representations, while fine-tuning measures the adaptability of pre-trained weights to new tasks. The quality of representations learned by masked autoencoders such as MAE [155] has been extensively evaluated under both protocols on ImageNet-1K, establishing strong baselines for subsequent methods.

Domain-agnostic benchmarking has emerged as a particularly important direction. The DABS benchmark evaluates SSL methods across multiple modalities including images, text, audio, and sensor data, highlighting that methods designed for natural images do not always transfer effectively to other domains. Modality-agnostic frameworks such as MetaMAE [156] have been specifically proposed and evaluated on DABS, demonstrating that meta-learning-based enhancements to the masked autoencoder paradigm can significantly improve cross-modal transferability compared to vanilla baselines. This work underlines the importance of designing SSL methods that are not overly tailored to a single domain if universal pre-training is the goal.

For remote sensing and satellite imagery, domain-specific adaptations of SSL methods have shown considerable promise. Feature Guided MAE (FG-MAE) [157] evaluates the transferability of masked autoencoder pre-training to SAR and multispectral remote sensing images, demonstrating that reconstructing domain-specific features such as Histograms of Oriented Gradients (HOG) and Normalized Difference Indices (NDI) substantially improves downstream performance compared to pixel-level reconstruction. These findings suggest that for specialized imagery domains with distinct statistical properties, naive application of SSL methods designed for natural images is suboptimal, and domain-informed reconstruction targets are necessary for effective transfer.

For medical imaging, multi-modal masked autoencoders have been explored specifically in histopathology. The MMAE framework [158] demonstrates that leveraging the compositional structure of H&E-stained whole slide images through multi-modal masked autoencoding outperforms standard supervised baselines and other SSL methods on tissue phenotyping tasks with very limited labeled data, underscoring the value of domain-adapted SSL pre-training for biological imagery benchmarks.

Transfer from video to image domains and vice versa has also been systematically evaluated. ViC-MAE [159] presents transfer learning evaluations spanning both ImageNet-1K and video benchmarks such as Something-Something-v2, showing that combining contrastive and masked autoencoding objectives facilitates more balanced transfer across image and video domains. This cross-domain evaluation highlights that SSL methods combining complementary objectives can better generalize across heterogeneous task distributions than single-objective approaches.

For audio and speech domains, Audio-MAE [160] and MSM-MAE [161] provide systematic evaluations across multiple audio classification and speech recognition benchmarks, including CREMA-D, LibriCount, and the HEAR 2021 NeurIPS Challenge suite. These evaluations reveal that masked spectrogram modeling with MAE-style architectures achieves competitive or state-of-the-art performance across diverse audio tasks, establishing strong SSL baselines for audio representation transfer.

At the architecture level, large-scale evaluations have shown that scaling model capacity consistently improves transfer performance. MAE [155] demonstrates that a ViT-Huge model pre-trained on ImageNet-1K achieves 87.8% top-1 accuracy with fine-tuning, showcasing promising scaling behavior. Similarly, contrastive masked autoencoder methods such as CAN [142] demonstrate superior linear probe performance over both SimCLR and MAE on larger uncurated datasets like JFT-300M, suggesting that combining contrastive and generative objectives yields representations that generalize more robustly to challenging transfer settings.

Supervised extensions of MAE, such as SupMAE [162], provide complementary insights by showing that incorporating labeled supervision into the masked autoencoder framework improves robustness and transferability to ImageNet variants and downstream domains, while requiring significantly less compute than standard supervised pre-training. This controlled comparison between supervised and self-supervised pre-training regimes is valuable for understanding when SSL is sufficient and when labeled supervision still provides meaningful gains.

Collectively, these benchmarking studies reveal both the strengths and limitations of current SSL methods for universal pre-training. Strengths include strong transfer on natural image classification and detection tasks, competitive performance with supervised pre-training in many settings, and effective generalization to specialized domains when domain-specific reconstruction targets or multi-modal signals are incorporated. Limitations include sensitivity to domain shift when using methods designed for natural images on specialized data such as SAR, histopathology, or audio, the high computational cost of large-scale pre-training, and the need for domain-specific augmentation strategies and reconstruction targets. Future progress in universal SSL pre-training will likely require more principled domain-agnostic frameworks, richer multi-modal training signals, and standardized evaluation suites that span a truly diverse range of modalities and downstream tasks.

## 6 Challenges, Open Problems, and Future Directions

### 6.1 Scalability and Computational Efficiency

## 6.1 Scalability and Computational Efficiency

Scalability and computational efficiency represent one of the most pressing challenges in self-supervised representation learning. While SSL eliminates the need for expensive human annotations, it often substitutes annotation costs with substantial computational costs, requiring large-scale pretraining on massive datasets with powerful hardware infrastructure that remains inaccessible to many research groups and practitioners. This section examines the nature of these costs and surveys the strategies that have been proposed to make SSL more tractable, setting the stage for the related discussion of robustness challenges in Section 6.2.

### High Computational and Memory Costs of Pretraining

The computational burden of SSL pretraining is particularly severe. Large-scale contrastive and generative methods typically require training on hundreds of millions of images with large batch sizes, extensive data augmentation pipelines, and multi-GPU or multi-node distributed training setups. As noted in surveys of the field [14], compute cost is a critical practical consideration that significantly limits the accessibility and reproducibility of state-of-the-art SSL methods. For speech and audio modalities, this challenge is even more pronounced, as highlighted by studies on efficiency-oriented approaches [5], where the high energy consumption and resource requirements of large self-supervised models limit deployment, constrain the size of training datasets, and restrict the number of research groups capable of conducting meaningful experiments. Beyond direct training costs, the environmental impact of training increasingly large SSL models raises important sustainability concerns that the community must address as a matter of priority.

### Data-Efficient SSL: Identifying the Most Informative Examples

One promising strategy to reduce the computational burden of SSL is to focus pretraining on the most informative subset of available data rather than consuming entire large-scale datasets indiscriminately. This idea parallels active learning principles applied to the SSL regime. Work on coreset sampling from open-set data [108] demonstrates that carefully selecting a subset of unlabeled data that is most representative of the downstream target distribution can substantially improve representation quality while reducing overall pretraining costs. Specifically, the SimCore algorithm identifies a coreset by measuring distances between open-set samples and the target domain in latent space, achieving strong performance across eleven fine-grained datasets with a fraction of the full data. Similarly, benchmarking studies [140] have explored how SSL scales across different data sizes, revealing that the relationship between data volume and downstream performance is not always monotonically beneficial, suggesting that smarter data selection can match or exceed brute-force scaling. These findings motivate the development of principled data curation strategies for SSL that balance informativeness, diversity, and computational budget.

### Scaled-Down SSL Paradigms

Reducing the scale of the SSL pipeline itself—through smaller input resolutions, lighter architectures, or fewer training epochs—is another avenue for improving efficiency. Research on hybrid approaches such as combining self-supervised pretraining with compact architectures [19] demonstrates that effective representations can be learned even on small datasets with reduced computational resources when augmentation strategies and network designs are carefully chosen. Such work highlights that the conventional wisdom of "bigger is always better" in SSL does not universally hold, and that thoughtful architecture and training design can yield competitive results with far lower compute expenditure.

### Sustainable SSL Frameworks: Building on Existing Pretrained Models

A particularly impactful direction for sustainable SSL involves leveraging existing pretrained models as initialization rather than training from scratch, thereby avoiding the prohibitive cost of full pretraining. The iBoot framework [163] exemplifies this approach in the video domain, where a strong image-based SSL model pretrained on a general domain is used to bootstrap video SSL, allowing the video encoder to learn spatial and temporal information more efficiently with fewer epochs and smaller batch sizes. This paradigm of building upon existing representations rather than reinventing them from scratch aligns with the broader trend toward foundation models and transfer learning, where the amortized cost of large-scale pretraining is spread across many downstream applications.

### Trade-offs Between Model Size, Training Cost, and Representation Quality

A fundamental tension in SSL is the trade-off between model capacity, training cost, and the quality and generalizability of learned representations. Larger models and longer training schedules generally yield better downstream performance, but at rapidly increasing computational expense. Benchmarking studies [140] reveal important insights about these trade-offs, showing that simply scaling standard SSL methods does not always produce representations with strong high-level semantic content, and that the "hardness" of pretext tasks plays a critical role in determining whether additional compute translates to genuine representation quality improvements. This finding suggests that efficiency gains should be pursued not only through hardware and data optimizations, but also through the design of more informationally rich and challenging pretext tasks that extract maximum learning signal from each training example.

Overall, addressing scalability and computational efficiency in SSL requires a multi-pronged approach: developing smarter data selection strategies, designing architectures and objectives that extract more signal from less data, building upon existing pretrained models rather than always training from scratch, and establishing rigorous benchmarks that evaluate performance relative to computational cost rather than absolute performance alone. Progress on these fronts will be essential for making SSL practically accessible and sustainable across diverse application domains. As we will discuss in the following section, many of these efficiency considerations intersect with robustness challenges—particularly when limited data availability compounds both the computational and distributional difficulties that arise in real-world deployment.

### 6.2 Robustness to Dataset Imbalance and Distribution Shift

## 6.2 Robustness to Dataset Imbalance and Distribution Shift

Real-world data rarely conforms to the balanced, curated distributions assumed by most self-supervised learning benchmarks. These distributional challenges have direct implications for both the efficiency concerns raised in Section 6.1 and the model compression considerations addressed in Section 6.3: limited or skewed data compounds the computational difficulties of pretraining, and representations that are fragile to distribution shift will remain fragile even after compression. Two closely related but distinct challenges arise in practice: class imbalance or long-tailed distributions, and domain shift between pretraining and deployment. Understanding how SSL methods behave under these conditions is critical for their adoption in real-world applications.

### Long-Tailed and Imbalanced Distributions

In standard supervised learning, class imbalance is well-known to degrade performance on tail (minority) classes, as models tend to overfit to the dominant head classes. Self-supervised learning has been posited as a more robust alternative, since it does not directly optimize class-discriminative objectives and therefore may not inherently favor majority classes. Indeed, several studies suggest that SSL representations are relatively more robust to class imbalance compared to supervised counterparts, as the pretext tasks operate over unlabeled data without explicit exposure to class frequency information [15].

However, this advantage is not absolute. When the underlying data distribution is heavily skewed, contrastive learning methods tend to produce feature spaces dominated by head-class samples, as the majority of negative pairs are drawn from frequent categories. This implicitly biases the learned embedding space, causing tail-class representations to cluster poorly and collapse into regions of the feature space that are difficult to separate downstream. The phenomenon of dimensional collapse—where representations span only a low-dimensional subspace—is particularly exacerbated in imbalanced settings, as minority classes contribute few samples to the batch statistics that most SSL objectives depend upon [25]. Notably, this same collapse phenomenon resurfaces in the context of knowledge distillation, where capacity-constrained student models are similarly susceptible to degenerate representations when trained on skewed data distributions, as discussed in Section 6.3.

To mitigate these issues, several directions have been explored. Geometric harmonization approaches seek to equalize the contribution of different samples or classes to the overall feature space geometry, applying re-weighted regularization that up-weights tail-class samples during the computation of variance-covariance penalties or contrastive objectives. These methods are inspired by classical techniques from imbalanced supervised learning but must be adapted to the label-free SSL setting, where class membership is not directly available. Alternative strategies leverage clustering-based SSL methods to first identify dominant clusters and then apply rebalancing within the pseudo-label assignment step, helping to prevent the collapse of minority clusters [15].

The relative merits of contrastive versus generative SSL objectives under imbalance also warrant further investigation. Generative approaches such as masked autoencoders reconstruct input data without explicit pairwise comparisons, and may be less susceptible to majority-class dominance since reconstruction quality does not inherently scale with class frequency [24]. Nevertheless, if the pretraining corpus is heavily imbalanced, generative models may allocate more representational capacity to frequent patterns, leaving rare ones underrepresented. This consideration also bears on the data curation strategies discussed in Section 6.1: principled coreset selection that ensures sufficient coverage of minority patterns is valuable not only for computational efficiency but also for representational robustness.

### Distribution Shift and Domain Adaptation

Beyond class imbalance, distribution shift between the pretraining domain and the deployment domain represents a fundamental challenge for transferring SSL representations. When pretraining data differs substantially from the target domain in terms of appearance statistics, object scales, or semantic categories—as is common when moving from natural ImageNet imagery to medical, satellite, or industrial data—the representations learned by SSL may not transfer effectively [22]. This fragility is particularly consequential in the context of model compression: as discussed in Section 6.3, even when a distilled student model performs comparably to its teacher on in-distribution benchmarks, degraded out-of-domain performance often emerges precisely because the compressed model has absorbed the teacher's representational biases along with its useful features.

One key source of fragility is the augmentation policy used during pretraining. Standard augmentations such as random cropping, color jittering, and horizontal flipping are designed for natural images, and the invariances they induce may be inappropriate or even harmful for other domains. For example, color invariance is desirable for object recognition but destructive for pathology or remote sensing tasks where color encodes diagnostic information. This motivates augmentation-robust contrastive learning approaches that adapt the augmentation strategy to the target domain, either through domain-specific handcrafting or through automatic augmentation policy search [109]. Augmentation diversity also emerges as a promising lever for preserving out-of-domain transferability during distillation, connecting the robustness strategies explored here to the distillation challenges examined in the following section.

Transferable contrastive learning for domain adaptation aims to bridge the gap between source and target distributions by incorporating domain alignment objectives alongside the standard contrastive loss. These methods seek to learn representations that are simultaneously discriminative within each domain and invariant across domains, drawing on ideas from domain adversarial training and optimal transport. However, designing contrastive objectives that successfully balance these competing requirements without degrading performance on either domain remains an open problem.

The analysis of contrastive learning transferability across diverse domains reveals that performance degrades substantially on fine-grained classification tasks and domains with significant texture-shape trade-offs [22; 109]. These findings highlight that the invariances learned by SSL methods on large-scale internet imagery do not universally generalize, and that principled methods for adapting SSL pretraining to target distributions are needed. Future work should address how to incorporate lightweight target-domain signals—such as a small set of unlabeled target samples—to recalibrate the pretraining augmentation pipeline and improve robustness to distribution shift across the full spectrum of real-world deployment scenarios. As the following section will discuss, these same concerns about out-of-domain generalization become even more acute when models must additionally be compressed for deployment in resource-constrained environments.

### 6.3 Knowledge Distillation and Model Compression for SSL

## 6.3 Knowledge Distillation and Model Compression for SSL

Knowledge distillation and model compression have emerged as critical challenges in deploying self-supervised learning (SSL) models in resource-constrained real-world environments. While large SSL models achieve impressive representation quality, their computational and memory demands make them impractical for edge devices, mobile applications, and latency-sensitive systems. This challenge is closely related to the robustness concerns discussed in the preceding section: just as distribution shift and class imbalance complicate the deployment of SSL representations, so too does the gap between the scale at which SSL models are most effective and the scale at which they must operate in practice. Bridging the gap between the representational power of large pretrained SSL models and the efficiency requirements of deployment settings therefore constitutes a significant open research problem with direct implications for real-world applicability.

A central approach involves task-agnostic distillation, where a smaller student model is trained to mimic the representations of a larger SSL-pretrained teacher without requiring labeled data or task-specific fine-tuning. This paradigm is particularly appealing because the distilled model inherits the general-purpose nature of the teacher's representations, making it applicable across diverse downstream tasks. However, maintaining this generalizability under out-of-domain settings remains a persistent challenge, as student models trained to replicate teacher outputs on a fixed pretraining dataset may fail to preserve the breadth of knowledge needed for transfer to novel domains and tasks [49]. This limitation echoes the distribution shift problems discussed previously, but manifests specifically as a consequence of capacity reduction rather than domain mismatch in pretraining data alone.

The choice of student architecture exerts a profound influence on distillation outcomes. Heterogeneous teacher-student pairs, where the student employs a fundamentally different architecture than the teacher, introduce representation alignment difficulties. For instance, distilling from a Vision Transformer-based teacher into a lightweight convolutional student requires careful consideration of how local versus global feature structures are matched across architectures. Multi-mode online knowledge distillation frameworks address this challenge by enabling collaborative learning among multiple heterogeneous models simultaneously, rather than relying on a fixed pretrained teacher. In such frameworks, models with different inductive biases mutually supervise one another during training, facilitating knowledge transfer across architectural boundaries and potentially improving the robustness of the resulting compact models.

A particularly subtle failure mode in compressing SSL models is semantic collapse in lightweight student networks. When smaller models are trained under distillation objectives that emphasize instance-level similarity, they may produce degenerate representations where semantically distinct concepts are mapped to nearby points in the embedding space — a phenomenon that parallels the dimensional collapse discussed in the context of imbalanced pretraining data. Relation knowledge distillation approaches address this by transferring not only individual representation vectors but also the relational structure among samples, preserving pairwise or higher-order similarity relationships between embeddings. This relational transfer helps lightweight models maintain semantic discriminability even when their capacity is severely constrained.

Distillation from multiple self-supervised teachers offers another promising direction. Rather than compressing knowledge from a single pretrained model, comprehensive distillation frameworks aggregate complementary representations from multiple SSL teachers trained with diverse objectives — such as contrastive, masked prediction, and clustering-based methods — into a unified compact student. This multi-teacher paradigm can yield richer representations in the student than any single teacher alone, as different SSL objectives encode complementary aspects of the input, such as global semantics, local structure, and temporal dynamics [103]. However, balancing contributions from multiple teachers and resolving potential conflicts between their representation spaces remains an open methodological challenge.

The generalizability of distilled SSL models under distribution shift and out-of-domain settings constitutes perhaps the most pressing open problem in this area. Empirical evidence suggests that even when a distilled model performs comparably to its teacher on in-distribution benchmarks, it may exhibit substantially degraded performance on data from different domains [82]. This discrepancy arises because distillation objectives typically optimize for fidelity on the pretraining data distribution, potentially sacrificing the breadth of generalization that large SSL models achieve through exposure to diverse training signals. Developing distillation strategies that explicitly preserve out-of-domain transferability — perhaps through augmentation diversity, domain-randomized distillation, or regularization of the student's feature covariance structure — represents a critical avenue for future research, and connects directly to the augmentation-based robustness strategies discussed in the preceding section.

Efficiency-oriented SSL frameworks that build upon existing pretrained models rather than training from scratch further alleviate the computational burden associated with self-supervised pretraining. By leveraging already-pretrained SSL representations as initializations or frozen feature extractors, such approaches reduce the cost of adapting SSL to new domains and enable more sustainable deployment pipelines. Nevertheless, comprehensive benchmarks that jointly evaluate compression ratio, downstream task performance, out-of-domain generalization, and computational cost remain scarce, hindering principled comparison and progress in this important subfield. Understanding what is preserved and what is lost during compression also raises fundamental interpretability questions — questions that will be taken up directly in the following section on the interpretability and explainability of learned representations.

### 6.4 Interpretability and Explainability of Learned Representations

## 6.4 Interpretability and Explainability of Learned Representations

Despite the remarkable empirical successes of self-supervised learning (SSL), understanding *what* these models actually learn—and *why* their representations transfer effectively—remains one of the most pressing open challenges in the field. The opacity of SSL encoders raises fundamental questions about trustworthiness, debugging, and principled design, motivating a growing body of interpretability and explainability research. These questions are also closely entangled with the compression and deployment challenges examined in the preceding section: understanding what information is preserved or discarded during distillation is inseparable from understanding what information SSL representations encode in the first place. Equally, as we will discuss in the following section, the structure of learned representations is itself shaped by the modality of the input data, making interpretability analyses an important foundation for extending SSL to heterogeneous and specialized data types.

**Empirical Analyses of Representation Spaces.** A central approach to understanding SSL representations involves systematic empirical comparisons across diverse objectives and architectures. Studies have examined how contrastive, non-contrastive, generative, and clustering-based SSL methods differ in the geometric structure of their embedding spaces, revealing that different objectives encode qualitatively distinct information despite achieving similar linear probing accuracies. The geometry of the embedding space—including its intrinsic dimensionality, isotropy, and clustering structure—has emerged as a useful diagnostic lens. Research on evaluation protocols for SSL [43] has highlighted that standard linear probing metrics can be sensitive to hyperparameters and may fail to capture the true transferability of representations, underscoring the need for more robust and interpretable evaluation frameworks. This observation is particularly consequential for the modality-specific settings examined in the following section, where evaluation protocols developed for vision and language tasks are often insufficient proxies for representational quality in specialized domains.

**Probing Studies and Encoded Properties.** Probing classifiers have become a standard tool for investigating what information is encoded at different layers of SSL encoders. These studies train lightweight classifiers on frozen representations to predict various properties—semantic category membership, spatial layout, temporal ordering, syntactic structure in language, and activity patterns in sensor data—without updating the encoder. Such analyses have revealed that SSL representations tend to encode rich semantic clustering structure even without explicit label supervision. Investigations of self-supervised speech representations [164] have demonstrated that redundancy reduction objectives accelerate learning and promote cross-domain transfer, but face limitations in fully disentangling and factorizing learned codes into modular, interpretable components. This underscores a persistent gap between the information richness of SSL representations and their interpretability in terms of human-understandable factors. Probing studies on projection heads [40; 42] have further illuminated the division of labor between the encoder and projector, showing that the projector primarily handles augmentation invariance—often by discarding information—while the encoder retains more semantically complete and transferable features. Critically, this division of labor also bears directly on the compression strategies examined in the preceding section: understanding which components of a pretrained SSL model encode transferable versus task-specific information is essential for principled decisions about what to distill and what to discard.

**Saliency-Based and Attribution Methods.** Saliency-based explanation methods, originally developed for supervised models, have been adapted to assess and compare the quality of SSL representations. These methods generate input-space attribution maps that highlight which regions of an input are most influential for a given representation. When applied to SSL encoders, saliency maps can reveal whether representations focus on semantically meaningful regions (e.g., object foregrounds in images) or spurious low-level cues. Empirical findings suggest that certain SSL objectives—particularly those using strong augmentation policies—tend to produce representations with broader, more spatially distributed attention, whereas weaker augmentation regimes may lead to representations that focus narrowly on texture or color statistics. The interplay between augmentation strength and saliency patterns connects directly to the geometric relationship between the encoder and projector discussed below, and also foreshadows the critical role of augmentation design in shaping representations across the diverse modalities examined in the following section.

**Unsupervised Quality Scoring and Label-Free Indicators.** A practically important interpretability challenge is assessing SSL representation quality *without* access to downstream labels—a concern that is equally relevant to the model compression setting, where labeled evaluation data may be unavailable at deployment time. Several directions have been explored. Unsupervised quality scoring methods aim to predict which samples are likely to be misclassified under a downstream linear probe, enabling practitioners to identify failure modes and assess representation quality in the absence of labeled evaluation sets. Another promising direction involves power-law analyses of feature covariance spectra: the eigenvalue distribution of the feature covariance matrix encodes information about the effective dimensionality and structure of the representation space, and has been proposed as a label-free proxy for downstream generalization capability. Representations with more uniformly distributed eigenvalue spectra—indicative of higher effective rank—tend to generalize better across tasks, connecting to theoretical work on dimensional collapse [41].

**Geometric Relationship Between Encoders, Projectors, and Augmentation Strength.** The geometric relationship between the encoder representation, the projection head output, and the data augmentation policy has received increasing theoretical and empirical attention. Research [41] has revealed that as augmentation strength increases, the projector becomes increasingly driven to project data into a low-dimensional manifold—effectively discarding information about the data—while the encoder retains a richer, higher-dimensional representation. This explains the well-known empirical observation that discarding the projector and using encoder representations for downstream tasks yields superior transfer performance. Complementary work [42] has proposed modified SSL architectures (e.g., shortcut connections between encoder and projector outputs) to better balance uniformity and semantic retention, offering a more interpretable decomposition of the roles played by different architectural components. These geometric insights also carry practical implications for distillation: as noted in the preceding section, they clarify why the encoder—rather than the full encoder-projector pipeline—is typically the appropriate target for knowledge transfer to compact student models.

**The Transferability–Interpretability Connection.** Several studies have noted a tension between optimizing for transfer performance and achieving interpretable representations. For instance, adding MLP projectors during pretraining—whether supervised or self-supervised—improves downstream transferability by retaining intra-class variation and reducing feature distribution mismatch [52], but may simultaneously make the features less interpretable by mixing semantic information across dimensions. Similarly, approaches that control the dimensionality of the backbone's final layer as a lever to mitigate pretraining bias [51] improve transfer but do not necessarily improve interpretability of the resulting features. This transferability–interpretability tension takes on additional significance when SSL is extended to new modalities, as the following section discusses: without interpretable representations, it is difficult to diagnose why SSL methods that work well on standard benchmarks fail to transfer their advantages to specialized domains.

**Open Challenges.** Despite progress, significant challenges remain. First, most existing interpretability tools were developed for supervised models and do not account for the absence of labels during SSL training; adapting them to the SSL setting requires new theoretical frameworks. Second, probing classifiers evaluate representations in terms of predefined properties, but SSL representations may encode information that is not captured by any human-specified probe. Third, there is currently no consensus on which geometric or statistical properties of SSL representations are most predictive of downstream generalization, making it difficult to use interpretability analyses for principled SSL design. Fourth, interpretability in multimodal and cross-modal SSL settings is substantially more complex, as representations encode shared structure across heterogeneous data types—a challenge that becomes even more acute in the novel modalities discussed in the following section, where standard evaluation and probing protocols developed for vision and language tasks may be wholly inapplicable. Addressing these challenges will require tighter integration between theoretical analyses, empirical probing methodologies, and the development of new label-free quality metrics that are both informative and practically accessible, and that generalize beyond the modalities for which current interpretability tools were designed.

### 6.5 Extending SSL to New Modalities and Heterogeneous Data

## 6.5 Extending SSL to New Modalities and Heterogeneous Data

While self-supervised learning has achieved remarkable success in vision and natural language processing, extending these techniques to heterogeneous and specialized data types remains a significant open challenge. This challenge is not merely technical but also conceptual: just as interpretability analyses have revealed that SSL representations encode qualitatively distinct information depending on the objective and architecture, the structure of learned representations is even more fundamentally shaped by the modality of the input data itself. The fundamental difficulty lies in the fact that the core machinery of SSL—augmentation strategies, positive/negative pair construction, and pretext task design—was developed with image and text data in mind, and does not straightforwardly transfer to domains with radically different structural properties. Moreover, as we will discuss in the following section, deploying SSL in these new domains under non-stationary, continually evolving data streams introduces yet another layer of complexity that compounds the challenges addressed here.

**Tabular Data.** Tabular data presents unique difficulties for SSL because features are typically heterogeneous, with mixed continuous and categorical types, and lack the spatial or sequential redundancy that makes augmentation natural for images or text. Unlike pixel perturbations or token masking, which preserve semantic content, naive perturbations of tabular features can destroy meaningful relationships entirely. Approaches such as feature subsetting to create multi-view learning problems have been proposed to address this gap, but designing augmentations that respect the statistical dependencies between features remains an open research problem. Self-training with regularized pseudo-labeling has also been explored for tabular domains, though generalizing such methods across diverse table schemas and dataset sizes without task-specific tuning is still challenging [165].

**Graph-Structured and Molecular Data.** Graph data presents another frontier where standard SSL assumptions break down. Meaningful augmentations for graphs—such as node dropping, edge perturbation, or subgraph sampling—must preserve the graph's semantic and topological integrity, which is highly domain-dependent. In molecular graphs, for instance, removing a bond or atom can fundamentally alter the chemical meaning of the molecule. Self-supervised learning on graphs has been explored through contrastive, generative, and predictive paradigms [28], each with distinct trade-offs in terms of expressiveness and scalability. Persistent homology-based approaches that capture topological features of molecular structures offer a promising direction for SSL in molecular property prediction, as they provide augmentation-invariant structural descriptors that encode multi-scale geometric information not captured by standard graph neural network embeddings.

**Sensor-Based and Time Series Data.** Wearable sensor data and physiological time series are of growing importance for healthcare and activity recognition applications, yet they present distinct challenges including non-stationarity, missing data, and high inter-subject variability. SSL methods for time series have explored a variety of strategies, including predicting temporal context, contrastive learning with temporal augmentations, and generative reconstruction objectives [60; 59]. However, the choice of augmentation has an outsized impact in this domain: augmentations that preserve temporal structure and frequency content are critical, and domain-inappropriate augmentations can corrupt the supervisory signal entirely. Extending these methods to multivariate sensor streams with spatial correlations, such as EEG or multi-site accelerometry, requires augmentation strategies that jointly respect temporal, spatial, and frequency-domain properties [63]. Notably, the non-stationarity inherent to sensor-based data streams connects directly to the continual learning challenges explored in the following section, where evolving data distributions must be handled without catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired representations.

**Hyperspectral and Point Cloud Data.** High-dimensional imaging modalities such as hyperspectral remote sensing data and 3D point clouds introduce challenges of extreme dimensionality, sparse annotations, and the absence of natural augmentation policies analogous to those used in RGB imagery. Point clouds in particular lack a canonical ordering, making it difficult to apply token-masking or patch-based SSL objectives without accounting for permutation invariance. Developing SSL pretext tasks that exploit the 3D geometric structure while remaining robust to occlusion, density variation, and viewpoint change is an active area of research.

**Scientific Simulation and Specialized Domains.** Scientific domains such as computational biology, materials science, and climate modeling generate vast quantities of simulation data that are expensive to label with expert annotations. These data types often require domain-specific inductive biases in the SSL framework, and generic augmentation pipelines designed for natural images are largely inapplicable. Re-simulation-based SSL, where alternative simulation runs serve as augmented views, has been proposed as one avenue, but designing such strategies requires deep domain knowledge and is not easily automated.

**Generic and Modality-Agnostic SSL Frameworks.** A key open challenge—one that unifies the modality-specific difficulties described above—is the development of truly generic SSL frameworks that can operate across diverse modalities without requiring hand-crafted augmentation policies for each new domain. Some efforts have explored self-transformation mechanisms that learn or discover transformations from the data itself, replacing manually designed augmentations with learned ones applicable across modalities [166]. Domain-agnostic benchmarking efforts have also highlighted that SSL methods which perform well on standard visual benchmarks often fail to transfer their advantages to specialized domains, underscoring the need for modality-aware SSL design principles. This transferability gap echoes the interpretability challenges discussed in the previous section, where standard evaluation protocols developed for vision and language tasks were shown to be insufficient proxies for true representational quality in diverse settings.

In summary, extending SSL to new modalities requires rethinking every component of the SSL pipeline—from augmentation and positive pair construction to pretext task objectives and evaluation protocols—in a manner that respects the unique structural, statistical, and semantic properties of each data type. These modality-specific challenges are further magnified when data arrives in non-stationary streams, motivating the continual SSL strategies examined in the following section. Progress in this direction will be essential for realizing the full potential of SSL as a universal representation learning paradigm.

### 6.6 Continual and Lifelong Self-Supervised Learning

## 6.6 Continual and Non-Stationary Self-Supervised Learning

Deploying self-supervised learning (SSL) in dynamic, non-stationary environments where data streams arrive continuously presents a distinct set of challenges that go beyond those encountered in standard offline pretraining scenarios. As established in the preceding discussion of modality-specific challenges, many real-world data streams—including sensor-based physiological signals, evolving scientific simulations, and multi-domain corpora—are inherently non-stationary, and this non-stationarity introduces an additional layer of complexity that compounds the difficulties of extending SSL to new domains. Unlike supervised continual learning, where task boundaries are often explicit and labeled data provides a clear learning signal, continual SSL must navigate the stability-plasticity dilemma without the guidance of annotations. The model must remain plastic enough to absorb new distributional information while retaining previously acquired representations—a balance that is inherently difficult to strike in a purely self-supervised regime.

A central difficulty in continual SSL is catastrophic forgetting: as the model adapts to new data streams, previously learned representations may be overwritten or distorted, degrading performance on earlier tasks or domains. This problem is particularly acute in non-contrastive SSL methods such as BYOL and SimSiam, where the absence of explicit negative pairs makes the training objective more susceptible to representational drift over time. In such settings, continual learning constraints have been explored as regularizers to stabilize feature spaces and prevent representational collapse as the data distribution evolves. Bayesian perspectives, such as elastic weight consolidation (EWC), offer a principled mechanism for this stabilization by placing a quadratic penalty on changes to parameters deemed important for previous tasks, thereby approximating a Laplace posterior over model weights. These approaches effectively slow the forgetting of previously learned features while allowing selective adaptation to new information.

Architectural considerations also play a crucial role in managing the stability-plasticity trade-off. Empirical analyses suggest that batch normalization layers tend to absorb distributional shifts and can be leveraged to control plasticity, while convolutional layers tend to encode more stable, task-agnostic features suited to long-term retention. Selectively updating different components of the network—for instance, through branch-tuning strategies that introduce lightweight adapter modules for new data streams while freezing the core encoder—allows for efficient continual adaptation without requiring access to previously observed data. Such strategies are especially appealing in privacy-sensitive or storage-constrained settings where rehearsal-based approaches, which rely on storing and replaying past examples, are not feasible.

The challenge of continual adaptation is further compounded in multimodal and multi-domain SSL settings, where new modalities or domains may arrive incrementally [29]. In these scenarios, the model must not only avoid forgetting previously learned unimodal or cross-modal representations but also generalize to newly introduced data types without full retraining. Self-reinforcing and domain-adaptive frameworks that iteratively refine representations from emerging modalities offer one promising avenue [167], though scaling these approaches to diverse and heterogeneous data streams remains an open problem. This challenge is closely related to the question of how SSL objectives, augmentation strategies, and architectural choices interact with distributional shift—a question that, as the following section will discuss, also sits at the heart of developing rigorous theoretical foundations for SSL.

Beyond architectural and algorithmic interventions, a theoretical understanding of why and when continual SSL succeeds or fails is still nascent. The interaction between SSL objectives, data augmentation policies, and non-stationarity is poorly understood, and principled guidelines for designing continual SSL systems are lacking. Benchmarks that rigorously evaluate continual SSL across diverse domains, such as the domain-agnostic evaluation framework proposed in [76], provide a useful starting point but do not yet fully capture the complexity of real-world non-stationary data distributions. Addressing these theoretical and empirical gaps represents one of the most important open frontiers in self-supervised representation learning research—and one that must ultimately be resolved through the kind of rigorous theoretical analysis examined in the section that follows.

### 6.7 Theoretical Foundations and Principled SSL Design

Despite the remarkable empirical successes of self-supervised learning, rigorous theoretical foundations that explain *why* these methods work and *how* they should be designed remain elusive. Bridging this gap between practice and theory constitutes one of the most pressing open challenges in the field—one that must be resolved before SSL can be elevated from an empirically driven discipline to a theoretically principled science. Several promising directions have emerged in recent years, each offering a distinct lens through which the behavior of SSL methods can be understood and improved.

**Spectral and Manifold Learning Perspectives.** A growing body of work seeks to connect SSL objectives to classical spectral embedding methods such as Laplacian Eigenmaps, Multidimensional Scaling, and kernel PCA. These analyses reveal that contrastive and non-contrastive objectives can be interpreted as minimizing globally or locally defined embedding losses on an implicit similarity graph defined by the augmentation distribution. Such spectral perspectives not only unify disparate SSL algorithms under a common theoretical lens but also yield closed-form characterizations of optimal representations in the linear and kernel regimes, offering principled guidance for architecture and objective design.

**Probabilistic Latent Variable Models.** Probabilistic frameworks that model the data-generating process offer another avenue for grounding SSL. By positing latent variable models in which multiple views are generated from a shared latent cause, contrastive and clustering-based objectives can be derived from first principles as approximate posterior inference procedures. These generative interpretations clarify the role of augmentation design, encoder capacity, and projection heads in shaping learned representations, and establish theoretical conditions under which the learned representations align with semantically meaningful factors of variation [34].

**Information-Theoretic Frameworks.** Information-theoretic analyses, particularly those grounded in mutual information maximization and partial information decomposition, provide formal tools for understanding the trade-off between capturing information shared across views (redundancy) and information unique to each view (synergy) [34]. These frameworks reveal that naive mutual information maximization objectives may discard task-relevant synergistic information while retaining task-irrelevant redundant information. Partial information decomposition offers a principled remedy by explicitly balancing synergistic and redundant components, pointing toward new objective functions that are better aligned with downstream task requirements.

**Power-Law Analyses of Feature Covariance Spectra.** Recent empirical investigations suggest that the eigenvalue spectrum of learned feature covariance matrices follows power-law scaling, and that the exponent of this power law correlates with downstream generalization performance. This observation opens the possibility of label-free quality indicators for SSL representations, enabling practitioners to diagnose representation quality without labeled evaluation data and providing a theoretically motivated diagnostic tool for comparing SSL methods and hyperparameter configurations.

**Generalization and Transfer Theory.** Theoretical studies on the generalization capabilities of SSL representations remain in their infancy. Key open questions include: under what augmentation distributions and data conditions do SSL representations achieve near-optimal downstream accuracy, how do encoder architecture and inductive biases interact with the SSL objective to determine transferability, and what theoretical quantities govern the gap between self-supervised and supervised pre-training under domain shift [109; 82; 168]. Addressing these questions requires developing generalization error bounds that account for the multi-view structure of SSL, the non-i.i.d. nature of augmented pairs, and the hierarchical architecture of modern encoders.

**Toward Principled SSL Design.** Perhaps the most important open challenge is translating theoretical insights into actionable design guidelines. Current SSL practice relies heavily on empirical trial-and-error, with practitioners tuning augmentation pipelines, projection head dimensions, batch sizes, and learning rate schedules through expensive ablation studies. Theoretical frameworks that predict the effect of these design choices on representation quality would dramatically reduce this search cost and enable more systematic progress. For instance, unifying gradient analyses have revealed structural equivalences among contrastive, non-contrastive, and feature-decorrelation objectives, suggesting that their practical differences may stem from implicit optimization biases rather than fundamentally distinct learning principles. Similarly, understanding the role of batch normalization and stop-gradient operations through the lens of implicit regularization theory could yield more principled alternatives to these empirically motivated components.

In summary, while SSL has demonstrated transformative empirical results across diverse modalities and applications [77; 3], the field urgently needs rigorous theoretical foundations that unify existing methods, explain their empirical behavior, and guide the design of next-generation SSL algorithms. Progress on spectral perspectives, probabilistic generative models, information decomposition frameworks, and generalization theory will collectively move SSL from an empirically driven discipline toward a theoretically principled science—laying the groundwork for the ambitious future directions explored in the sections that follow, from continual and multimodal learning to the development of large-scale foundation models.

### 6.8 Foundation Models, Cross-Modal Unification, and Future Directions

## 6.8 Foundation Models, Cross-Modal Unification, and Future Directions

Building upon the theoretical frameworks and open questions identified in the preceding section—spanning spectral perspectives, information-theoretic analyses, and generalization theory—the trajectory of self-supervised representation learning (SSL) is increasingly converging toward the development of large-scale foundation models and unified cross-modal systems. These represent not only the natural culmination of the empirical and theoretical progress surveyed throughout this work, but also the most transformative and challenging frontiers in modern machine learning research. General-purpose systems pretrained with SSL objectives on massive, diverse datasets can be adapted to a wide spectrum of downstream tasks with minimal supervision, and understanding how to build, analyze, and deploy such systems constitutes a defining challenge for the field.

### Foundation Models and Universal Pretraining

The paradigm of foundation models fundamentally reshapes how we conceptualize SSL. Rather than training separate models for individual domains or tasks, the goal is to develop a single, scalable pretrained backbone whose representations transfer broadly across vision, language, audio, and beyond. The success of masked language modeling objectives in NLP and masked image modeling in vision suggests that unified generative pretraining objectives may underpin such universal systems. However, achieving truly general-purpose representations requires confronting the tension between learning task-agnostic features during pretraining and ensuring sufficient task-specific expressiveness during fine-tuning—a tension that connects directly to the generalization and transfer questions raised in the previous section. Open research questions include how to design pretraining objectives that simultaneously support diverse downstream requirements, how to scale training efficiently without prohibitive computational cost, and how to prevent the dominant modalities or data distributions from overshadowing underrepresented ones.

### Cross-Modal Unification Frameworks

A particularly promising direction involves cross-modal unification—building SSL frameworks that learn coherent, shared representations spanning multiple modalities including natural images, video, speech, audio, natural language text, graphs, and structured scientific data. Current multimodal SSL approaches, such as audio-visual correspondence learning, video-language alignment, and unified frameworks that process speech, vision, and language through shared objectives, demonstrate that cross-modal supervisory signals provide rich semantic grounding that unimodal pretraining cannot achieve alone. Future unified frameworks must go beyond pairwise modality alignment to capture higher-order dependencies among three or more modalities simultaneously, handle asynchronous and incomplete modality combinations at inference time, and ensure that shared representations do not lose modality-specific nuances required by specialized downstream tasks. The development of large-scale multimodal pretraining datasets and evaluation protocols that rigorously assess cross-modal transfer will be essential for driving progress in this direction.

### Hybrid SSL Objectives Combining Generative, Contrastive, and Predictive Paradigms

No single SSL paradigm—whether generative, contrastive, clustering-based, or predictive—has proven universally superior across all settings. Generative objectives such as masked autoencoding excel at learning fine-grained local structure but may encode redundant low-level details. Contrastive objectives learn globally discriminative features but are sensitive to augmentation design and the quality of negative pairs. Predictive and clustering-based objectives offer complementary advantages but introduce their own design challenges. Hybrid frameworks that judiciously combine multiple objectives represent a compelling path toward more robust and general representations. Future work should investigate principled strategies for balancing multiple loss terms, understanding interference or synergy between objectives, and designing architectures that efficiently share computation across paradigms. Theoretical frameworks that unify these objectives under common mathematical principles—such as the spectral embedding, information-theoretic, and probabilistic generative perspectives discussed in the previous section—will be critical for moving beyond empirical trial-and-error toward theoretically motivated hybrid designs.

### Leveraging Generative Models to Enrich Augmentation Diversity

Data augmentation is the cornerstone of most SSL frameworks, and the diversity and quality of augmented views directly determine the richness of learned representations. A significant future direction involves leveraging the expressive power of modern generative models—including diffusion models, generative adversarial networks, and large language models—to synthesize augmented views that go far beyond hand-crafted photometric and geometric transformations. Generative augmentation strategies can create semantically meaningful variations that preserve label-relevant content while introducing realistic distributional diversity, thereby expanding the effective support of the training distribution and improving representation robustness. However, this approach raises important questions about augmentation fidelity, the risk of introducing artifacts or distributional biases from the generative model, and how to ensure that generatively augmented views remain informative rather than trivially easy or impossibly hard. Systematic evaluation of generative augmentation strategies across modalities and downstream tasks remains an important open problem.

### Active and Interactive Self-Supervised Learning

While SSL is fundamentally designed to operate without labels, a promising direction involves incorporating minimal, low-cost human supervision in the form of semantic relationship queries—for example, asking whether two samples are semantically similar or belong to the same category—to guide the learning process. Such active self-supervised learning frameworks would allow practitioners to inject domain knowledge or semantic structure into the representation space at very low annotation cost, bridging the gap between fully unsupervised SSL and label-efficient semi-supervised learning. Incorporating semantic oracle queries can help resolve ambiguities in augmentation-based positive pair construction, reduce false negatives in contrastive learning, and align representation geometry with human-defined semantic hierarchies. Future work should explore how to design efficient query strategies that maximize information gain per annotation, how to integrate semantic feedback with existing SSL objectives without disrupting pretraining dynamics, and how to extend such frameworks to diverse modalities where semantic similarity is not straightforward to define.

### Self-Supervised Learning for Scientific and Specialized Domains

Beyond standard vision and language benchmarks, SSL holds transformative potential for scientific domains including molecular biology, materials science, climate modeling, and medical imaging, where labeled data is particularly scarce and domain expertise is costly. Re-simulation-based SSL strategies—where physical simulations provide abundant synthetic data from which self-supervised signals can be derived—offer a powerful mechanism for pretraining representations grounded in scientific priors. For molecular property prediction, biological sequence modeling, and physical system identification, pretext tasks can be designed to exploit known domain symmetries, invariances, and conservation laws, yielding representations that are both data-efficient and scientifically interpretable. Persistent challenges include defining meaningful augmentations for highly structured scientific data such as molecular graphs and protein structures, handling multi-scale phenomena, and ensuring that learned representations support not just prediction but mechanistic scientific understanding.

### Toward Universal, Efficient, Interpretable, and Theoretically Grounded SSL

The broader challenge unifying all future directions in SSL is building systems that are simultaneously universal, computationally efficient, interpretable, and theoretically grounded. Universality requires representations that transfer broadly without task-specific pretraining; efficiency demands reducing the enormous computational footprint of large-scale SSL while maintaining representation quality; interpretability calls for understanding what information is encoded, where it resides in the network, and how it relates to downstream task requirements; and theoretical grounding requires the principled frameworks—spanning information theory, spectral analysis, probabilistic modeling, and optimization theory—that the previous section identified as urgently needed to explain why SSL works, predict when it will fail, and guide the design of novel objectives and architectures. Progress on these interconnected challenges will not only advance SSL as a technical discipline but also enable its responsible and reliable deployment in real-world applications spanning healthcare, scientific discovery, autonomous systems, and beyond. The continued development of rigorous benchmarking protocols, interpretability tools, and theoretical analyses will be essential for measuring and accelerating progress toward this ambitious vision, ultimately fulfilling the transformative promise of self-supervised representation learning that has motivated the entirety of this survey.
