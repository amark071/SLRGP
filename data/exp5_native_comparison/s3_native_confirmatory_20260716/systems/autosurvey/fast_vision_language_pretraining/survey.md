# Vision-Language Pretraining: Architectures, Objectives, Data Strategies, Applications, and Future Directions

## 1 Introduction and Background

### 1.1 Motivation and Significance of Vision-Language Pretraining

The rapid proliferation of multimodal content on the internet has created an unprecedented opportunity for learning joint visual and linguistic representations. Billions of image-text pairs, ranging from social media posts to news articles and e-commerce listings, are now freely available at web scale, providing a rich and diverse source of supervision for training multimodal models without the need for costly manual annotation [1]. This abundance of weakly aligned data has served as a primary catalyst for the emergence and growth of Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP) as a dominant paradigm in multimodal artificial intelligence.

Prior to the VLP era, the prevailing approach to vision-language tasks involved designing and training separate, task-specific models tailored to individual problems such as visual question answering, image captioning, or image-text retrieval [2]. These task-specific models required expensive, curated datasets with precise human annotations, limiting their scalability and generalizability. Each new task necessitated building a new data collection pipeline, designing custom architectures, and training from scratch—a process that was not only resource-intensive but also failed to leverage the shared knowledge across related vision-language tasks [3]. Furthermore, the performance of these models was inherently bounded by the quantity and quality of available task-specific labeled data, making it difficult to achieve robust generalization to unseen scenarios.

The fundamental motivation behind VLP is to overcome these limitations by learning universal, transferable multimodal representations from large-scale, weakly supervised data [4]. By pretraining a single model on diverse image-text corpora, VLP methods can capture rich cross-modal semantic correspondences that generalize across a broad spectrum of downstream tasks. Once pretrained, these models can be fine-tuned with relatively little task-specific data, dramatically reducing the annotation burden while achieving strong performance [5]. This transfer learning paradigm mirrors the success of large-scale language pretraining in NLP, where models such as BERT and GPT demonstrated that representations learned from massive text corpora could be effectively adapted to diverse downstream applications—a conceptual foundation that, as discussed in the following section, directly inspired the trajectory of vision-language research.

The significance of VLP extends across a wide range of practically important tasks. In visual question answering (VQA), pretrained multimodal models can leverage both visual understanding and linguistic reasoning to answer complex questions about image content [2]. In image captioning, VLP enables models to generate fluent and semantically accurate descriptions by learning fine-grained visual-textual correspondences [6]. For image-text retrieval, pretrained dual-encoder architectures can efficiently index and query large-scale multimodal databases by aligning visual and textual representations in a shared embedding space [1]. Beyond these core tasks, VLP has shown promise in visual grounding, visual reasoning, scene text understanding [7], and even domain-specific applications such as medical image analysis [8].

In summary, VLP represents a paradigm shift from isolated, task-specific learning to unified, data-driven pretraining of multimodal representations. By harnessing the scale of web-crawled image-text data and reducing dependence on expensive annotations, VLP has democratized access to powerful vision-language capabilities and established a new state of the art across a diverse array of downstream tasks [9]. The growing significance of VLP motivates a systematic and comprehensive examination of the methods, architectures, objectives, and challenges that define this rapidly evolving field—an examination that begins with a closer look at the historical developments that set the stage for the modern VLP paradigm.

### 1.2 Historical Development: From Task-Specific Models to Pretrained Multimodal Models

The development of vision-language research has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past two decades, evolving from narrowly scoped task-specific systems to broadly capable pretrained multimodal models. Understanding this trajectory is essential for appreciating the current state of the field and the motivations behind modern vision-language pretraining (VLP) approaches—motivations that, as outlined in the preceding section, are fundamentally rooted in the limitations of earlier paradigms and the opportunities afforded by web-scale data.

**Early Task-Specific Methods**

In the early era of vision-language research, the dominant paradigm involved designing and training separate, specialized models for each individual task. Methods for visual question answering, image captioning, visual grounding, and image-text retrieval were developed largely in isolation, each with its own bespoke architecture, training pipeline, and dataset. These task-specific models were effective within their target domains but suffered from a fundamental limitation: knowledge and representations learned for one task could not easily be transferred to another. Every new task required collecting task-specific labeled data, designing a new model architecture, and training from scratch—a costly and inefficient process that severely limited scalability and generalization [10].

**The Deep Learning Revolution in Computer Vision and NLP**

The landscape began shifting with the advent of deep learning, which brought about powerful feature representations that could be learned automatically from large amounts of data. In computer vision, the introduction of deep convolutional neural networks led to transformative progress on tasks such as image classification and object detection, and the paradigm of pretraining on large datasets like ImageNet followed by fine-tuning on downstream tasks became standard practice. Concurrently, in natural language processing (NLP), the transformer architecture and large-scale language pretraining models such as BERT demonstrated the tremendous value of learning general-purpose representations from massive text corpora and subsequently adapting them to a wide variety of downstream language tasks [2]. These dual revolutions—whose technical underpinnings are examined in greater depth in the following section—established the conceptual and technical foundations upon which vision-language pretraining would be built.

**The Emergence of Pretrained Vision-Language Models**

Inspired by the success of transfer learning in unimodal domains, researchers began exploring how to jointly pretrain models on large collections of image-text pairs in order to learn universal multimodal representations. Rather than training task-specific models from scratch, the new paradigm involved a two-stage process: first, pretraining on large-scale, often weakly labeled or web-crawled image-text data to learn rich cross-modal representations, and then fine-tuning these representations on specific downstream tasks [11]. Early VLP models demonstrated that pretraining on diverse image-caption data could lead to strong performance improvements across multiple tasks simultaneously, effectively replacing the fragmented ecosystem of task-specific models with a more unified and reusable framework [12].

This shift represented a fundamental reconceptualization of vision-language research. Instead of treating each task as an isolated problem, the pretraining paradigm viewed diverse VL tasks as different manifestations of underlying shared skills—such as understanding object attributes, spatial relationships, and semantic correspondences between visual and textual content. Multi-task training further reinforced this view by demonstrating that jointly learning across multiple VL tasks with a single model could improve performance compared to task-specific baselines [3].

**Scaling and Consolidation**

As the field matured, VLP models grew rapidly in scale and capability. The development of large transformer-based architectures enabled richer and deeper cross-modal interactions, while the availability of increasingly large web-crawled image-text datasets provided the data necessary to train such models effectively. Critically, the introduction of unified transformer architectures—drawing directly on the architectural convergence between NLP and computer vision discussed in the following section—allowed a single pretrained model to serve as a versatile backbone for a broad range of downstream tasks including visual question answering, image captioning, image-text retrieval, and visual grounding, often achieving state-of-the-art results across all of them simultaneously [13]. This convergence marked a definitive transition from the fragmented task-specific era to the era of large-scale pretrained multimodal foundation models [14].

In summary, the historical evolution of vision-language research reflects a broader trend in machine learning toward general-purpose, scalable pretraining paradigms. The movement from task-specific models to pretrained multimodal models was driven by the practical limitations of task-specific training, inspired by the success of transfer learning in NLP and computer vision, and enabled by the growing availability of large-scale paired image-text data and powerful transformer architectures [15]. It is precisely these two unimodal pretraining traditions—and the technical insights they contributed—that the following section examines in detail, tracing how advances in NLP and computer vision converged to shape the methodological foundations of modern VLP.

### 1.3 Roots in Transfer Learning: NLP and Computer Vision Foundations

The success of vision-language pretraining (VLP) is deeply rooted in the transformative advances in transfer learning that emerged separately within natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV) before their eventual convergence into unified multimodal frameworks. As outlined in the preceding section, the transition from task-specific models to large-scale pretrained multimodal models was not spontaneous but was enabled by a set of powerful methodological breakthroughs in unimodal learning. Understanding these foundations is essential for appreciating both the design choices of modern VLP architectures and the technical challenges they must overcome—challenges that will be examined in detail in the following section.

**Transfer Learning in NLP: BERT, GPT, and the Pretraining Paradigm**

The modern era of transfer learning in NLP was catalyzed by the development of large-scale pretrained language models. Models such as BERT and GPT demonstrated that pretraining a deep transformer network on vast amounts of unlabeled text using self-supervised objectives—such as masked language modeling and autoregressive language generation—could yield rich, generalizable linguistic representations that transfer effectively to a wide range of downstream tasks with minimal task-specific modifications [16; 17]. This paradigm fundamentally shifted the field away from training task-specific models from scratch, establishing pretraining followed by fine-tuning as the dominant methodology [18]. The impact was profound: models pretrained on large corpora could capture syntactic structures, semantic relationships, and even commonsense knowledge, enabling dramatic performance improvements across tasks such as question answering, natural language inference, and machine translation [17].

Knowledge distillation approaches, such as DistilBERT [19], further demonstrated that these large pretrained representations could be compressed while retaining most of their linguistic understanding, underscoring the richness and transferability of the learned features. Similarly, parameter-efficient adaptation strategies such as adapter layers [20] showed that only small portions of pretrained model parameters needed to be updated to achieve competitive performance on new tasks, making large pretrained models practically deployable across diverse downstream applications. These insights into model compression and efficient adaptation would later prove directly relevant to the practical deployment of large-scale multimodal models.

**Transfer Learning in Computer Vision: From ImageNet to Vision Transformers**

Parallel developments in computer vision established a similarly powerful pretraining paradigm. The ImageNet challenge demonstrated that convolutional neural networks (CNNs) pretrained on large-scale image classification benchmarks learned broadly useful visual feature hierarchies, and fine-tuning these pretrained representations became standard practice for tasks such as object detection, semantic segmentation, and image captioning. This established the principle that large-scale visual pretraining on proxy tasks could yield transferable representations applicable across diverse visual domains.

The introduction of Vision Transformers (ViTs) represented a major architectural shift, applying the transformer architecture—originally developed for NLP—directly to image patches, achieving competitive or superior performance compared to CNNs on standard benchmarks [21]. This convergence of architectural choices between NLP and CV laid crucial groundwork for multimodal modeling, as a shared transformer backbone could, in principle, process both visual patches and textual tokens within a unified framework [13; 22]. Importantly, this architectural unification also introduced the challenge of bridging the representational gap between continuous visual patches and discrete textual tokens—a core technical difficulty that the following section addresses in depth.

**Inspiring the Vision-Language Pretraining Paradigm**

The success of unimodal pretraining in both NLP and CV naturally inspired researchers to ask whether analogous large-scale pretraining could be applied to joint vision-language modeling. Early VLP works such as ViLBERT [23] directly adapted the BERT pretraining framework to a multimodal setting, extending masked language modeling and next-sentence prediction-style objectives to image-text pairs collected at web scale. These models demonstrated that pretraining on large, automatically collected image-text datasets such as Conceptual Captions could yield multimodal representations that transfer effectively to diverse downstream tasks including visual question answering, image-text retrieval, and visual reasoning, achieving strong performance without task-specific architectural modifications—precisely the kind of unified, reusable framework whose historical emergence was traced in the previous section.

Subsequent work continued to draw on the lessons of unimodal pretraining. The prefix language modeling objective in SimVLM [4] was directly inspired by GPT-style autoregressive pretraining, applied to visual and textual token sequences jointly. The unified VLP model [6] adopted bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence masked prediction objectives analogous to BERT, enabling the same model to support both generation and understanding tasks. More recent architectures such as BEiT-3 [13] generalized masked image modeling—itself inspired by BERT's masked language modeling—to image tokens represented as visual "words," treating images as a foreign language to be processed by the same transformer backbone used for text.

The adoption of vision transformers as the visual backbone in VLP models further cemented this convergence. Works such as ViLT [21] replaced expensive region-based visual feature extractors with simple patch-based ViT encoders, dramatically reducing preprocessing costs while enabling end-to-end multimodal pretraining. This architectural unification meant that advances in training stable and scalable transformers—such as those studied in Foundation Transformers [22]—directly benefited multimodal models as well. Yet this same unification brings to the fore a set of fundamental technical challenges: how to align heterogeneous representation spaces, how to capture fine-grained semantic correspondences, and how to handle the noise inherent in web-crawled image-text data—all of which are examined in the following section.

In summary, VLP emerged at the intersection of two powerful pretraining traditions: the self-supervised language pretraining paradigm exemplified by BERT and GPT, and the visual representation learning paradigm powered by large-scale image pretraining and vision transformers. The lessons learned in each domain—regarding the importance of scale, the design of self-supervised pretraining objectives, parameter-efficient adaptation, and the versatility of the transformer architecture—collectively shaped the methodological foundations of modern vision-language pretraining [2; 11; 12]. These foundations, however, also set the stage for a new set of challenges unique to the multimodal setting, which the following section systematically characterizes.

### 1.4 Fundamental Challenges in Aligning Visual and Textual Modalities

Vision-language pretraining faces a constellation of fundamental technical challenges that arise from the inherently heterogeneous nature of visual and textual data, making robust cross-modal alignment a deeply non-trivial problem. While the previous section traced how the convergence of NLP and computer vision pretraining paradigms—through architectures such as BERT, GPT, and Vision Transformers—laid the methodological foundations for VLP, this same convergence introduced a set of unique difficulties that unimodal pretraining did not encounter. Understanding these challenges is essential for appreciating the architectural and objective innovations that the field has developed in response, and for contextualizing the core components of VLP systems described in the following section.

**Heterogeneous Representation Spaces.** Visual and textual data are represented in fundamentally different spaces. Images are continuous, high-dimensional pixel arrays or region feature vectors, while text consists of discrete symbolic sequences. Bridging this semantic gap requires careful architectural design, as visual patches and textual tokens differ substantially in semantic granularity and abstraction levels. This representational mismatch is not merely a surface-level incompatibility: it persists even when both modalities are processed through a shared transformer backbone, as the transition from CNN-based to ViT-based visual encoders did not by itself resolve the underlying alignment difficulty. As noted in work on Finite Discrete Tokens [24], directly aligning cross-modal information using patch and token embeddings is inherently difficult because these two types of representations operate at mismatched semantic levels and granularities, motivating the development of shared discrete visual-semantic concept spaces.

**Fine-Grained Semantic Correspondence.** Establishing precise, fine-grained correspondences between image regions and textual tokens remains one of the most persistent challenges in VLP. Early approaches relied on global image-text alignment, which is insufficient for tasks requiring localized understanding. Cross-modal masked language modeling and region-word alignment objectives have been proposed to address this, but capturing fine-grained token-to-patch correspondences without dense annotations is difficult. Works such as [25] highlight how standard global contrastive objectives miss the fine-grained expressiveness between image patches and textual words, motivating token-wise interaction mechanisms. Similarly, [26] demonstrates the need for phrase-region alignment tasks that go beyond simple image-level matching to achieve meaningful cross-modal semantic grounding at different levels of abstraction.

**Multi-Level Semantic Alignment.** Cross-modal alignment is not a single-level problem; it spans from pixel-level correspondences to high-level conceptual associations. Some image-text pairs are aligned through simple, concrete semantics, while others require higher-level abstraction to find their common ground [27]. Models that only perform alignment at a single semantic level inevitably miss important correspondences, motivating multi-granularity frameworks [28; 29] that simultaneously handle coarse-grained global and fine-grained local alignment. The challenge of capturing both global and local semantics is further emphasized by [30], which observes that standard masked modeling tasks focus on local-to-local token reconstruction while neglecting global semantic representations, leading to limited cross-modal alignment capability at the global level.

**Noise and Misalignment in Web-Crawled Data.** The dominant paradigm for VLP relies on large-scale image-text pairs harvested from the internet, such as Conceptual Captions, LAION, and similar datasets. While these corpora offer enormous scale—and while their collection was precisely what enabled the BERT-inspired multimodal pretraining paradigm to take hold—they are inherently noisy: textual descriptions are often incomplete, inaccurate, or only loosely related to image content. This misalignment between paired modalities substantially degrades the quality of learned representations and introduces spurious correlations. The challenge is compounded by the long-tailed distribution of visual concepts in web data, where rare but semantically important concepts are poorly represented. Filtering approaches such as CLIPScore-based pruning have been proposed, but they themselves suffer from limitations including false positives and negatives inherited from the noise in their own pretraining data [31]. The difficulty of learning robust alignment from noisy paired data remains an open challenge that has motivated numerous noise-handling and data augmentation strategies throughout the VLP literature.

**Modality Bias and Unbalanced Information Utilization.** A subtle but significant challenge is modality bias, where VLP models over-rely on one modality—typically language—rather than learning genuinely grounded cross-modal representations. This manifests in models exploiting linguistic shortcuts or statistical biases in text without truly understanding the corresponding visual content. [32] explicitly identifies the problem of focal bias, noting that information disparity between visual and textual inputs causes models to neglect fine-grained visual features, leading to unbalanced representations. Similarly, probing studies [33] reveal that VLP models tend to focus disproportionately on aligning objects with visual words while neglecting global semantics, preferring fixed sentence patterns and over-weighting visually concrete vocabulary. Addressing modality bias requires designing objectives and architectures that enforce genuine bidirectional dependence between both modalities—a consideration that directly informs the design choices of the multimodal fusion architectures and pretraining objectives described in the following section.

**Scalability of Visual Feature Extraction.** The choice of visual encoder critically impacts the quality and efficiency of cross-modal alignment. Early VLP models relied on pretrained object detectors such as Faster R-CNN to extract region-based visual features, which provide semantically rich representations but introduce computational bottlenecks, restrict the concept vocabulary to the detector's training categories, and decouple visual and cross-modal learning [34]. End-to-end visual learning paradigms that replace these offline detectors with grid-based or patch-based encoders alleviate these limitations but introduce new challenges in learning visually discriminative representations without explicit object-level supervision. Establishing effective visual representations that are both semantically rich and computationally tractable for large-scale pretraining remains a core technical challenge, and it is one that the visual feature extraction strategies surveyed in the following section are specifically designed to address.

**Syntactic and Compositional Understanding.** Beyond object-level correspondences, VLP models are expected to understand complex linguistic structures including attribute binding, relational predicates, spatial relationships, and negation. However, numerous diagnostic studies reveal that current VLP models struggle with these compositional aspects of language. [35] demonstrates that many state-of-the-art models perform poorly at understanding syntactic dependencies between predicates and nouns in visual contexts, highlighting that standard pretraining objectives are insufficient to instill robust compositional cross-modal understanding. This compositional alignment challenge is compounded by the bag-of-words tendencies observed in contrastive models, which fail to leverage word order and syntactic structure when computing cross-modal similarity scores—a limitation that, as the following section discusses, has direct implications for the design of text encoding strategies and pretraining objectives.

Collectively, these challenges—heterogeneous representation spaces, fine-grained correspondence, multi-level alignment, noisy supervision, modality bias, visual encoder limitations, and compositional understanding—define the core research agenda of vision-language pretraining. They are not independent difficulties but form an interconnected set of problems, each influencing and compounding the others. The subsequent sections of this survey examine in detail the foundational building blocks of VLP systems—including visual encoders, text encoders, fusion architectures, and pretraining objectives—before tracing the architectural innovations, data strategies, and specialized methods that the field has developed to address each of these fundamental difficulties.

### 1.5 Key Components of Vision-Language Pretraining Frameworks

Vision-language pretraining (VLP) frameworks are built upon several core components that work in concert to enable joint understanding of visual and textual information. Before examining the challenges that motivate the field's innovations and the taxonomy of approaches that have emerged in response, it is essential to establish a clear understanding of these foundational building blocks. The following overview presents the main constituents common to VLP systems, providing the technical vocabulary and conceptual grounding necessary for the discussions that follow.

**Visual Feature Extraction.** The visual encoder is responsible for transforming raw image pixels into feature representations suitable for cross-modal reasoning. Early VLP models relied heavily on region-based features extracted by pretrained object detectors such as Faster R-CNN, which produced sets of region-of-interest (RoI) features representing salient objects and their attributes. While powerful, this two-stage pipeline introduced significant computational overhead and constrained the visual vocabulary to the detector's predefined categories—a limitation directly connected to the scalability challenges discussed in the preceding section. To address these limitations, subsequent work explored grid-based convolutional features that bypass explicit region proposals [36], and patch-based encoders inspired by Vision Transformers (ViT) that divide images into fixed-size patches and process them through transformer layers [37]. The shift toward patch-based encoders enabled end-to-end training and substantially reduced inference latency, as demonstrated by ViLT [37] which showed that convolution-free visual encoding can be competitive with region-based approaches while being orders of magnitude faster. Comprehensive empirical studies such as METER [38] further investigated the relative merits of different visual backbones—including CLIP-ViT and Swin Transformer—within fully transformer-based VLP pipelines.

**Text Encoding Strategies.** Textual inputs in VLP models are typically tokenized using subword tokenization schemes (e.g., WordPiece or Byte-Pair Encoding) and encoded through transformer-based language models. Early VLP models employed BERT-style encoders to produce contextualized token embeddings. The choice of text encoder—whether a general-purpose language model like RoBERTa or a domain-specific encoder—significantly influences the quality of cross-modal alignment. In medical VLP, for instance, specialized encoders are used to capture clinical terminology present in radiology reports [39]. Some unified frameworks employ shared transformer networks for both encoding and decoding, enabling the same model to handle both understanding and generation tasks [6]. The capacity of the text encoder to capture syntactic and compositional structure is particularly relevant given the compositional understanding challenges identified above, as models that treat text as a bag of words are inherently limited in their ability to ground relational and attributive language in visual context.

**Multimodal Fusion Architectures.** Once visual and textual features are extracted, they must be integrated through a fusion mechanism. Two broad architectural families have emerged, each with distinct trade-offs that map directly onto the architectural taxonomy elaborated in the following section. *Single-stream* models concatenate visual and textual token sequences and feed them jointly into a unified transformer, enabling deep cross-modal attention at every layer. This design, adopted by models such as ViLT [37], allows rich bidirectional interaction between modalities but can be computationally intensive for retrieval tasks. *Dual-stream* (two-tower) models, by contrast, maintain separate unimodal encoders and introduce cross-modal interaction only at a higher level, typically through contrastive objectives or shallow cross-attention. This separation enables efficient independent encoding of each modality, making dual-stream architectures particularly well-suited for large-scale image-text retrieval. METER [38] provides a systematic empirical comparison of merged attention (single-stream) versus co-attention (dual-stream) fusion modules, offering important insights into how fusion depth and placement affect downstream performance. Hybrid designs such as SemVLP [27] further explore iterative combinations of single- and two-stream pre-training to align semantics at multiple granularity levels, directly addressing the multi-level semantic alignment challenges that motivate much of the field's architectural innovation.

**Pretraining Objectives.** The learning signal used during pretraining fundamentally shapes the nature of the representations learned, and the diversity of objectives that have been proposed reflects the breadth of challenges outlined in the previous section. Several families of objectives have been proposed and are commonly combined in practice. *Masked Language Modeling* (MLM) adapts the BERT-style objective to the multimodal setting, requiring the model to predict masked textual tokens conditioned on both the remaining text and the visual context, thereby encouraging the model to ground language in visual content [2]. Variants such as structural knowledge masking in ROSITA [40] and saliency-based masking in EPIC [41] have been introduced to improve the quality and efficiency of this objective. *Masked Visual/Region Modeling* extends the masking paradigm to the visual modality, requiring reconstruction of masked image regions or patches, as explored in VLMAE [32] and end-to-end frameworks like E2E-VLP [42]. *Image-Text Matching* (ITM) is a discriminative objective that trains the model to distinguish genuine image-text pairs from mismatched ones, providing a global alignment signal that complements token-level MLM. *Contrastive Learning* objectives, exemplified by approaches that align global image and text representations in a shared embedding space, have proven highly effective for learning transferable representations, with EPIC [41] further extending contrastive ideas to per-token consistency. More recent unified frameworks such as BLIP [43] combine contrastive, matching, and generative objectives within a single model to simultaneously support both understanding and generation tasks, while FLM [44] proposes decoupling prediction and corruption rates in masked modeling to substantially accelerate convergence. GLIPv2 [45] further unifies localization and language understanding through region-word contrastive learning and masked language modeling in a joint pretraining framework.

Together, these components—visual encoders, text encoders, multimodal fusion architectures, and pretraining objectives—form the foundational building blocks upon which the diverse landscape of VLP methods is constructed. Their interactions and individual design choices directly determine a model's capacity to address the core challenges of heterogeneous representation alignment, fine-grained correspondence, and noisy supervision discussed above. The following sections of this survey provide in-depth analyses of each component along the five taxonomic dimensions of architecture, objectives, data, tasks, and evaluation, tracing their evolution and examining the empirical evidence that has guided modern VLP design.

### 1.6 Taxonomy and Landscape of Vision-Language Pretraining Research

The vision-language pretraining (VLP) research landscape has grown remarkably diverse, encompassing a wide range of model architectures, learning objectives, data strategies, downstream applications, and evaluation paradigms. Having established the foundational building blocks common to VLP systems—visual encoders, text encoders, multimodal fusion architectures, and pretraining objectives—we now turn to the broader organizational framework that structures this survey. To navigate the complexity of the field, it is essential to establish a structured taxonomy that organizes the literature along its principal dimensions. Figure 1 illustrates this high-level taxonomy, which we elaborate upon below, and which directly informs the organization of the sections that follow.

**Model Architecture Design.** VLP models can be broadly categorized by their architectural paradigm. Single-stream fusion encoders jointly process concatenated visual and textual tokens through shared transformer layers, while dual-stream (two-tower) frameworks maintain separate unimodal encoders before performing cross-modal interaction. Encoder-decoder architectures support both understanding and generation tasks within a unified framework [6; 4], and unified transformer models with modality-specific components offer flexible deployment as either fusion or dual encoders. Within each family, architectural choices regarding visual feature extraction—ranging from region-based detectors to patch-based Vision Transformers [21]—further differentiate the landscape. These architectural distinctions build directly on the single-stream and dual-stream fusion designs introduced in the preceding discussion of multimodal fusion architectures.

**Pretraining Objectives and Learning Strategies.** The field has developed a rich repertoire of pretraining objectives that extend and refine the core families described above. These span discriminative objectives such as image-text matching and masked language modeling, contrastive objectives that align global or local visual and textual representations, and generative objectives such as prefix language modeling and sequence-to-sequence prediction. Advanced strategies including momentum distillation, bootstrapped caption filtering, scheduled sampling, and multi-grained semantic alignment objectives have further refined how models learn from large-scale noisy data [46; 26; 47; 32].

**Data Sources and Supervision Paradigms.** VLP research can be organized according to the nature and scale of pretraining data. Fully supervised approaches leverage high-quality annotated image-text pairs, while weakly supervised paradigms exploit noisily aligned web-crawled data [4], and unsupervised methods operate without parallel corpora. Tag and knowledge augmentation strategies [46; 48; 47] enrich training signals, while noise filtering and bootstrapped refinement techniques address data quality challenges at scale.

**Supported Downstream Tasks.** VLP models are evaluated across a diverse suite of downstream tasks that test different aspects of cross-modal understanding and generation. Core tasks include visual question answering, image-text retrieval, visual grounding, image captioning, visual reasoning, and scene text understanding. The breadth of tasks supported by a single pretrained model serves as a key differentiator among approaches [2; 9].

**Specialized Settings.** Beyond standard English image-text pretraining, the field has expanded into several specialized settings. Multilingual VLP extends cross-modal representations to non-English languages [49; 50], video-language pretraining addresses temporal dynamics in video-text pairs [51], and domain-specific pretraining targets applications such as medical imaging [52; 53]. Additionally, parameter-efficient adaptation methods including prompt tuning and continual pretraining [54] represent emerging directions with significant practical relevance.

**Evaluation Methodologies.** Finally, the field has developed increasingly sophisticated evaluation frameworks. Beyond aggregate accuracy on standard benchmarks, diagnostic and probing evaluations such as VL-CheckList [55] and SNARE [56] expose fine-grained model capabilities and limitations across object, attribute, and relation understanding. Holistic multi-task benchmarks such as VLUE [57] assess generalization and efficiency-performance trade-offs under diverse and out-of-distribution conditions.

Together, these five dimensions—architecture, objectives, data, tasks, and evaluation—constitute the organizing framework of this survey. By situating each reviewed work within this taxonomy, we provide readers with a structured map for understanding how individual contributions relate to the broader field. The following subsection outlines the scope and organization of the survey, describing how each of these taxonomic dimensions corresponds to a dedicated section and how the coverage has been curated to reflect both foundational contributions and emerging frontiers.

## 2 Model Architectures for Vision-Language Pretraining

### 2.1 Overview and Taxonomy of VLP Architectures

The rapid development of vision-language pretraining (VLP) has given rise to a diverse ecosystem of model architectures, each embodying distinct design philosophies regarding how visual and textual information should be encoded, fused, and leveraged for downstream tasks [2]. At a high level, existing VLP architectures can be organized into four major paradigms: (1) **single-stream fusion encoders**, (2) **dual-stream (two-tower) frameworks**, (3) **encoder-decoder networks**, and (4) **unified transformer-based models with modality-specific components**. Understanding the trade-offs inherent in each paradigm is essential for appreciating the motivation behind successive architectural innovations in the field.

**Single-stream fusion encoders** concatenate visual and textual token sequences and pass them jointly through a shared transformer network, enabling deep cross-modal interaction from the earliest layers. Representative models in this family, such as VisualBERT and Unicoder-VL, demonstrate strong performance on understanding tasks by leveraging self-attention across both modalities simultaneously [2]. The primary advantage is rich cross-modal fusion, but this comes at the cost of computational efficiency, since every image-text pair must be processed jointly, making large-scale retrieval expensive. As will be explored in the following subsection, this architectural choice directly shapes the design of pretraining objectives—such as Masked Language Modeling, Masked Object Classification, and Image-Text Matching—that are used to instill deep cross-modal semantic understanding.

**Dual-stream (two-tower) frameworks**, by contrast, maintain separate unimodal encoders for vision and language, performing cross-modal interaction only at a later stage or through a lightweight matching function. This architectural choice, exemplified by models such as CLIP and ALIGN [1], enables efficient pre-computation of unimodal embeddings, making them particularly well-suited for large-scale image-text retrieval. However, the shallower cross-modal interaction can limit performance on tasks requiring fine-grained semantic alignment between image regions and textual tokens.

**Encoder-decoder architectures** are designed to support both vision-language understanding and generation within a single framework. Models such as the unified VLP framework [6] and SimVLM [4] adopt a shared transformer or prefix language modeling objective to jointly handle discriminative tasks like VQA and generative tasks like image captioning. This versatility is achieved by controlling attention masking strategies during pretraining, but unifying bidirectional and autoregressive behaviors within one model introduces additional training complexity.

**Unified transformer-based models with modality-specific components** represent a more recent and flexible paradigm that seeks to combine the strengths of the above approaches. These architectures introduce modality-specific expert modules or multiway transformer networks to allow the same model to function as either a fusion encoder or a dual encoder depending on the downstream task requirements [2]. Such designs aim to achieve task versatility without sacrificing the efficiency afforded by modality-specific encoding, though they introduce additional complexity in terms of training objectives and expert routing mechanisms.

Across all four paradigms, the central design tension is the **trade-off between cross-modal fusion depth, computational efficiency, and task versatility**. Deeper fusion generally improves performance on tasks requiring fine-grained cross-modal understanding, such as visual question answering and visual grounding, but increases computational cost per inference. Conversely, shallow or no fusion enables efficient retrieval and zero-shot transfer but can underperform on tasks requiring compositional reasoning. The evolution of VLP architectures can thus be understood as a sustained effort to navigate this three-way trade-off, with different works prioritizing different points along the spectrum [21][5]. The following subsections examine each architectural family in detail, tracing their defining characteristics, representative models, and the empirical insights that have shaped ongoing architectural innovation.

### 2.2 Single-Stream Fusion Encoders

Single-stream fusion encoders represent one of the earliest and most influential architectural paradigms in vision-language pretraining (VLP). As introduced in the previous section, this design philosophy places cross-modal fusion at the center of the architecture: visual features (typically derived from region-based object detectors or patch embeddings) and textual token embeddings are concatenated into a single unified sequence, which is then processed jointly by a shared transformer network. This architecture enables deep cross-modal interaction through self-attention across all tokens, allowing every visual region to attend to every textual token and vice versa, facilitating rich multimodal fusion from the very first transformer layer.

Representative models in this family include VisualBERT, Unicoder-VL, and UFO, which extended the BERT-style pretraining paradigm to the multimodal domain by treating image-region features and word tokens as a unified input stream. These models demonstrated that the bidirectional self-attention mechanism inherent to transformer architectures is naturally suited for cross-modal reasoning, as it allows the model to dynamically align semantic concepts across modalities without requiring explicit modality-specific fusion modules.

The pretraining objectives commonly associated with single-stream architectures are designed to encourage the model to develop deep cross-modal semantic understanding. Masked Language Modeling (MLM) is adapted to the VLP setting, requiring the model to predict masked textual tokens conditioned on both the surrounding text and visual context, thereby forcing the language stream to ground its representations in visual content [13]. Masked Object Classification (MOC) extends this idea to the visual modality by masking image region features and requiring the model to predict their semantic categories, encouraging the visual representations to be informed by linguistic context. Additionally, Image-Text Matching (ITM) objectives train the model to discriminate between semantically aligned and misaligned image-text pairs, providing a global cross-modal alignment signal that complements the local token-level objectives. As will be discussed in the following section on dual-stream architectures, variants of these objectives — particularly contrastive image-text matching — are adapted and repurposed in two-tower frameworks, though the architectural separation of modalities fundamentally changes how these signals are computed and utilized.

A key advantage of the single-stream design is the depth of cross-modal fusion it enables: since visual and textual tokens interact through every transformer layer, the resulting representations are deeply entangled and highly expressive for tasks requiring fine-grained cross-modal understanding, such as visual question answering and visual reasoning [2]. Comprehensive surveys of VLP models have recognized single-stream architectures as foundational to the field, noting their strong performance on understanding-oriented benchmarks and their influence on subsequent architectural innovations [11; 12].

However, single-stream architectures also carry notable limitations that directly motivate the alternative paradigms examined in the following sections. The joint encoding of visual and textual tokens within a single transformer incurs quadratic attention complexity with respect to the combined sequence length, making inference computationally expensive. Furthermore, because visual and textual representations are fused immediately and inseparably, single-stream models are ill-suited for efficient large-scale image-text retrieval tasks, which benefit from independently encoded unimodal representations that can be pre-computed, indexed, and compared at scale. It is precisely these computational and scalability constraints that motivated the development of dual-stream frameworks such as CLIP and ViLBERT, where separate unimodal encoders allow modality-specific embeddings to be processed and stored independently — a design philosophy explored in depth in the following section.

### 2.3 Dual-Stream (Two-Tower) Encoder Frameworks

Dual-stream (two-tower) architectures represent a fundamentally different design philosophy compared to the single-stream fusion encoders discussed in the previous section. Rather than concatenating visual and textual tokens at the input level and jointly processing them through a shared transformer, dual-stream models maintain separate, independent unimodal encoders for vision and language, allowing each modality to develop rich intra-modal representations before any cross-modal interaction takes place. This architectural separation offers several compelling advantages, particularly for large-scale image-text retrieval tasks where computational efficiency is paramount, and it sets the stage for the encoder-decoder designs explored in the following section.

The most prominent examples of this paradigm are contrastive learning frameworks such as CLIP and ALIGN, which train vision and language towers independently using large-scale noisy image-text pairs. By projecting both modalities into a shared embedding space and maximizing the cosine similarity between matched pairs while minimizing it for non-matched pairs, these models learn powerful cross-modal alignments without requiring deep cross-modal fusion. The dual-encoder design enables efficient retrieval at inference time: image and text embeddings can be pre-computed independently and stored as indexed vectors, allowing similarity search to be performed via simple dot products across millions or billions of candidates. This stands in sharp contrast to single-stream models, which require joint forward passes for every image-text pair, making exhaustive retrieval computationally prohibitive at scale.

The separation of modality-specific processing also allows each tower to be independently scaled, pretrained, or swapped with stronger backbones. For instance, as discussed in [21], early VLP models heavily relied on region-based visual features from pretrained object detectors, but dual-stream contrastive frameworks have progressively shifted toward end-to-end patch-based vision transformers (ViT) as the visual backbone, significantly reducing preprocessing overhead while improving representational quality. This shift aligns with broader observations in the field that convolution-free, patch-based visual encoders can match or surpass region-supervised counterparts in downstream performance.

A key design dimension within the dual-stream family concerns the depth and nature of cross-modal interaction. In purely contrastive two-tower models like CLIP and ALIGN, cross-modal interaction is entirely shallow — restricted to a final alignment step in the shared embedding space, with no explicit token-level cross-attention between the modalities. This shallow interaction is sufficient for coarse-grained image-text matching and zero-shot classification tasks, but it limits the model's capacity for fine-grained understanding tasks such as visual question answering, visual reasoning, and referring expression comprehension, where deeper token-level alignment is often necessary. This limitation directly motivates the richer cross-modal fusion strategies pursued by single-stream architectures on one end, and the generative capabilities enabled by encoder-decoder designs on the other.

To address this limitation within the dual-stream paradigm itself, several hybrid architectures have been proposed that augment dual-stream encoders with additional cross-modal fusion layers. Models such as ViLBERT [23] pioneered the two-stream approach in early VLP by processing visual and textual inputs through separate transformer streams that interact via co-attentional transformer layers inserted at multiple depths. This co-attention mechanism allows each stream to attend to the other's representations, enabling richer bidirectional cross-modal reasoning while preserving the benefits of independent modality encoding in the early layers. ViLBERT demonstrated that this architecture could be pretrained on large-scale image-text datasets and fine-tuned across multiple vision-language tasks — including visual question answering, visual commonsense reasoning, referring expressions, and caption-based image retrieval — achieving state-of-the-art results at the time [23].

The trade-off between shallow and deep cross-modal interaction thus emerges as a central design consideration in dual-stream architectures. Shallow interaction (e.g., final-layer contrastive alignment) maximizes retrieval efficiency and scalability to billion-scale datasets but sacrifices fine-grained semantic alignment. Deep cross-modal interaction (e.g., co-attention at multiple transformer layers) enables richer compositional understanding and stronger performance on complex reasoning benchmarks but substantially increases computational cost and precludes the independent pre-indexing of modality embeddings. As discussed in [2], this architectural tension has motivated a wide spectrum of hybrid designs that attempt to combine the scalability of dual encoders with the expressive power of fusion encoders, often through cascaded architectures or asymmetric interaction modules appended to frozen dual-stream backbones.

More recent works have also explored unified transformer architectures with modality-specific components that can flexibly operate as either a fusion encoder or a dual encoder depending on the task, further blurring the boundary between single-stream and dual-stream paradigms. Efficient VLP approaches such as [63] have further demonstrated that the visual sequence length in ViT-based backbones can be dynamically reduced using text-guided patch selection, substantially accelerating both training and inference in VLP models that incorporate dual-stream processing stages. Together, these developments reflect an ongoing convergence between the retrieval-oriented efficiency of two-tower models and the representational depth demanded by complex vision-language understanding tasks — a convergence that finds its fullest expression in the encoder-decoder architectures examined in the next section.

### 2.4 Encoder-Decoder Architectures for Vision-Language Generation

Encoder-decoder architectures represent a significant paradigm in vision-language pretraining (VLP), bridging the gap between the discriminative encoding designs discussed in the previous sections and the unified modality-specific transformer designs explored in the following section. Whereas single-stream and dual-stream encoders focus primarily on discriminative understanding tasks such as image-text matching and retrieval, encoder-decoder models introduce an autoregressive decoder component that enables the generation of free-form textual outputs conditioned on visual inputs. This makes them well-suited for tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering in generative settings, and cross-modal text generation, effectively extending the scope of VLP beyond classification and alignment objectives toward open-ended language generation.

A foundational distinction in encoder-decoder VLP designs lies in whether the encoder and decoder share parameters or operate as decoupled modules. Shared encoder-decoder designs promote parameter efficiency and encourage the model to learn representations that are simultaneously useful for bidirectional understanding and unidirectional generation. In contrast, decoupled designs allow the encoder to specialize in building rich cross-modal representations while the decoder focuses on autoregressive token prediction, offering greater architectural flexibility at the cost of increased parameter count. This design choice directly influences the downstream versatility of the resulting model, and as explored in the following section, it also connects to the broader question of how modality-specific components can be integrated into unified transformer frameworks.

The E2E-VLP model exemplifies the encoder-decoder approach by proposing the first end-to-end vision-language pretrained model that jointly handles both V+L understanding and generation within a unified Transformer encoder-decoder framework [34]. Rather than relying on a frozen object detector to extract regional visual features—as was common in earlier VLP models—E2E-VLP directly feeds CNN-extracted grid features into the Transformer, allowing visual representation learning and semantic alignment to occur jointly during pretraining. This end-to-end design not only improves computational efficiency but also avoids the representational bottleneck imposed by task-specific object detectors, which tend to constrain the visual concept space available to the model. To further enrich visual learning, E2E-VLP incorporates object detection and image captioning as auxiliary pretraining tasks, enabling the model to develop fine-grained visual understanding alongside cross-modal alignment capabilities.

SimVLM (Simple Visual Language Model) advances encoder-decoder VLP by adopting a minimalist pretraining strategy centered on prefix language modeling, wherein the visual features and a prefix portion of the associated text are provided to the encoder, while the decoder is trained to predict the remaining textual tokens autoregressively. This approach elegantly unifies understanding and generation in a single objective, eliminating the need for multiple complex pretraining tasks and leveraging the generalization power of large-scale weakly supervised image-text pairs for training.

The mPLUG model further extends the encoder-decoder paradigm by introducing cross-modal skip-connections, which create inter-layer shortcuts that skip certain self-attention layers on the visual side to reduce computational overhead while maintaining effective cross-modal alignment [64]. This architectural innovation directly addresses one of the core efficiency limitations of dual-stream and full self-attention encoder designs, where long visual token sequences impose substantial computational cost. By enabling mPLUG to scale to large-scale image-text pretraining with both discriminative and generative objectives, these skip-connections yield strong performance on tasks including image captioning, visual question answering, and image-text retrieval.

A key challenge for encoder-decoder VLP is balancing the differing inductive biases required for bidirectional understanding versus autoregressive generation. Bidirectional attention in the encoder captures rich contextual representations necessary for understanding tasks, while the causal masking used in decoders enforces the left-to-right generation order. Various works have explored hybrid attention masking strategies, where the encoder employs full self-attention over concatenated visual and textual tokens, while the decoder uses causal attention masks, thereby enabling a single pretrained model to switch between understanding and generation modes depending on the downstream task formulation. This capacity for flexible deployment anticipates the more explicit architectural flexibility introduced by modality-specific unified transformers in the following section.

Unified encoder-decoder architectures have also been explored in the context of generative text-guided image understanding. For example, VALOR demonstrates a tri-modal encoder-decoder design where separate encoders capture vision, audio, and language representations, while a shared decoder generates textual outputs conditioned on multimodal inputs, showing that encoder-decoder pretraining can be scaled to more than two modalities [65]. Similarly, BEiT-3 adopts a Multiway Transformer backbone that supports both encoder-style and encoder-decoder-style computation through modality-specific feed-forward networks, demonstrating that a single architecture can flexibly handle the full spectrum of VLP tasks including generation and understanding [13]. BEiT-3's Multiway Transformer design, with its pool of modality-specific experts, serves as a natural conceptual bridge to the unified transformer models with modality-specific components examined in the next section.

Overall, encoder-decoder architectures offer a compelling framework for VLP by unifying the complementary requirements of visual understanding and language generation. Their continued development—spanning end-to-end visual learning, prefix language modeling, cross-modal skip-connections, and multi-modal generative pretraining—has significantly advanced the state of the art across diverse vision-language benchmarks, while also laying the architectural groundwork for the more general unified transformer designs that follow.

### 2.5 Unified Transformer Models with Modality-Specific Components

A significant architectural innovation in vision-language pretraining involves the design of unified transformer models that incorporate modality-specific components, enabling a single model to flexibly serve as either a fusion encoder for understanding tasks or a dual encoder for efficient retrieval, without requiring separate model architectures for different downstream applications. This design philosophy builds naturally upon the encoder-decoder paradigm discussed previously, extending the principle of architectural unification to explicitly address modality-specific representation learning alongside cross-modal fusion.

**Motivation and Core Design Philosophy**

The central motivation behind this architectural paradigm stems from the fundamental tension between two competing requirements in VLP: (1) deep cross-modal fusion for tasks requiring rich multimodal understanding, such as visual question answering and visual reasoning, and (2) independent unimodal encoding for efficient image-text retrieval where computing separate embeddings is essential. Earlier approaches were forced to commit to one architectural paradigm or the other, sacrificing either fusion quality or retrieval efficiency. Unified transformer models with modality-specific components address this dilemma by maintaining a shared transformer backbone augmented with specialized routing mechanisms that can dynamically adapt to different encoding requirements. This challenge is closely related to the visual encoder selection decisions discussed in the following section, as the expressiveness of modality-specific components depends critically on the quality of the underlying visual representations they process.

**VLMo and Mixture-of-Modality-Experts (MoME)**

One of the most influential designs in this direction is VLMo, which introduces the Mixture-of-Modality-Experts (MoME) transformer architecture. In VLMo, each transformer layer contains a pool of modality-specific feed-forward network (FFN) experts alongside shared self-attention modules. During processing, image tokens are routed through vision-specific FFN experts, text tokens through language-specific FFN experts, and image-text pairs through vision-language joint experts. The self-attention layers, being shared across modalities, handle cross-modal interactions, while the modality-specific FFNs capture the distinct statistical properties and representations of each modality. This design allows VLMo to be deployed as a dual encoder—activating only the vision or language experts—for efficient retrieval tasks, while switching to fusion-encoder mode with joint experts for understanding tasks. The training strategy in VLMo follows a stage-wise pretraining approach: first training the vision expert on image data, then the language expert on text, and finally jointly pretraining with all components, allowing the model to accumulate strong unimodal representations before learning multimodal fusion.

**BEiT-3 and Multiway Transformers**

BEiT-3 extends this concept through the Multiway Transformer architecture, which generalizes the MoME design by replacing standard FFN layers with multiway FFNs consisting of a pool of modality experts. Each transformer block contains modality-specific experts for vision, language, and vision-language fusion, alongside shared self-attention modules. BEiT-3 pretrains by performing masked data modeling across monomodal and multimodal data simultaneously, treating image patches, text tokens, and image-text pairs uniformly within the same transformer framework. The Multiway Transformer enables BEiT-3 to serve as a general-purpose multimodal foundation model, achieving competitive performance across a wide range of tasks including image-text retrieval, visual question answering, image captioning, and visual reasoning, all from a single pretrained model. The unified architecture effectively amortizes pretraining costs while delivering versatile downstream capabilities.

**EVE and Modality-Aware Sparse Mixture-of-Experts**

EVE adopts a complementary approach through modality-aware sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE), where expert routing is conditioned not only on the input content but also on the modality identity of each token. Unlike dense MoME designs where all modality-specific experts are always active, EVE's sparse routing selects a subset of experts per token, improving computational efficiency while maintaining modality specialization. The modality-aware gating mechanism ensures that visual tokens and textual tokens are consistently routed to their respective specialized experts, preserving the distinct representation spaces of each modality while still enabling cross-modal interaction through shared attention layers. This sparse design scales more favorably to larger model sizes and longer token sequences, making it particularly suited for pretraining on large-scale web data.

**Flexible Deployment as Fusion or Dual Encoders**

A key practical advantage of these unified architectures is their flexible deployment capability. By selectively activating different expert pathways, the same pretrained model can serve multiple roles. For image-text retrieval benchmarks, the model operates in dual-encoder mode, independently encoding images and texts to produce comparable embedding vectors that can be efficiently searched at scale. For tasks requiring cross-modal reasoning, the model switches to fusion-encoder mode, jointly processing image-text pairs with full cross-modal attention and joint expert routing. This flexibility eliminates the need to maintain separate specialized models for different task categories, significantly reducing deployment complexity and memory requirements. As noted in empirical studies of end-to-end VLP frameworks [38], the choice of fusion strategy critically impacts downstream performance, and modality-specific component designs offer a principled way to navigate these trade-offs without hard architectural commitments.

**Comparison with Single-Stream and Dual-Stream Alternatives**

Compared to pure single-stream models that concatenate all tokens and apply uniform attention, unified models with modality-specific components achieve better modality-specialized representations by dedicating distinct parameters to each modality's unique characteristics. Compared to pure dual-stream models that maintain entirely separate encoders with no shared parameters, these unified designs achieve greater parameter efficiency and enable richer knowledge transfer between modalities through shared attention layers. The result is a middle ground that captures the strengths of both paradigms: deep cross-modal fusion capability from single-stream designs and modality-independent encoding efficiency from dual-stream designs, all within a single coherent architecture. Crucially, the effectiveness of these modality-specific components is tightly coupled to the quality of the visual features they receive as input—a relationship that motivates the detailed examination of visual encoder design choices in the following section. This architectural family represents a maturing understanding of the structural requirements for building versatile, high-performance vision-language pretrained models that can be practically deployed across the full spectrum of multimodal downstream tasks.

### 2.6 Visual Encoders in VLP: Region-Based, Patch-Based, and End-to-End Approaches

The choice of visual encoder has been a central design decision in vision-language pretraining, profoundly shaping both the quality of learned representations and the computational efficiency of the overall framework. As discussed in the previous section, unified transformer architectures with modality-specific components depend critically on the quality of visual representations they receive as input—making the selection and design of the visual encoder not merely a peripheral engineering choice, but a foundational decision that conditions the effectiveness of the entire VLP system. The evolution of visual feature extraction in VLP models can be broadly divided into three paradigms: region-based approaches relying on pretrained object detectors, patch-based approaches using Vision Transformers (ViT), and fully end-to-end visual learning strategies.

**Region-Based Visual Encoders**

Early VLP models predominantly adopted region-based visual features extracted from pretrained object detectors, most commonly Faster R-CNN. In this paradigm, the visual encoder first detects a set of salient object regions within an image and then represents each region as a fixed-dimensional feature vector, which is subsequently fed into the multimodal transformer alongside textual tokens. This approach was motivated by the intuition that object-centric representations naturally align with noun-phrase-level textual descriptions, thereby facilitating cross-modal semantic grounding. Models such as VisualBERT, ViLBERT, and UNITER adopted this strategy and achieved strong performance on vision-language benchmarks including VQA and image-text retrieval.

However, region-based approaches suffer from several well-documented limitations. First, the two-stage pipeline is computationally expensive, as feature extraction using an object detector adds significant overhead before any cross-modal interaction takes place [21]. Second, the representational capacity of the visual encoder is fundamentally bounded by the predefined object vocabulary and detection capabilities of the upstream detector, limiting the model's ability to capture visual information beyond object-level semantics [21]. Third, end-to-end joint optimization of the visual encoder with the multimodal transformer is typically infeasible under this paradigm, constraining the model's ability to learn task-relevant visual representations during pretraining. Similarly, end-to-end VLP frameworks such as KD-VLP explicitly identified these bottlenecks and proposed to bypass the frozen detector by using grid-level CNN features combined with object knowledge distillation to transfer semantic supervision without requiring a two-stage pipeline [26].

**Patch-Based Visual Encoders: Vision Transformers**

The introduction of the Vision Transformer (ViT) provided a compelling alternative to region-based visual encoding. Rather than detecting and cropping object regions, ViT divides an image into a fixed grid of non-overlapping patches and encodes each patch as a token via linear projection, processing the resulting sequence through a standard transformer. This patch-based paradigm eliminates the need for an external object detector and enables fully differentiable visual encoding that can be jointly optimized with the language and fusion components—a property that is particularly valuable in the unified architectures discussed in the preceding section, where modality-specific experts operate directly on the visual token representations produced by the encoder.

The transition to patch-based encoding was partly catalyzed by ViLT [21], which demonstrated that removing convolutional and region-supervision components altogether and instead processing image patches through the same lightweight transformer used for text could yield competitive downstream performance while dramatically accelerating inference. ViLT showed that the processing of visual inputs need not be disproportionately expensive compared to textual inputs, challenging the prevailing assumption that heavy visual feature extractors were necessary for strong VLP performance.

Subsequent work increasingly adopted ViT-based visual backbones, often initialized from large-scale self-supervised or supervised pretraining on image-only datasets. Among these, CLIP-pretrained ViT encoders became particularly popular due to their strong visual representations learned from web-scale image-text contrastive training, providing a powerful initialization for the visual stream in downstream VLP models. The Swin Transformer, with its hierarchical feature extraction via shifted window attention, offered an alternative ViT variant that captures multi-scale visual representations more naturally, benefiting tasks requiring localization and spatial understanding.

**End-to-End Visual Learning**

Beyond patch-based ViTs, a third paradigm has emerged in which the entire visual encoding process is learned end-to-end as an integral component of the VLP framework, rather than relying on pretrained visual backbones frozen during pretraining. SimVLM [4] exemplifies this direction by directly feeding raw image patches into a large transformer encoder-decoder and training the entire model jointly with a prefix language modeling objective on weakly supervised web data, achieving strong performance across both generative and discriminative vision-language tasks without relying on any separately pretrained object detectors or visual encoders.

End-to-end approaches offer the appealing property of allowing visual representations to be shaped directly by the multimodal learning signal rather than being fixed to representations optimized for a different task or dataset distribution. KD-VLP [26] further bridges the gap between region-based and end-to-end paradigms by incorporating object knowledge distillation into an end-to-end framework, using object features and semantic labels from an external detector only as soft supervisory signals rather than as hard input representations, thereby allowing the model to internalize object-level knowledge while retaining end-to-end trainability.

**Impact of Visual Encoder Choice on Downstream Performance**

The impact of visual encoder selection on VLP performance has been studied systematically, revealing that the choice of visual backbone substantially affects both representation quality and task-specific outcomes. The family of CLIP-pretrained ViT models, including ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14, and their variants, has consistently demonstrated superior performance as visual encoders in VLP systems, largely due to the richness of the visual representations acquired through large-scale contrastive pretraining. The Swin Transformer has proven effective for tasks requiring dense spatial predictions. In contrast, raw patch-based ViTs pretrained only on image classification tend to underperform relative to CLIP-ViTs when used as frozen visual encoders in VLP models, underscoring the importance of the pretraining signal used to initialize the visual backbone.

Importantly, the interaction between visual encoder type and cross-modal fusion strategy can produce non-trivial effects on downstream task performance. For instance, using deeper and more expressive visual encoders does not uniformly translate into better cross-modal alignment, as the fusion architecture must also be appropriately matched to the granularity and dimensionality of the visual representations it processes. This interdependence between visual encoding and fusion design directly motivates the analysis in the following section, where the mechanisms by which visual and textual representations are combined are examined in detail.

Overall, the field has broadly converged toward patch-based ViT encoders, particularly CLIP-pretrained variants, as the visual backbone of choice in modern VLP systems, reflecting both their computational efficiency relative to region-based pipelines and their strong representational capacity. Nevertheless, end-to-end learning approaches continue to gain traction, especially in large-scale settings where joint optimization of the visual encoder and the multimodal model can yield superior generalization [4]. As will become clear in the following section, the granularity and dimensionality of the visual representations produced by these encoders are not incidental properties—they directly constrain the cross-modal fusion mechanisms that can be effectively applied, making visual encoder design and fusion strategy fundamentally co-dependent choices in the overall VLP architecture.

### 2.7 Cross-Modal Fusion Mechanisms

Cross-modal fusion is a central design decision in vision-language pretraining (VLP) architectures, determining how visual and textual representations are combined to produce joint multimodal embeddings. Building upon the visual encoding strategies discussed in the previous section—whether region-based, patch-based, or end-to-end—the choice of fusion strategy profoundly affects both representational quality and the range of downstream tasks a model can effectively address. Indeed, even the most expressive visual backbone cannot compensate for a fusion mechanism that fails to adequately integrate visual and linguistic information at the appropriate depth and granularity.

**Merged Attention (Single-Stream Fusion).** The most straightforward fusion paradigm concatenates visual and textual token sequences and feeds them jointly into a shared transformer network, allowing every token to attend to every other token through standard self-attention. Models such as VisualBERT, Unicoder-VL, and UFO adopt this design, enabling deep bidirectional cross-modal interaction from the earliest layers [11; 2]. The advantage of merged attention is that it captures fine-grained cross-modal dependencies early and uniformly across all layers. However, this design is computationally expensive because the sequence length grows with the sum of visual and textual tokens, and it is less amenable to efficient retrieval since image and text representations cannot be independently encoded [11].

**Co-Attention (Dual-Stream Cross-Attention).** An alternative strategy maintains separate unimodal encoders for vision and language and introduces cross-modal interaction through dedicated co-attention modules, where each modality's representations attend to the other's via cross-attention. This design, employed in models such as LXMERT and ViLBERT, permits modality-specific processing to proceed independently before interaction, which can preserve unimodal representational quality and facilitate efficient retrieval by allowing independent encoding of each modality [2; 12]. The depth at which cross-modal attention is introduced—shallow versus deep interaction—significantly affects the trade-off between fusion richness and computational cost.

**Empirical Findings on Fusion Design.** Systematic investigations into fusion mechanism effectiveness reveal that the choice between merged attention and co-attention is not straightforwardly resolved in favor of one paradigm across all tasks. BEiT-3 introduces Multiway Transformers, where modality-specific expert feed-forward networks are interleaved with shared self-attention layers, enabling the model to function simultaneously as a fusion encoder for understanding tasks and as dual unimodal encoders for retrieval [13]. This architecture demonstrates that deep fusion and modality-specific encoding need not be mutually exclusive design choices, pointing toward a more flexible understanding of the fusion design space.

**Gated and Bridging Mechanisms.** Beyond standard attention-based fusion, gated fusion mechanisms incorporate learnable gates or bottleneck modules to control the flow of information between modalities. These mechanisms can reduce noise from irrelevant cross-modal correspondences and allow the model to selectively integrate visual context into language representations. Bridging architectures connect intermediate layers of separately pretrained unimodal encoders to cross-modal encoder layers, enabling bottom-up alignment that captures multi-level semantic correspondences rather than relying solely on top-layer feature fusion [11]. As will be discussed further in the following section, this bridging principle is central to architectures such as BridgeTower, which establishes explicit connections between the intermediate layers of unimodal encoders and the cross-modal encoder to enable progressive multi-level alignment. The Q-Former design in BLIP-2 represents an extreme form of cross-modal bridging, using a small set of learned query tokens to extract relevant visual information from a frozen image encoder and pass it to a language model, effectively decoupling the visual and linguistic processing pipelines while maintaining cross-modal alignment [14].

**Impact of Fusion Depth and Placement.** The depth and placement of cross-modal fusion layers significantly influence representation quality. Fusing only at the top layers, as in many two-tower models, limits the model's ability to capture fine-grained region-word correspondences, while fusing at every layer in single-stream models maximizes interaction but at substantial computational cost. Hybrid designs that perform shallow fusion at some layers and deep fusion at others offer a promising middle ground [2; 13]. For generation tasks, encoder-decoder architectures typically employ cross-attention between the decoder and encoded visual representations at each decoder layer, providing the generative component continuous access to visual context throughout the decoding process [11]. These considerations directly motivate the architectural innovations explored in the following section, where intermediate-layer bridging is shown to substantially improve cross-modal representation quality with minimal additional overhead.

Overall, the diversity of cross-modal fusion mechanisms reflects the breadth of requirements across vision-language tasks: retrieval tasks benefit from independent unimodal encoding with lightweight interaction, dense grounding tasks require deep and fine-grained fusion, and generation tasks demand persistent access to visual information throughout decoding. Critically, fusion strategy cannot be treated as an isolated design choice—it must be co-designed with the visual encoder and pretraining objectives, as the granularity and dimensionality of visual representations directly constrain the fusion mechanisms that can be effectively applied. Empirical studies consistently highlight that fusion strategy should be co-designed with the visual encoder and pretraining objectives to achieve optimal downstream performance [2; 13].

### 2.8 BridgeTower and Multi-Level Cross-Modal Alignment

A fundamental limitation shared by many two-tower vision-language pretraining architectures is their tendency to fuse visual and textual representations only at the topmost layers of their respective unimodal encoders. This design choice restricts the depth and richness of cross-modal interaction, as the cross-modal encoder only receives fully abstracted, high-level representations and cannot benefit from the intermediate semantic levels captured throughout the unimodal encoding process. As introduced in the previous section, bridging architectures address this limitation by establishing explicit connections between intermediate unimodal layers and the cross-modal encoder—and BridgeTower represents the most fully realized instantiation of this principle.

BridgeTower [66] directly addresses this architectural bottleneck by introducing multiple bridge layers that establish explicit connections between the intermediate layers of pretrained unimodal encoders and each corresponding layer of the cross-modal encoder. Rather than waiting until the top-layer outputs of the vision and language encoders are available, BridgeTower enables a bottom-up cross-modal alignment process wherein visual and textual representations at varying levels of semantic abstraction are progressively fused throughout the cross-modal encoding stack. This layer-wise bridging mechanism allows the model to capture correspondences between low-level visual features and their linguistic counterparts, as well as higher-level conceptual alignments, producing richer and more nuanced joint representations.

From an empirical standpoint, BridgeTower demonstrates compelling performance improvements over prior two-tower models while incurring only minimal additional parameters and computational overhead [66]. Specifically, using only 4 million pretraining images, BridgeTower achieves an accuracy of 78.73% on the VQAv2 test-std benchmark, surpassing the state-of-the-art METER model [38] by 1.09% under identical pretraining conditions. When scaled further, BridgeTower attains 81.15% accuracy, outperforming models pretrained on orders-of-magnitude larger datasets, underscoring the efficiency and effectiveness of its multi-level fusion strategy.

The success of BridgeTower highlights a broader architectural insight: the semantic granularity at which cross-modal interaction occurs profoundly influences representation quality. Earlier approaches such as those studied in METER [38] and VLMo [67] demonstrated that the choice of visual encoder and fusion mechanism both matter considerably, but BridgeTower further reveals that the depth and placement of cross-modal fusion—specifically enabling alignment across multiple intermediate layers rather than only at the top—constitutes a critical yet previously underexplored design dimension [66]. By enabling richer bottom-up cross-modal alignment with negligible parameter cost, BridgeTower establishes an important design principle for future VLP architectures seeking to maximize cross-modal representation learning efficiency. Crucially, this principle also informs the following research direction: if deep multi-layer fusion yields such gains, it is natural to ask whether comparably strong performance can be recovered through far simpler connector modules—a question that motivates the investigation into lightweight MLP-based and adapter-style fusion alternatives discussed in the next section.

### 2.9 Lightweight and MLP-Based Architectures for VLP

As vision-language pretraining models have grown increasingly large and computationally demanding, a significant line of research has explored whether the heavy transformer-based fusion modules central to most VLP frameworks can be replaced with simpler, more lightweight alternatives without sacrificing downstream performance. This question is particularly relevant in the context of the architectural evolution described above, where models like BridgeTower demonstrate that richer cross-modal interaction through deeper layer-wise bridging yields performance gains—yet such designs still rely on substantial fusion machinery. A complementary research direction asks whether effective cross-modal alignment can instead be achieved through far simpler connector modules.

One prominent direction investigates the use of multilayer perceptron (MLP) architectures as alternatives to cross-modal transformer fusion. The empirical study on MLP-based vision-language fusion (MLP-ViL) revealed that simple MLP connectors, when combined with strong unimodal encoders and appropriate pretraining objectives, can close much of the performance gap traditionally attributed to deep transformer fusion networks. A key finding from this line of work is that VL pretraining itself plays a critical role in compensating for the reduced expressiveness of MLP fusion layers: with sufficient pretraining data and well-designed objectives, all-MLP VL models can achieve competitive results across standard benchmarks such as VQA and image-text retrieval, challenging the assumption that transformer-based cross-modal attention is strictly necessary for effective multimodal representation learning.

This insight has broader implications for the architectural design of VLP systems. Rather than designing ever-deeper fusion transformers, lightweight connector modules can serve as practical bridges between frozen or lightly fine-tuned unimodal encoders. This philosophy underlies parameter-efficient designs such as the Querying Transformer (Q-Former) introduced in BLIP-2, which uses a small trainable module with a limited number of learnable query tokens to extract relevant visual information from a frozen image encoder and pass it to a large language model, enabling effective vision-language alignment at a fraction of the computational cost of full end-to-end pretraining.

Adapter-based approaches extend this idea further by inserting small bottleneck modules into pretrained unimodal networks rather than training large fusion transformers from scratch. Models such as MAGMA augment frozen autoregressive language models with lightweight visual adapter layers, enabling the language model to condition on visual inputs while keeping the majority of parameters fixed. These designs are particularly attractive in settings where computational resources are limited or where the goal is to rapidly adapt general-purpose models to vision-language tasks.

The convergence of MLP-based fusion, adapter layers, and lightweight cross-attention connectors reflects a broader trend in VLP architecture design: the recognition that the expressiveness of unimodal encoders, the quality of pretraining objectives, and the scale of pretraining data often matter more than the complexity of the fusion module itself [38]. This has democratized VLP research by enabling practitioners to build competitive vision-language systems without access to the massive computational infrastructure required by earlier region-feature-based or heavily parameterized transformer fusion architectures. As the following section discusses, this shift toward modularity and parameter efficiency becomes even more pronounced when scaling vision-language systems to billions of parameters, where the ability to freeze large pretrained components and train only lightweight connectors becomes not merely convenient but essential.

### 2.10 Scalable and Parameter-Efficient Architectural Adaptations

As vision-language pretraining models have grown increasingly large and computationally demanding, a significant line of research has explored whether the heavy transformer-based fusion modules central to most VLP frameworks can be replaced with simpler, more lightweight alternatives without sacrificing downstream performance. This question is particularly relevant in the context of the architectural evolution described above, where models like BridgeTower demonstrate that richer cross-modal interaction through deeper layer-wise bridging yields performance gains—yet such designs still rely on substantial fusion machinery. A complementary research direction asks whether effective cross-modal alignment can instead be achieved through far simpler connector modules, and more broadly, how large-scale pretrained unimodal encoders can be leveraged without full retraining through parameter-efficient architectural adaptations.

A central insight motivating this line of work is that large-scale pretrained unimodal encoders—whether visual transformers or language models—already encode rich representations that can be leveraged without full retraining. The challenge then becomes designing efficient connectors that translate between modality-specific representation spaces. Several architectural strategies have been developed to this end, including adapter layers inserted into transformer blocks, prompt and prefix tuning methods that prepend learnable tokens to frozen model inputs, and more sophisticated cross-attention bridging modules that mediate information exchange between frozen visual encoders and autoregressive language models.

One prominent direction investigates the use of multilayer perceptron (MLP) architectures as alternatives to cross-modal transformer fusion. The empirical study on MLP-based vision-language fusion (MLP-ViL) revealed that simple MLP connectors, when combined with strong unimodal encoders and appropriate pretraining objectives, can close much of the performance gap traditionally attributed to deep transformer fusion networks. A key finding from this line of work is that VL pretraining itself plays a critical role in compensating for the reduced expressiveness of MLP fusion layers: with sufficient pretraining data and well-designed objectives, all-MLP VL models can achieve competitive results across standard benchmarks such as VQA and image-text retrieval, challenging the assumption that transformer-based cross-modal attention is strictly necessary for effective multimodal representation learning.

Adapter-based approaches extend this philosophy further by inserting small bottleneck modules into pretrained unimodal networks rather than training large fusion transformers from scratch. This paradigm has proven effective in parameter-efficient fine-tuning across both NLP and vision domains, and its extension to vision-language settings has enabled practical deployment of large VLP systems. Models such as MAGMA exemplify this direction by augmenting frozen autoregressive language models with lightweight visual adapter layers that inject image features into the language modeling pipeline, enabling image-conditioned text generation with only a small fraction of trainable parameters while keeping the majority of parameters fixed.

Perhaps the most influential architectural innovation in this space is BLIP-2's Querying Transformer (Q-Former), which introduces a lightweight transformer architecture that sits between a frozen image encoder and a frozen large language model. The Q-Former contains a fixed set of learnable query tokens that interact with frozen image features through cross-attention, extracting the most task-relevant visual information and projecting it into the input space of the language model. This design decouples visual feature extraction from language generation, allowing each component to be independently scaled and updated. The Q-Former is trained in two stages: first to align visual and language representations, and then to connect visual features to language generation, all while keeping both the image encoder and language model frozen. This two-stage strategy significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters while preserving the knowledge encoded in large pretrained foundations.

Prompt tuning and prefix tuning offer complementary parameter-efficient strategies. Rather than modifying model weights, these approaches prepend learnable continuous vectors to the input sequence of frozen transformers, steering their behavior toward vision-language tasks. Such methods have been applied in VLP contexts to adapt large pretrained language models for multimodal understanding without modifying any existing weights, making them particularly attractive when computational resources are limited or when the pretrained model must be shared across many tasks.

Cross-attention bridging modules represent another architectural pattern, where a lightweight cross-attention layer is introduced to allow a language model to selectively attend to visual features produced by a frozen visual encoder. This design preserves the autoregressive generation capabilities of the language model while granting it access to visual context through a minimal parameter overhead. The flexibility of such connectors allows them to be paired with a wide range of frozen visual backbones and language models, enabling modular composition of pretrained components.

The convergence of MLP-based fusion, adapter layers, lightweight cross-attention connectors, and prompt-based methods reflects a broader trend in VLP architecture design: the recognition that the expressiveness of unimodal encoders, the quality of pretraining objectives, and the scale of pretraining data often matter more than the complexity of the fusion module itself. The practical implications of these parameter-efficient architectural adaptations are significant. By freezing large pretrained models and training only lightweight connectors, researchers can perform vision-language pretraining and adaptation on hardware that would be insufficient for full-model training. Moreover, the modular nature of these designs facilitates continual updates: new visual encoders or language models can be swapped in with minimal retraining of the connector module. This composability is especially valuable as the field rapidly advances and new foundation models are regularly released.

This shift toward modularity and parameter efficiency connects directly to the comparative architectural principles examined in the following section. As the subsequent analysis demonstrates, the choice of lightweight connector versus deep fusion transformer is but one dimension of a broader set of design decisions—spanning visual encoder type, parameter sharing strategy, and scalability considerations—that collectively determine the suitability of a VLP architecture for understanding versus generation tasks. Collectively, these scalable and parameter-efficient architectural adaptations represent a pragmatic and increasingly popular paradigm for VLP, enabling the community to leverage the growing power of large pretrained unimodal models while maintaining tractable training costs and supporting flexible deployment across diverse downstream vision-language tasks.

### 2.11 Comparative Analysis and Architectural Design Principles

## 2.11 Comparative Analysis and Architectural Design Principles

Having surveyed the diverse landscape of VLP architectures—from single-stream and dual-stream fusion models, through encoder-decoder designs, to the parameter-efficient bridging modules discussed in the preceding section—it is instructive to synthesize the key empirical findings and distill actionable design principles across several critical dimensions: fusion strategy, visual encoder type, parameter sharing, scalability, and suitability for understanding versus generation tasks. This comparative analysis serves both to consolidate the architectural insights developed throughout this chapter and to provide a principled foundation for the pretraining objectives and downstream adaptation strategies discussed in subsequent sections.

**Fusion Strategy.** The choice between single-stream and dual-stream fusion has profound implications for both task performance and computational efficiency. Single-stream models, which concatenate visual and textual tokens and process them through a shared transformer, enable deep cross-modal interaction at every layer and generally excel on understanding tasks such as VQA and visual reasoning. Dual-stream models, by contrast, maintain separate unimodal encoders and perform cross-modal fusion only at select layers or through contrastive objectives, making them highly efficient for retrieval tasks but potentially less expressive for tasks requiring fine-grained cross-modal reasoning. Studies such as those underlying METER have empirically confirmed that the depth and placement of cross-modal fusion significantly affect representation quality, with deeper fusion generally yielding stronger understanding performance at the cost of increased computation. Furthermore, BridgeTower demonstrated that fusing only at the topmost layer of unimodal encoders leaves valuable intermediate-level semantic information unused, and that introducing bridge layers connecting intermediate encoder representations to each layer of the cross-modal encoder substantially improves alignment quality with minimal additional parameters. This finding suggests that **multi-level cross-modal fusion is preferable to single top-layer fusion** when computational budget permits.

**Visual Encoder Type.** The evolution from region-based Faster R-CNN features to patch-based Vision Transformer (ViT) encoders represents one of the most impactful architectural transitions in VLP. Region-based features, while semantically rich and well-aligned with object-level tasks, introduce computational bottlenecks due to the dependency on offline object detectors and the inability to be optimized end-to-end during pretraining. Patch-based encoders, popularized by ViT and its variants such as CLIP-ViT and Swin Transformer, offer end-to-end differentiability, simpler pipelines, and competitive or superior downstream performance. Comparative studies have shown that the choice of visual backbone substantially affects downstream task performance, with stronger visual encoders generally leading to better VLP outcomes. End-to-end approaches such as E2E-VLP [34] further eliminate the two-stage bottleneck by jointly learning visual representations and cross-modal alignments within a unified transformer encoder-decoder, demonstrating that full end-to-end training can match or exceed the performance of pipeline-based methods. The general principle emerging from these findings is that **end-to-end, patch-based visual encoders with strong unimodal pretraining (e.g., CLIP-ViT) provide the best foundation for modern VLP systems**.

**Parameter Sharing and Modality-Specific Components.** A recurring design tension in VLP architecture is the degree to which parameters should be shared across modalities. Fully shared parameters promote compact models and reduce overfitting but may fail to capture modality-specific nuances. Architectures such as VLMo introduce Mixture-of-Modality-Experts (MoME), where feed-forward network experts are modality-specific while self-attention layers remain shared, enabling the model to simultaneously function as a dual encoder for retrieval and a fusion encoder for understanding tasks. Similarly, EVE employs modality-aware sparse Mixture-of-Experts to handle visual and textual tokens differently within a unified transformer, and BEiT-3's Multiway Transformers implement modality-specific feed-forward networks alongside shared self-attention, achieving flexible deployment across diverse tasks. These designs converge on a clear principle: **partial parameter sharing with modality-specific expert modules offers the best balance between representational capacity, flexibility, and parameter efficiency**. Notably, this principle connects directly to the parameter-efficient bridging paradigm discussed in the preceding section, where lightweight connectors and frozen unimodal encoders achieve a complementary form of modular parameter management.

**Scalability.** Scalability is a critical consideration as VLP models are increasingly trained on web-scale datasets with billions of parameters. Fully shared single-stream models scale straightforwardly but may become computationally prohibitive for retrieval at inference time, since each image-text pair requires a joint forward pass. Dual-stream and MoE-based architectures offer better scalability for deployment, as unimodal encoders can independently encode queries and keys. Lightweight architectural adaptations, including adapter layers, MLP connectors, and Q-Former bridging modules, provide effective mechanisms for scaling large frozen unimodal models to multimodal tasks with significantly reduced trainable parameters—a point thoroughly illustrated in the preceding discussion of parameter-efficient architectures. Free language modeling (FLM) [44] addresses pretraining scalability by decoupling prediction rate from corruption rate in masked language modeling, achieving up to 2.5× pretraining speedup while maintaining competitive performance, highlighting that **pretraining objective design is as important as architectural design for scalability**.

**Understanding vs. Generation Tasks.** A fundamental axis of differentiation among VLP architectures is their suitability for understanding versus generation tasks. Understanding tasks such as VQA, image-text retrieval, and visual reasoning benefit from deep bidirectional cross-modal attention that allows unrestricted information flow between modalities. Generation tasks such as image captioning and visual question generation require autoregressive decoding with causal masking, which is incompatible with unrestricted bidirectional attention. Encoder-decoder architectures such as those in VLP [6] and E2E-VLP [34] address this by using shared transformer networks with different attention masks for encoding and decoding stages. The decoupled encoder-decoder design in TDEN [68] goes further, employing two separate cross-modal encoder and decoder streams that independently handle understanding and generation proxy tasks, thereby avoiding the architectural compromises required by fully shared designs. Moreover, TDEN [68] introduces scheduled sampling to mitigate the train-test discrepancy caused by mask token substitution during pretraining, demonstrating that **training strategy choices such as scheduled sampling are critical complements to architectural design in encoder-decoder VLP frameworks**.

**Distilled Design Principles.** Synthesizing across the reviewed architectures, several actionable principles emerge for practitioners designing or selecting VLP systems:

1. **Prefer patch-based end-to-end visual encoders** initialized from strong unimodal pretraining (e.g., CLIP-ViT) over pipeline-based region feature extractors, as they offer better efficiency, flexibility, and end-to-end optimization.

2. **Employ multi-level cross-modal fusion** rather than fusing only at the topmost layer, as intermediate-layer feature alignment captures richer hierarchical semantics and leads to stronger downstream performance.

3. **Use modality-specific expert components** within shared transformer backbones to balance representational capacity across modalities while maintaining flexible deployment as both fusion and dual encoders.

4. **Decouple encoder and decoder** in models targeting both understanding and generation, using appropriate attention masking and training strategies such as scheduled sampling to bridge the gap between pretraining and fine-tuning [68].

5. **Leverage lightweight connectors and parameter-efficient adapters** when integrating large pretrained unimodal models, as they provide competitive performance with substantially reduced computational and memory overhead.

6. **Optimize pretraining objectives alongside architecture**, as approaches such as FLM [44] demonstrate that objective design innovations can yield substantial efficiency gains independent of architectural changes.

**Current Gaps and Open Questions.** Despite significant progress, several important open questions remain in VLP architecture research. First, the optimal balance between shared and modality-specific parameters across diverse tasks and scales is not fully understood, and systematic ablation studies across a wider range of downstream tasks are needed. Second, the interaction between visual encoder pretraining quality and cross-modal fusion design remains underexplored; it is unclear whether improvements in visual backbone pretraining can substitute for more sophisticated fusion mechanisms. Third, most architectural comparisons are conducted on standard English image-text benchmarks, and it remains an open question whether design principles generalize to multilingual, video-language, and 3D scene understanding settings. Fourth, the trade-off between architectural flexibility and task specialization—particularly for highly heterogeneous task distributions—calls for further investigation into mixture-of-experts and modular architectures at larger scales. Addressing these gaps will be essential for advancing the next generation of VLP architectures toward greater generality, efficiency, and robustness, and will inform the pretraining strategies and objective formulations examined in the chapters that follow.

## 3 Pretraining Objectives and Learning Strategies

### 3.1 Overview and Taxonomy of Pretraining Objectives

Pretraining objectives lie at the heart of vision-language pretraining (VLP), as they determine what kinds of cross-modal correspondences a model learns and how effectively visual and textual representations become aligned. Over the course of VLP research, a rich and diverse ecosystem of objectives has emerged, each designed to capture different facets of the relationship between images and text. To provide a principled understanding of this landscape, it is useful to organize these objectives into a structured taxonomy, broadly categorized into three families: **discriminative**, **generative**, and **contrastive** objectives.

**Discriminative objectives** aim to train the model to distinguish between matched and mismatched image-text pairs, or to predict masked tokens conditioned on cross-modal context. The most prominent example is Masked Language Modeling (MLM), which requires the model to predict masked textual tokens given visual context, encouraging the model to ground linguistic concepts in visual content. Closely related is image-text matching (ITM), a binary classification objective that trains the model to determine whether a given image and text are semantically aligned. These objectives, widely adopted across foundational VLP models, are central to learning discriminative cross-modal representations [2; 21]. As the following subsection will discuss in detail, MLM in particular has given rise to a rich family of variants—including saliency-based masking, modality-aware masking, and Free Language Modeling—that progressively refine the learning signal to better account for the structure of multimodal data.

**Generative objectives** encourage the model to produce coherent textual or visual outputs conditioned on cross-modal inputs, thereby learning to capture the compositional and sequential structure of language grounded in visual content. Representative generative objectives include sequence-to-sequence masked prediction, prefix language modeling, and autoregressive language modeling, which are used to support downstream tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering [6; 4]. Unlike purely discriminative approaches, generative objectives require the model to not only recognize correspondences but also produce fluent and contextually appropriate outputs, lending themselves naturally to generation-oriented downstream tasks.

**Contrastive objectives** operate by pulling aligned image-text pairs closer together in a shared embedding space while pushing apart non-matching pairs, typically using in-batch negatives or hard negatives mined from large data collections. Contrastive learning has proven particularly effective for tasks such as zero-shot image-text retrieval and open-vocabulary recognition [1]. Contrastive objectives excel at learning globally aligned representations across modalities but may be insufficient alone for fine-grained understanding tasks.

In practice, modern VLP frameworks rarely rely on a single objective in isolation. Instead, they combine objectives from multiple families to capture complementary aspects of visual-textual alignment. For instance, BLIP [5] jointly employs contrastive learning, image-text matching, and language modeling objectives within a single model, enabling it to excel at both understanding and generation tasks simultaneously. This multi-objective strategy reflects the broader insight that discriminative objectives excel at encoding semantic correspondences, contrastive objectives promote globally coherent embedding spaces, and generative objectives ensure that the model acquires sufficient compositional language understanding grounded in vision.

The choice and combination of pretraining objectives also interact closely with architectural design decisions. Single-stream fusion encoders are well-suited to discriminative objectives such as MLM and ITM, while encoder-decoder architectures naturally accommodate generative objectives. Dual-stream two-tower models are the dominant setting for contrastive learning, where independent encoders for each modality allow efficient similarity computation. As surveyed in [2], different objective families exhibit varying strengths across downstream tasks, motivating the field's trend toward unified frameworks that integrate multiple objectives. With this taxonomy established, the following subsections examine each objective family in depth, analyzing their formulations, variants, and roles within the broader VLP training paradigm—beginning with the discriminative family and, in particular, the foundational and evolving role of masked language modeling.

### 3.2 Masked Language Modeling and Its Variants

Masked Language Modeling (MLM), originally introduced in BERT-style language pretraining, has become one of the most foundational pretraining objectives in vision-language pretraining (VLP). In the standard NLP setting, MLM randomly masks a subset of input tokens and trains the model to predict the masked tokens from their surrounding context. When adapted to vision-language settings, cross-modal MLM extends this paradigm by conditioning the prediction of masked textual tokens not only on the surrounding textual context but also on the paired visual input, thereby encouraging the model to learn grounded cross-modal representations that connect visual content with linguistic semantics. As introduced in the preceding taxonomy, MLM belongs to the discriminative family of pretraining objectives and serves as a cornerstone of cross-modal representation learning alongside image-text matching objectives.

Early VLP models such as those surveyed in [2] and [11] established cross-modal MLM as a standard pretraining objective, typically applied alongside image-text matching objectives to jointly learn fine-grained textual grounding and global image-text alignment. In these settings, a fixed proportion of text tokens—commonly 15% following the original BERT protocol—are replaced with a special [69] token, and the model leverages visual region features extracted from object detectors alongside unmasked language tokens to reconstruct the corrupted text. This formulation encourages the model to associate specific visual regions with textual concepts, fostering a tightly coupled multimodal representation.

Beyond this standard formulation, several advanced variants of MLM have been proposed to address its limitations and improve learning efficiency or representation quality. Saliency-based masking strategies selectively mask tokens that are semantically salient or visually grounded, prioritizing the learning of meaningful cross-modal correspondences over random masking of function words or prepositions that provide little visual grounding signal. Modality-aware masking approaches further differentiate the masking strategy based on the nature of tokens, applying distinct masking schedules or rates to visual and linguistic tokens to better balance the learning signal across modalities.

Structural knowledge-enhanced masking, as exemplified by approaches that incorporate scene graphs or relational knowledge into the masking process, selectively masks entity mentions, relational phrases, or attribute tokens guided by structural semantic annotations, ensuring that the model is exposed to rich relational and compositional learning signals rather than learning primarily from high-frequency or low-information tokens. This direction aligns with broader efforts in VLP to move beyond surface-level token statistics toward deeper semantic and structural understanding.

A notable advancement in MLM-variant design is Free Language Modeling (FLM), which decouples the prediction rate from the corruption rate that is tightly coupled in standard MLM. In conventional MLM, only masked tokens contribute to the training loss, meaning that the proportion of tokens masked directly determines both the difficulty of the prediction task and the density of the learning signal. FLM relaxes this constraint by allowing the model to predict any token in the sequence regardless of whether it was masked, effectively increasing the number of training targets per sample and accelerating convergence. This approach has been shown to improve training efficiency in VLP settings where the cross-modal MLM signal can be sparse, particularly when applied to short image descriptions with limited masking opportunities.

Together, these variants of MLM reflect the growing recognition that the design of pretraining objectives in VLP must account for the unique structure of multimodal data, where the density, informativeness, and cross-modal grounding of masked tokens significantly affect the quality of learned representations [13]. The evolution from simple random masking toward principled, semantics-aware, and efficiency-optimized masking strategies highlights the importance of pretraining objective design as a key lever for improving VLP model performance across diverse downstream vision-language tasks [2]. Notably, while these MLM variants operate primarily on the textual side of the multimodal input, a parallel and equally important family of objectives targets the visual side—namely masked visual and region modeling, which will be examined in the following subsection.

### 3.3 Masked Visual and Region Modeling

Masked visual and region modeling constitutes a critical family of pretraining objectives in vision-language pretraining, complementing the masked language modeling and image-text matching objectives discussed above by enforcing representation learning on the visual side. Just as cross-modal MLM encourages models to ground textual understanding in visual content, masked visual modeling objectives train models to reconstruct or predict visual content from partial observations, thereby learning fine-grained and semantically meaningful visual features that support downstream understanding tasks.

**Region-Based Visual Masking**

Early VLP models adopted region-based visual masking strategies, largely influenced by the availability of pretrained object detectors such as Faster R-CNN that provided region-of-interest (RoI) features. In these approaches, a subset of detected region features is masked or corrupted, and the model is trained to either regress the original feature vectors (masked region feature regression) or predict the object category of the masked region (masked region classification). These objectives were integral to foundational VLP models such as ViLBERT [23], where masked region prediction served as a visually grounded pretraining signal. The intuition is that by forcing the multimodal transformer to reconstruct masked visual regions using the surrounding image context and associated text, the model learns richer cross-modal correspondences at the object and attribute level—an objective that directly parallels the token reconstruction goals of cross-modal MLM but operates in the visual domain.

However, region-based supervision introduces several limitations. It tightly couples VLP performance to the quality and vocabulary of the external object detector, restricting the range of visual concepts that can be learned. Furthermore, the two-stage pipeline involving separate feature extraction and multimodal modeling creates significant computational overhead, as highlighted in efficiency-focused analyses [21].

**Patch-Based and End-to-End Visual Masking**

With the advent of Vision Transformers (ViT), patch-based visual masking has emerged as a compelling alternative that addresses many of the limitations inherent in region-based approaches. Instead of relying on detected regions, these approaches divide images into fixed-size patches and mask a random subset of patch tokens, training the model to predict either the raw pixel values, discrete visual tokens, or patch-level features. This paradigm is more computationally efficient and does not require external detector supervision, enabling fully end-to-end VLP training.

BEiT-3 [13] exemplifies this direction by treating images as a foreign language ("Imglish") and applying unified masked prediction across images, texts, and image-text pairs within a Multiway Transformer architecture. Masked image modeling on patch tokens is performed jointly with masked language modeling on text, enabling both modalities to benefit from shared masked prediction objectives within a single training framework. This unified approach achieves strong performance across a diverse set of vision-language benchmarks, demonstrating the power of patch-based visual masking as a pretraining signal.

Similarly, VL-BEiT [70] introduces a generative pretraining paradigm where masked prediction is performed on monomodal images, monomodal texts, and multimodal image-text pairs using a single shared transformer backbone. For visual inputs, VL-BEiT performs masked image modeling on patch tokens, which complements masked language modeling and masked vision-language modeling objectives. The unified framework allows the model to learn transferable visual representations that generalize well not only to vision-language tasks such as VQA and image-text retrieval but also to unimodal vision tasks including image classification and semantic segmentation.

**Generative Masked Autoencoder Approaches**

Beyond discriminative masked prediction, generative masked autoencoder frameworks have been introduced to further improve visual representation quality in VLP. VLMAE [32] explicitly adopts visual generative learning within a VLP context, employing masked autoencoders to reconstruct masked image patches. The motivation behind VLMAE is to address the problem of focal bias, a phenomenon where models over-focus on a limited set of salient visual regions due to information disparity between image and text modalities—a problem that bears a structural resemblance to the modality imbalance issues observed in cross-modal MLM and ITM objectives. By training the model to attend to and reconstruct a comprehensive set of image patches rather than only the most salient ones, VLMAE encourages more balanced and fine-grained visual feature learning. Experimental results demonstrate that VLMAE achieves improved performance on visual question answering, image-text retrieval, and visual grounding, while also yielding up to 20% pretraining speedup through efficient masking-based training.

**Contrasting Region-Based and Patch-Based Approaches**

Region-based masking objectives offer strong semantic grounding through object-level supervision, making them particularly effective for tasks requiring fine-grained localization and object-level reasoning. However, they are constrained by detector vocabulary and computational inefficiency. Patch-based and end-to-end objectives, by contrast, are more scalable, flexible, and do not presuppose any fixed visual vocabulary. The transition from region-based to patch-based visual masking reflects a broader architectural shift in VLP from CNN-based feature extractors toward ViT-based visual encoders, mirroring the evolution from random to semantics-aware masking strategies observed in the MLM literature.

End-to-end VLP frameworks such as E2E-VLP [34] and KD-VLP [26] further demonstrate this trajectory, incorporating object detection and image captioning objectives within unified transformer architectures to jointly learn visual representations and cross-modal alignments, thereby eliminating the dependency on frozen external detectors while maintaining rich visual supervision through distillation from object-level knowledge.

Collectively, masked visual and region modeling objectives play an essential role in grounding language representations in visual content, promoting fine-grained feature learning, and complementing the masked language modeling and image-text matching objectives examined in previous sections to produce more comprehensive and robust multimodal representations for a wide range of downstream vision-language tasks.

### 3.4 Image-Text Matching and Alignment Objectives

Image-text matching (ITM) stands as one of the most fundamental discriminative pretraining objectives in vision-language pretraining, tasking models with determining whether a given image-text pair is semantically aligned or not. In its simplest form, ITM is formulated as a binary classification problem: given an image-text pair, a model must predict whether the two modalities are genuinely paired or artificially mismatched. This objective directly encourages the model to learn holistic cross-modal correspondences and has been adopted widely across single-stream and dual-stream architectures alike [71; 34]. As a discriminative pretraining signal, ITM complements the masked language modeling objectives discussed in previous sections by operating at the level of global image-text correspondence rather than token-level reconstruction, and it lays the groundwork for the contrastive learning paradigms explored in the following section.

A critical design consideration in ITM is the construction of negative examples. Early approaches relied on random sampling of mismatched pairs from within a training batch, but such random negatives are often too easy for models to distinguish, providing limited learning signal. To address this, **hard negative mining** strategies have been developed to select challenging negative pairs whose semantic content is close but not perfectly aligned, thereby compelling the model to develop more nuanced cross-modal understanding. In-batch hard negative sampling, as employed in contrastive and matching frameworks, selects the most semantically similar but mismatched counterparts within a mini-batch, significantly improving the discriminative power of the ITM objective. This challenge of identifying informative negatives is closely related to the design of contrastive objectives, where the quality of negatives similarly governs the richness of the alignment signal.

A related challenge in ITM training is the problem of **false negatives**—pairs that are incorrectly labeled as negative despite being semantically compatible. This issue arises naturally in large-scale web-crawled datasets where multiple captions may accurately describe the same image or where different images share highly similar semantic content. False negatives can actively harm model training by penalizing correct cross-modal associations. Methods designed to handle false negatives, such as label smoothing and soft negative conversion techniques, seek to reduce this interference by assigning fractional or soft labels to suspected false negatives, thereby improving the robustness of the ITM signal under noisy data conditions.

Beyond binary matching, researchers have explored richer alignment objectives that address specific shortcomings of standard ITM. One important limitation identified in cross-modal masked language modeling (MLM) is **modality bias**: models trained with standard MLM objectives tend to over-rely on textual context for token prediction and under-utilize the visual modality, since visible (unmasked) tokens already provide strong language priors for predicting masked tokens. This results in the visual information being insufficiently leveraged during pretraining. The image-token consistency task, motivated by the need to address both modality bias and the under-utilization of unmasked tokens in cross-modal MLM, encourages the model to maintain consistency between image representations and all text tokens—not just the masked ones—thereby promoting more balanced and thorough visual-linguistic alignment across the full input sequence.

The SyncMask approach further refines ITM-style training by addressing information mismatches that arise in datasets where image and text do not co-express the same content uniformly [72]. By synchronizing the masking of image patches and text tokens to focus on co-occurring information, SyncMask improves the reliability of matching-based objectives and mitigates the degradation that occurs when non-co-occurring elements are inadvertently masked, thereby strengthening fine-grained visual-textual alignment. This synchronized masking strategy shares a conceptual affinity with the patch-based and region-based visual masking approaches discussed in the masked visual modeling section, as both seek to guide the model's attention toward semantically meaningful correspondences.

Additionally, structural and semantic augmentations have been incorporated into the ITM framework to provide richer supervision. For instance, incorporating object tags as semantic anchors during image-text alignment, as in [71], transforms the matching task from a raw pixel-to-text problem into a more semantically structured one, easing the alignment learning by leveraging salient object-level correspondences. Similarly, multi-level semantic alignment strategies [29; 27] extend ITM-style objectives beyond coarse image-sentence matching to encompass phrase-region and concept-level matching, enriching the granularity of cross-modal alignment supervision.

Collectively, these advances reflect a progression from simple binary ITM formulations toward more sophisticated alignment objectives that account for hard negatives, false negatives, modality balance, and multi-granular semantic structure, each contributing to more robust and comprehensive cross-modal representation learning. This trajectory from coarse binary matching toward fine-grained and scalable alignment naturally motivates the contrastive learning paradigms examined in the following section, which extend these alignment principles to operate over continuous similarity landscapes across large sets of pairs.

### 3.5 Contrastive Learning Objectives

Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful pretraining paradigm in vision-language pretraining (VLP), enabling models to align visual and textual representations by pulling together semantically matched image-text pairs while pushing apart mismatched ones in a shared embedding space. Unlike discriminative objectives such as image-text matching (ITM)—which rely on binary labels over individual pairs—contrastive objectives operate over large sets of in-batch negatives, providing dense and scalable training signals without requiring expensive bounding box or region-level annotations. While ITM directly encourages holistic cross-modal correspondence through binary classification, contrastive learning complements this by optimizing a continuous similarity landscape across many pairs simultaneously, offering a richer and more flexible alignment signal. Together, these objectives form a complementary foundation for cross-modal representation learning, with contrastive pretraining particularly suited to scaling across modalities and enabling zero-shot transfer.

**Global Image-Text Contrastive Learning**

The most widely adopted form of contrastive learning in VLP operates at the global image-sentence level. In this paradigm, a visual encoder and a text encoder independently produce unimodal representations, which are then aligned through a contrastive loss such as InfoNCE. This approach encourages the model to maximize the similarity between paired image-text embeddings while minimizing similarity with all other non-paired samples in the batch. Such global contrastive objectives have proven highly effective for learning transferable cross-modal representations and form the foundation of prominent models including CLIP and ALIGN. The contrastive objective directly benefits downstream tasks requiring coarse-grained cross-modal alignment, particularly image-text retrieval, where the dual-encoder structure supports efficient large-scale search. Building upon such frameworks, [73] proposes an unbiased contrastive visual-linguistic pretraining (CVLP) approach that constructs visual self-supervised losses built upon contrastive learning, demonstrating the superiority of contrastive objectives over conventional region regression and classification losses in alleviating domain gap and noisy label issues.

**Dense Region-Word Contrastive Learning**

While global contrastive learning aligns images and sentences at a coarse level, many downstream tasks require fine-grained understanding of spatial regions and their correspondences with specific words or phrases. To address this, dense contrastive learning approaches extend the contrastive paradigm to the region-word level. [45] introduces region-word contrastive learning as a novel pretraining task within its unified grounded vision-language understanding framework. In GLIPv2, contrastive signals are established between image region features and corresponding word embeddings, enabling the model to learn fine-grained localization-aware representations without requiring dense bounding box supervision beyond phrase grounding reformulations of detection tasks. This dense contrastive objective is complemented by phrase grounding and masked language modeling, creating a unified pretraining framework that simultaneously improves localization and language understanding performance. The region-word contrastive approach demonstrates that fine-grained cross-modal alignment can be achieved through contrastive objectives applied at sub-image granularity, substantially benefiting tasks such as visual grounding and open-vocabulary object detection.

Similarly, [73] demonstrates through evaluation on VQA, GQA, and NLVR2 tasks that contrastive visual objectives provide more robust multimodal representations by constructing self-supervised visual contrastive losses that are less susceptible to the noise and domain gap inherent in region feature extraction pipelines based on Visual Genome pretraining.

**Egocentric-Aware Contrastive Objectives**

Standard image-text contrastive objectives assume that paired samples share complete semantic correspondence, which may not hold in specialized domains such as egocentric video understanding. Egocentric video-language data is characterized by first-person perspectives, action-centric content, and fine-grained temporal dynamics that standard global contrastive objectives fail to capture adequately. Egocentric-aware contrastive learning, such as the EgoNCE objective, addresses these challenges by adapting the contrastive formulation to account for the unique properties of egocentric data, including interactions with objects and hands, nuanced action distinctions, and temporally localized events. Such objectives mine hard negatives that are semantically similar at a coarse level but differ in action-relevant details, forcing the model to develop more discriminative representations suitable for downstream tasks like natural language query, moment query, and action recognition.

**Masked Contrastive Alignment**

Another significant extension of contrastive learning in VLP combines masking-based pretraining with contrastive objectives to achieve robust cross-modal alignment. Masked contrastive alignment paradigms apply contrastive losses over representations derived from masked inputs, encouraging the model to learn alignment that is robust to partial input corruption. By contrasting representations of masked and unmasked counterparts alongside cross-modal pairs, such approaches bridge the gap between masked prediction objectives and contrastive alignment, enabling the model to leverage both reconstruction signals and global alignment supervision simultaneously. This strategy is particularly relevant when pretraining on noisy web-crawled data, where individual tokens or regions may be unreliable, and robust alignment requires leveraging broader context.

**Consistency and Multi-View Contrastive Learning**

Beyond standard positive-negative pair construction, contrastive objectives in VLP have been extended to enforce consistency across multiple views or augmented versions of the same content. [74] introduces a multi-modal alignment module based on cross-modal and intra-modal contrastive objectives within a unified backbone architecture for vision-language tracking, demonstrating that contrastive learning applied jointly across modalities and within each modality provides more semantically coherent representations for downstream tracking tasks. The cross-modal contrastive component aligns vision and language features, while the intra-modal contrastive objective encourages consistency among different augmented views of the same modality.

**Contrastive Learning for Structured and Event-Level Alignment**

Beyond object-level and region-level contrastive learning, recent work extends the contrastive paradigm to structured semantic units such as events and their argument structures. [75] proposes a contrastive learning framework that enforces vision-language pretraining models to comprehend events and associated argument roles. By leveraging text information extraction technologies to obtain event structural knowledge and designing multiple prompt functions to contrast hard negative descriptions through event structure manipulation, CLIP-Event achieves event-level contrastive alignment. Additionally, an event graph alignment loss based on optimal transport captures event argument structures, enabling the model to go beyond object recognition and understand complex event semantics in images.

**Summary and Design Considerations**

Across the various contrastive learning formulations reviewed, several common design considerations emerge. First, the quality and difficulty of negative samples critically impacts contrastive learning effectiveness, motivating strategies such as hard negative mining, in-batch sampling with large batch sizes, and momentum queues for maintaining large and diverse negative sets—considerations that echo the hard negative and false negative challenges discussed in the context of ITM objectives. Second, the granularity of contrastive alignment—global, regional, or token-level—determines the types of downstream tasks for which the learned representations are most suitable. Third, combining contrastive objectives with complementary pretraining tasks such as masked language modeling [45] or image-text matching enriches the learned representations by capturing both alignment and fine-grained understanding signals. Finally, contrastive objectives offer a principled mechanism for learning from noisy web-crawled data, as the soft contrastive signal over many negatives is naturally more robust to individual incorrect pairings than hard binary classification objectives. Together, these properties make contrastive learning a cornerstone strategy in modern large-scale VLP frameworks, naturally bridging discriminative alignment objectives and the generative pretraining paradigms that will be explored in the following section.

### 3.6 Generative and Sequence-to-Sequence Pretraining Objectives

Generative pretraining objectives represent a fundamentally different paradigm from the discriminative, contrastive, and matching-based approaches discussed in the previous sections. While contrastive objectives optimize a continuous similarity landscape across matched and mismatched pairs, and masked prediction objectives recover corrupted tokens or regions, generative objectives train models to produce fluent, coherent textual sequences conditioned on visual inputs. This shift from recognition to production naturally equips VLP models with language generation capabilities alongside comprehension skills, making them well-suited for downstream tasks such as image captioning, open-ended visual question answering, and cross-modal text generation. Importantly, generative pretraining also lays the groundwork for the fine-grained and multi-grained alignment objectives explored in the following section, as generating accurate and grounded descriptions requires the model to implicitly learn correspondences between visual regions and linguistic constituents at multiple levels of granularity.

One of the most influential generative pretraining frameworks is prefix language modeling, as exemplified by SimVLM [4]. SimVLM simplifies the pretraining procedure by treating visual tokens as a prefix to the textual sequence and training the model end-to-end with a single autoregressive language modeling objective. This minimalist approach eliminates the need for expensive region-level annotations or multiple dataset-specific objectives, instead exploiting large-scale weak supervision from image-text pairs crawled from the web. The resulting model achieves strong performance across both discriminative and generative vision-language benchmarks, demonstrating that a single prefix language modeling objective can simultaneously support image captioning, VQA, and cross-modal transfer with zero-shot generalization capabilities [4].

Bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) masked prediction objectives offer another approach to joint understanding and generation pretraining. The unified VLP model [6] pioneered this direction by introducing two complementary pretraining tasks: bidirectional masked vision-language prediction, which conditions predictions on full context from both directions for understanding tasks, and seq2seq masked prediction, which restricts the attention mask to allow only left-to-right conditioning during decoding for generation tasks. Crucially, both objectives are realized within the same shared multi-layer transformer network by simply controlling the self-attention mask pattern, avoiding the need for separate encoder and decoder architectures. This design makes unified VLP one of the first models to achieve state-of-the-art performance simultaneously on image captioning and VQA, underscoring the value of unifying generation and understanding under a shared pretraining framework [6].

Encoder-decoder architectures provide a natural structural foundation for generative VLP. In such designs, a visual encoder processes image inputs and produces contextual representations that are consumed by an autoregressive decoder through cross-attention mechanisms. This separation of encoding and generation responsibilities allows the model to develop powerful visual representations independently while maintaining flexible text generation capacity. Variants include models that share parameters between encoding and decoding stages to promote transfer across tasks, as well as those that maintain distinct encoder and decoder weights to specialize each component for its respective role. Notably, this architectural flexibility directly enables the multi-grained alignment strategies discussed in the following section, as encoder representations can be probed or supervised at different levels of granularity to support region-phrase correspondences and structured semantic alignment.

Autoregressive language modeling, in which the model is trained to predict each token given all preceding tokens and the visual context, has also been applied as a generative objective in VLP. This approach, related to GPT-style language modeling extended to the multimodal setting, enables few-shot and zero-shot generalization by framing downstream tasks as text completion problems conditioned on visual inputs. Such formulations allow a single generatively pretrained model to be prompted toward diverse tasks without task-specific fine-tuning heads. While powerful for fluent text generation, purely autoregressive objectives can fall short of explicitly supervising fine-grained cross-modal correspondences—a limitation that motivates the multi-grained alignment objectives explored subsequently.

The broader VLP literature, as surveyed in [2] and [9], highlights how generative objectives complement masked and contrastive objectives, with many modern VLP systems combining them to achieve versatility across both understanding-oriented and generation-oriented downstream tasks. The effectiveness of seq2seq and prefix language modeling objectives lies in their ability to expose the model to the full complexity of natural language generation during pretraining, encouraging it to learn rich compositional representations that support fluent and factually grounded output at inference time. However, as the following section discusses, these broad generative signals benefit greatly from being augmented with explicitly structured, hierarchical, and relational supervision that enforces precise correspondence between visual and linguistic elements at multiple levels of abstraction.

### 3.7 Multi-Grained and Semantic Alignment Objectives

Beyond token-level and global image-text matching, a growing body of work has recognized that effective vision-language pretraining requires alignment at multiple levels of granularity. Standard objectives such as masked language modeling and binary image-text matching operate at coarse levels of correspondence, often failing to capture the rich structural relationships between fine-grained visual regions and specific linguistic constituents. While generative objectives such as prefix language modeling and seq2seq prediction encourage fluent text generation, they too can fall short of explicitly supervising fine-grained cross-modal correspondences. Multi-grained alignment objectives address this limitation by explicitly supervising correspondences at the levels of regions, phrases, sentences, and semantic structures, providing a complementary form of supervision that bridges the gap between holistic scene-level understanding and detailed local grounding.

One important family of objectives focuses on region-to-tag and region-to-phrase alignment, where individual image regions are matched to corresponding words or short phrases rather than entire captions. This approach encourages the model to learn localized cross-modal correspondences, which are particularly beneficial for downstream tasks requiring fine-grained understanding such as visual grounding and referring expression comprehension. By associating visual patches or object proposals with their descriptive textual fragments, models acquire a more compositional understanding of scenes.

Semantic completion learning represents another multi-grained strategy, wherein models are trained on objectives that require completing partial semantic descriptions given visual inputs, or vice versa. Global-to-local and local-to-local semantic completion tasks, as explored in frameworks such as SCL and GLSCL, challenge models to infer missing semantic content at different levels of abstraction simultaneously. These objectives promote richer feature interactions by requiring the model to reconcile high-level scene semantics with detailed local region semantics.

Knowledge distillation-based phrase-region alignment, as exemplified by KD-VLP, leverages the supervision signal from pretrained object detectors or knowledge bases to guide the correspondence between noun phrases in text and visual regions. Rather than relying solely on weak image-caption co-occurrence signals, such approaches explicitly distill structured object knowledge into the pretraining process, yielding more precise and semantically meaningful cross-modal alignments with reduced annotation overhead. This strategy shares a common motivation with auxiliary tag prediction objectives—namely, the desire to enrich coarse caption supervision with more structured and semantically grounded signals—but operates directly at the level of phrase-region correspondence rather than through separate label augmentation pipelines.

Scene graph-based structural alignment provides yet another level of semantic richness by encoding objects, attributes, and their pairwise relationships as structured graphs derived from both image and text modalities. ROSITA exemplifies this direction by incorporating scene graph knowledge into the pretraining process, enabling the model to capture structural semantic correspondences that go well beyond bag-of-words or simple co-occurrence statistics. By aligning visual scene graphs with textual scene graphs, ROSITA encourages models to learn relational and compositional semantics that are important for complex visual reasoning tasks [2].

Together, these multi-grained alignment objectives complement the coarser pretraining signals introduced by generative, contrastive, and masked prediction objectives, as well as the auxiliary tag and label supervision discussed in the following section. By introducing structured, hierarchical, and relational supervision, multi-grained objectives ensure that learned representations encode semantics at multiple levels of abstraction, from individual visual tokens and textual words to complex scene-level structures. As demonstrated by diagnostic benchmarks that probe fine-grained capabilities [55], models trained purely on global objectives often exhibit significant weaknesses in attribute understanding, relational reasoning, and compositional language grounding. Multi-grained objectives offer a principled path toward addressing these limitations, bridging the gap between the fluency and broad coverage encouraged by generative pretraining and the precise visual grounding required by challenging downstream tasks.

### 3.8 Tag and Label Augmentation as Auxiliary Objectives

A recurring limitation in large-scale vision-language pretraining is the coarse and often incomplete nature of textual supervision derived from web-crawled alt-text captions. While the multi-grained alignment objectives discussed in the preceding section introduce structured, hierarchical supervision at the levels of regions, phrases, and scene graphs, they do not directly address the fundamental sparsity and noise of the raw caption signals themselves. Auxiliary label and tag prediction objectives complement these approaches by providing more explicit and semantically grounded supervision signals, enriching the textual side of cross-modal learning rather than solely refining the alignment mechanisms.

One widely adopted strategy involves enriching image-text pairs with object tags extracted using offline object detectors. By predicting these tags as an auxiliary objective, VLP models are encouraged to develop finer-grained visual concept representations and stronger object-level cross-modal alignment. This approach leverages the outputs of pretrained detectors—such as those trained on Visual Genome or OpenImages—as pseudo-labels that augment the original caption supervision. However, a significant drawback of this offline paradigm is the substantial computational overhead introduced by running large frozen detector networks during data preprocessing, as well as the static and potentially stale nature of the extracted tags, which do not adapt as the VLP model evolves during training.

To overcome these limitations, more recent work has proposed performing tag augmentation in an online fashion, tightly integrated within the pretraining loop itself. The IDEA framework exemplifies this direction by jointly performing online multi-label recognition alongside standard VLP objectives. Rather than relying on a separately frozen external detector to generate tags prior to training, IDEA trains a multi-label recognition head end-to-end with the vision-language model. This allows the tag predictions to be dynamically refined as the shared encoder improves, providing progressively more accurate and diverse textual supervision throughout training. The online nature of this approach avoids the redundant computational pipeline associated with frozen detectors while still supplying the richer semantic signals that short alt-text captions alone cannot provide.

More broadly, tag and label augmentation strategies reflect a recognition that the granularity of textual supervision profoundly influences the quality of learned cross-modal representations. Whether through offline detector pipelines or online multi-label recognition objectives, these auxiliary tasks push models to explicitly ground visual content in structured semantic categories, complementing the holistic sentence-level understanding encouraged by standard masked language modeling and image-text matching objectives. The result is a more comprehensive pretraining signal that captures both coarse image-caption correspondences and fine-grained object-concept associations, ultimately benefiting downstream tasks that require precise visual grounding and recognition. This concern for supervision quality—specifically, the challenge of learning reliably from imperfect, web-crawled data—motivates the momentum distillation and self-training strategies explored in the following section, which address noise at an even more fundamental level by dynamically refining the training signal itself rather than augmenting it with external sources.

### 3.9 Momentum Distillation and Self-Training Strategies

Momentum distillation and self-training strategies represent a class of advanced learning objectives in VLP that address a fundamental challenge: how to effectively learn from large-scale web-crawled image-text data that is inherently noisy, misaligned, and unreliable. Rather than treating all training signal as equally valid, these approaches leverage auxiliary models or iterative refinement procedures to generate higher-quality supervision signals, thereby improving the robustness and alignment quality of the learned multimodal representations. This focus on supervision quality connects naturally to the preceding discussion of auxiliary tag and label prediction objectives, which similarly seek to enrich and denoise the raw textual signals derived from web-crawled captions.

A pivotal contribution in this area is ALBEF's momentum distillation framework [76]. ALBEF observes that noisy web data poses a significant obstacle for standard cross-modal pretraining objectives such as image-text contrastive learning and image-text matching, since many image-text pairs on the web are only weakly or partially correlated. To mitigate this, ALBEF introduces a momentum model—a slowly updated exponential moving average (EMA) of the online model's parameters—that continuously generates soft pseudo-targets during training. These pseudo-targets serve as refined supervision signals that smooth over the label noise inherent in web-crawled data. Specifically, for the image-text contrastive loss and masked language modeling loss, the pseudo-targets produced by the momentum model encode richer distributional information than one-hot labels, allowing the online model to learn more nuanced cross-modal correspondences. ALBEF provides a theoretical grounding for this approach from a mutual information maximization perspective, interpreting different pretraining tasks as generating complementary views of an image-text pair [76]. Empirically, ALBEF achieves state-of-the-art performance on image-text retrieval, visual question answering, and visual reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating that momentum distillation is especially effective in low-quality data regimes.

Beyond momentum distillation, self-training strategies that iteratively refine training data have gained traction as a principled means of bootstrapping representation quality. In these frameworks, a pretrained model is used to assess or re-generate annotations for the training corpus, and the refined data is then used to train an improved model in a subsequent stage. This iterative loop between data curation and model training enables progressive noise reduction without requiring additional human annotations. The general principle aligns with the broader self-training literature in NLP and computer vision, where pseudo-label generation from confident model predictions progressively improves performance on noisy or partially labeled datasets.

These momentum distillation and self-training strategies are particularly valuable because they do not require additional annotated data or external knowledge sources, yet substantially improve alignment quality under noisy supervision. By incorporating soft targets from momentum models or iteratively filtering and refining training corpora, VLP frameworks can better exploit the vast but imperfect image-text data available on the web. This makes such strategies an important complement to the tag augmentation techniques discussed previously, as well as to the consistency-based objectives and contrastive distillation methods explored in subsequent sections, which further enforce representational stability and robustness from complementary perspectives. Together, these families of objectives collectively advance the robustness and effectiveness of vision-language pretraining at scale.

### 3.10 Consistency-Based and Contrastive Distillation Objectives

Consistency-based objectives and contrastive distillation strategies represent an important family of pretraining learning signals that enforce representational stability and robustness across diverse and challenging input conditions in vision-language pretraining. Building directly on the momentum distillation and self-training strategies discussed in the previous section—which address noise in web-crawled data through soft pseudo-targets and iterative data refinement—consistency-based approaches introduce complementary structural constraints that encourage models to produce stable representations under varying perturbations, viewpoints, or levels of supervision, thereby improving generalization and reducing sensitivity to noise from a different angle.

A central motivation for consistency-based objectives is the observation that web-crawled image-text pairs often contain semantic misalignment, meaning that a naive contrastive objective may reinforce spurious correlations. By introducing consistency constraints across multiple views or augmented versions of the same input, these approaches encourage the model to learn invariant representations that are not overly dependent on superficial surface-level cues. For instance, image-text contrastive consistency losses enforce that the similarity structure learned during contrastive pretraining remains coherent when evaluated against multiple sampled negatives within a batch, reducing the risk of false-negative penalization and improving the quality of learned cross-modal embeddings. This concern for embedding quality under noisy conditions directly complements the momentum distillation strategy in ALBEF, which similarly addresses false or weak associations in web data, but through soft pseudo-target generation rather than structural consistency constraints.

Cross-viewpoint alignment presents another form of consistency-based learning, particularly relevant for scenarios where the same scene or object can be observed from multiple spatial perspectives. This is especially important for downstream tasks such as 3D scene understanding and navigation, where visual appearance varies significantly across perspectives. Such formulations ensure that the encoder learns to minimize the worst-case discrepancy across different viewpoints, thereby achieving viewpoint-invariant multimodal representations that generalize more robustly beyond the training distribution.

Contrastive distillation methods extend the paradigm of knowledge distillation into the contrastive learning regime, allowing compact student VLP models to inherit the representational quality of larger teacher models without full retraining. This relational consistency is particularly valuable in cross-modal settings, where the geometry of the joint embedding space encodes nuanced semantic correspondences between images and texts that are difficult to recover from scratch with a smaller model. By aligning contrastive similarity matrices rather than point-wise feature vectors, such approaches achieve more faithful compression of large VLP models with minimal performance degradation. Notably, this direction shares a conceptual lineage with momentum distillation, as both leverage an auxiliary model—whether an EMA momentum model or a pretrained teacher—to provide higher-quality supervision than raw data labels alone.

Synchronized attentional masking offers yet another consistency-based perspective by enforcing coherence between the masked regions used in visual and textual branches during pretraining. Rather than masking visual patches and text tokens independently, synchronizing the masking process so that attended regions in the visual stream correspond to semantically relevant tokens in the textual stream encourages the model to develop tighter cross-modal correspondences. The consistency constraint thus acts as an implicit regularizer that promotes fine-grained cross-modal alignment beyond what is achievable with independent masking strategies.

Together, these consistency-based and contrastive distillation objectives reflect a broader trend in VLP research toward objectives that explicitly enforce representational stability across multiple dimensions: across viewpoints, across model scales, across masking configurations, and across in-batch negative sets. Such objectives complement standard contrastive and masked prediction losses—as well as the self-training and momentum distillation methods described previously—by introducing structural constraints on the learned embedding space, helping models become more robust to the diverse conditions encountered in real-world vision-language applications. These strategies naturally set the stage for the training strategy innovations discussed in the following section, where the focus shifts from what is optimized to how the optimization process itself is structured—through scheduled sampling, curriculum learning, and multi-stage training paradigms—to further stabilize convergence and improve cross-modal alignment. Future work in this direction may explore adaptive consistency weighting schemes, curriculum-based consistency scheduling, and the integration of these objectives with generative pretraining frameworks to further enhance the quality and robustness of pretrained multimodal representations.

### 3.11 Scheduled Sampling and Curriculum Learning Strategies

Training strategy innovations play a pivotal role in stabilizing and improving the optimization of vision-language pretraining (VLP) models, complementing the consistency-based objectives and contrastive distillation approaches discussed in the previous section. While those methods introduce structural constraints on the learned embedding space, the training strategies examined here address how the sequence, difficulty, and structure of the optimization process itself can be carefully designed to improve convergence, generalization, and cross-modal alignment. Among these, scheduled sampling, curriculum learning, and multi-stage training paradigms have emerged as particularly effective approaches for bridging the gap between pretraining and fine-tuning, managing the complexity of multimodal data, and progressively building robust cross-modal representations.

**Scheduled Sampling in VLP.** Scheduled sampling was originally proposed to address the exposure bias problem in sequence generation, where models trained with teacher forcing encounter a mismatch between training and inference conditions. In the context of VLP, this strategy has been adapted to handle the discrepancy that arises when mask tokens used during pretraining are absent during downstream fine-tuning. A notable application is found in the Transformer-based Decoupled Encoder-Decoder Network (TDEN) [68], which proposes a two-pass scheduled sampling strategy for simultaneously pretraining a model on both VL understanding and generation tasks. In this framework, a decoupled encoder-decoder structure is employed where two separate cross-modal components handle understanding and generation objectives independently, and scheduled sampling mitigates the train-test discrepancy by pretraining the network in two passes—first producing initial predictions and then refining them under partially corrupted input conditions. This elegant strategy enables the model to learn more robust representations that transfer more effectively to downstream tasks such as visual question answering and image captioning.

More broadly, scheduled sampling in VLP can be understood as a form of curriculum learning where the difficulty of the training signal is gradually modulated. Early training stages rely heavily on ground-truth tokens, while later stages increasingly expose the model to its own (potentially erroneous) predictions, simulating the conditions encountered at inference time. This progressive shift from easy to hard training conditions is a hallmark of curriculum learning strategies, which have been widely adopted across NLP and multimodal learning to improve convergence speed and generalization.

**Multi-Stage and Decoupled Training Paradigms.** Beyond scheduled sampling, multi-stage pretraining strategies have proven effective in managing the competing demands of different modalities and tasks in VLP. A key insight motivating these approaches is that visual and linguistic modalities exhibit distinct statistical properties and learning dynamics, and jointly optimizing them from scratch can lead to suboptimal representations for either modality. Decoupled training paradigms address this by separating the pretraining process into distinct stages, each focused on a specific subset of modalities or objectives.

For instance, approaches that decouple language pretraining from visual learning allow the language component to first acquire strong linguistic priors before being exposed to visual inputs. This philosophy is related to the motivation behind free language modeling [44], where decoupling the prediction rate from the corruption rate in masked language modeling enables faster and more flexible language learning within VLP frameworks, effectively acting as a curriculum over the difficulty of the prediction task. This connection between decoupled training and curriculum principles underscores a unifying theme: structuring the learning process to respect the natural asymmetries between modalities leads to more efficient and effective pretraining.

**Progressive Token and Feature Management.** Another dimension of curriculum-inspired strategies in VLP involves progressively managing the complexity of visual inputs during training. Visual sequences, particularly in video-language pretraining, can contain substantial temporal redundancy, and processing all tokens uniformly throughout training is computationally wasteful. Progressive token merging strategies address this by gradually reducing visual token redundancy as training proceeds—early training stages retain fine-grained visual detail to establish robust cross-modal correspondences, while later stages merge or prune redundant tokens to improve efficiency and encourage the model to focus on the most semantically relevant visual content. This mirrors classical curriculum learning principles where simpler, information-rich examples are prioritized early and complexity is incrementally introduced.

**Curriculum Learning for Multilingual and Cross-Modal Acquisition.** Multi-stage training strategies have also been explored in multilingual VLP settings, where the challenge of simultaneously learning visual, linguistic, and cross-lingual alignments is particularly acute. Lightweight language acquisition frameworks adopt two-stage curricula: an initial stage establishes strong vision-language alignment in a high-resource language, followed by a targeted multilingual acquisition stage that leverages the learned visual grounding as a pivot to extend representations to additional languages with minimal additional parameters. Such staged approaches reduce catastrophic forgetting and enable more sample-efficient transfer across languages.

**Relationship to Broader Optimization Principles.** The success of scheduled sampling and curriculum learning strategies in VLP reflects a broader principle: the order and difficulty of training examples and objectives matter significantly for multimodal optimization. By carefully structuring the training process—whether through scheduled exposure to model predictions, staged separation of modality-specific and cross-modal learning, or progressive reduction of visual redundancy—VLP models can achieve faster convergence, better generalization, and more robust cross-modal representations. These strategies complement the architectural and objective-level innovations discussed throughout this survey, and are increasingly recognized as an essential component of the VLP training recipe.

In summary, scheduled sampling [68], decoupled multi-stage pretraining, and progressive training curricula represent a rich family of learning strategy innovations that address fundamental optimization challenges in VLP. As models scale in size and data, these strategies become even more critical for efficient and effective vision-language pretraining—and naturally set the stage for the discussion of unified and joint objective frameworks that follows, where managing conflicts between multiple training signals becomes a central design concern.

### 3.12 Unified and Joint Objective Frameworks

A central trend in modern vision-language pretraining is the design of unified frameworks that integrate multiple complementary pretraining objectives into a single coherent training pipeline. Building naturally on the training strategy innovations discussed in the previous section—where scheduled sampling, curriculum learning, and multi-stage paradigms were shown to structure the optimization process—this section examines how diverse pretraining objectives themselves can be jointly designed and simultaneously optimized. Rather than relying on any single objective—such as masked language modeling or contrastive learning alone—these frameworks recognize that different objectives capture fundamentally different aspects of cross-modal alignment and representation learning, and that combining them leads to stronger and more generalizable multimodal models.

A prominent design principle underlying such unified frameworks is the recognition that discriminative, generative, and contrastive objectives are complementary rather than redundant. Contrastive objectives align global image-text representations across a shared embedding space, matching objectives train models to distinguish semantically aligned pairs from mismatched ones, and generative objectives encourage models to produce fluent and contextually grounded text conditioned on visual inputs. When combined, these objectives collectively encourage both coarse-grained semantic alignment and fine-grained compositional understanding.

The [77] model exemplifies this integration philosophy by employing a single transformer network that is shared across image encoding, text encoding, and multimodal fusion tasks. UFO jointly optimizes image-text contrastive loss, image-text matching loss, and masked language modeling loss within a unified architecture by leveraging both bidirectional and seq2seq attention masks. This design avoids the need for separate unimodal encoders and fusion networks, demonstrating that multi-task joint training with diverse objectives can reduce task conflicts and achieve strong performance across visual question answering, image captioning, and image-text retrieval benchmarks.

Similarly, the [13] framework, BEiT-3, achieves unification at the level of the pretraining task itself by treating all modalities—images, texts, and image-text pairs—as variants of a masked "language" modeling problem. By performing masked signal reconstruction uniformly across monomodal and multimodal data within a Multiway Transformer backbone, BEiT-3 effectively unifies what were previously separate pretraining stages into a single scalable objective. This approach demonstrates that architectural unification and objective unification are mutually reinforcing: the modular Multiway Transformer enables both deep multimodal fusion and modality-specific encoding, while the unified masked prediction task ensures that shared representations benefit from diverse data sources simultaneously.

Another dimension of joint objective design concerns the integration of localization and language understanding within a single framework. Models such as those that integrate region-level grounding with sentence-level matching demonstrate that objectives operating at different semantic granularities—from fine-grained region-word correspondences to global image-caption alignment—can be jointly optimized to produce representations that are simultaneously sensitive to local visual concepts and global semantic coherence. This multi-granular alignment strategy addresses a key limitation of purely contrastive approaches, which tend to capture holistic similarities while missing fine-grained spatial and attributional correspondences.

The [67] framework further advances joint objective design by introducing a stagewise pretraining strategy that progressively incorporates image-only, text-only, and image-text pair data. By combining Mixture-of-Modality-Experts (MoME) with a joint optimization of image-text contrastive, image-text matching, and masked language modeling objectives, VLMo enables the same model to serve as either a dual encoder for efficient retrieval or a fusion encoder for understanding tasks, depending on the downstream requirement. This flexibility is a direct consequence of the joint training regime, which ensures that both unimodal and multimodal representations are well-calibrated throughout pretraining. Notably, VLMo's stagewise strategy echoes the multi-stage and decoupled training paradigms discussed in the previous section, illustrating how training schedule design and objective unification are deeply intertwined concerns in VLP.

A critical challenge in unified objective frameworks is managing potential conflicts between competing training signals. Contrastive objectives, for example, operate on global representations and encourage separation between negatives, while masked prediction objectives require the model to attend to local token-level details. Balancing the gradients and loss scales of these objectives requires careful hyperparameter tuning, and naive combinations can lead to suboptimal convergence or objective dominance. Successful frameworks address this through loss weighting, stagewise training, modality-specific routing mechanisms, or architectural decoupling that allows different objective heads to interact with complementary representations.

Taken together, the trend toward unified and joint objective frameworks reflects a broader maturation of the VLP field, moving away from single-purpose pretraining recipes toward holistic training paradigms that prepare models for diverse downstream tasks from the outset. The most effective designs share common principles: they leverage architectural flexibility to support multiple inference modes, combine objectives that operate at complementary semantic granularities, and employ training strategies that mitigate inter-objective interference while maximizing the synergistic benefits of diverse supervision signals. In this way, unified objective frameworks represent the culmination of the many architectural, objective-level, and training strategy innovations surveyed throughout this work, integrating them into cohesive systems capable of achieving broad and robust vision-language understanding.

## 4 Data, Supervision, and Scalability

### 4.1 Overview of Pretraining Data Paradigms

The quality, scale, and nature of pretraining data are among the most critical determinants of downstream performance in vision-language pretraining (VLP). Broadly, VLP data paradigms can be organized into three major categories: **fully supervised**, **weakly supervised**, and **unsupervised** pretraining, each representing a distinct trade-off between annotation cost, data scale, and achievable downstream performance.

**Fully supervised paradigms** rely on meticulously curated image-text datasets with high-quality, manually verified alignments, such as MSCOCO Captions and Flickr30K. While these datasets offer precise semantic correspondences between visual and textual content, their construction is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming, severely limiting their scale. As a consequence, models trained exclusively on fully supervised data often struggle to generalize broadly across diverse real-world visual concepts.

**Weakly supervised paradigms** occupy the middle ground and represent the dominant strategy in modern VLP research. These approaches leverage large-scale image-text pairs harvested from the web, such as Conceptual Captions or the billion-scale alt-text corpora used in ALIGN [1]. While these datasets can contain hundreds of millions or even billions of pairs, they are inherently noisy—textual descriptions are often incomplete, inaccurate, or only loosely associated with their paired images [2]. To cope with this noise, researchers have proposed strategies such as bootstrapped caption filtering [5], online multi-label recognition to enrich textual supervision [47], and retrieval-based knowledge augmentation [48]. Notably, SimVLM [4] demonstrated that with an appropriate pretraining objective—specifically prefix language modeling—even weak supervision from noisy large-scale data can yield impressive results across both discriminative and generative tasks, reducing reliance on expensive annotations.

**Unsupervised paradigms** represent an emerging frontier in which VLP models are trained without any parallel image-text corpora. Instead, these methods exploit unpaired or unimodal data, employing cross-modal bridges such as object tags or prompting large language models (LLMs) to synthesize descriptive pseudo-captions. For instance, PiTL [78] uses category labels from ImageNet and leverages LLMs to generate knowledge-rich image descriptions, thereby entirely circumventing the need for paired image-text supervision.

Table 1 provides a conceptual taxonomy of representative VLP methods along these three paradigms:

| Paradigm | Data Scale | Annotation Cost | Representative Works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fully Supervised | Small–Medium | High | MSCOCO-based VLP, Flickr30K-based models |
| Weakly Supervised | Large–Billion-scale | Low–Medium | ALIGN, BLIP, SimVLM, IDEA, REAVL |
| Unsupervised | Variable | Very Low | PiTL, VLMixer-based methods |

The overall trend in the field has been a decisive shift toward weakly supervised pretraining at scale, driven by the insight that data volume and diversity, even at the cost of some label noise, often outweigh the benefits of small but clean datasets [2]. Concurrently, data quality improvement strategies have become indispensable companions to data scaling, as researchers seek to extract maximum signal from noisy real-world corpora. As will be explored in the following subsections, web-crawled datasets such as Conceptual Captions, LAION, FILIP300M, and ALT200M have emerged as the dominant vehicle for this weakly supervised paradigm, offering the breadth of visual and linguistic diversity necessary for training generalizable multimodal representations—while simultaneously introducing the noise, misalignment, and distributional challenges that motivate the filtering and augmentation strategies discussed thereafter.

### 4.2 Large-Scale Web-Crawled Noisy Image-Text Pairs

Large-scale web-crawled image-text datasets have become the dominant source of pretraining data for vision-language models, owing to their massive scale and relative ease of collection compared to manually annotated corpora. As introduced in the preceding subsection, the field has witnessed a decisive shift toward weakly supervised pretraining at scale, and it is precisely datasets such as Conceptual Captions, LAION, FILIP300M, and ALT200M that have driven this transition—aggregating hundreds of millions to billions of image-text pairs harvested from the internet to provide the breadth of visual and linguistic diversity necessary to train generalizable multimodal representations [2; 10].

The scale advantages of these datasets are considerable. By training on web-scale data, VLP models gain exposure to an extraordinarily wide range of visual concepts, linguistic styles, and cross-modal associations that would be prohibitively expensive to curate through human annotation. This abundance of data has been instrumental in enabling large-scale contrastive pretraining paradigms and generative pretraining approaches, contributing significantly to the strong zero-shot and few-shot transfer performance observed in modern VLP systems [11; 15].

However, the same properties that make web-crawled datasets attractive at scale also introduce substantial noise that poses fundamental challenges for learning robust cross-modal representations. A significant portion of collected image-text pairs exhibit loose or incorrect correspondences, where the accompanying alt-text or surrounding webpage text may describe only a tangentially related concept, reference unrelated content, or consist of non-descriptive metadata such as file names and generic labels [2]. Textual descriptions are frequently incomplete, failing to capture the full semantic content of the associated image, or overly generic in ways that provide insufficient grounding signal for fine-grained representation learning.

Beyond misalignment at the pair level, web-crawled datasets exhibit pronounced long-tailed concept distributions, where common objects, scenes, and attributes are vastly over-represented relative to rare or specialized concepts. This imbalance complicates the learning of robust representations for tail categories and can lead to models that perform well on frequent concepts while failing on less common but practically important visual and linguistic phenomena [10]. Furthermore, demographic and cultural biases embedded in the crawled data can propagate into pretrained model representations, raising concerns for downstream applications in sensitive domains.

The combination of scale-induced noise, semantic misalignment, and distributional skew means that simply increasing the volume of web-crawled data does not straightforwardly translate into proportional gains in downstream performance. These inherent data quality challenges—spanning noisy pair-level correspondences, long-tailed distributions, and embedded biases—form the central motivation for the noise handling and filtering strategies examined in the following subsection, as the community seeks to harness the scale advantages of web-crawled data while mitigating the detrimental effects of its noise characteristics [11; 14].

### 4.3 Noise Handling and Data Filtering Strategies

The quality of web-crawled image-text pairs is a central concern in vision-language pretraining, as the noise, misalignment, and incompleteness characteristic of large-scale crawled corpora can substantially degrade the quality of learned cross-modal representations. As established in the preceding discussion, web-crawled datasets such as Conceptual Captions and LAION offer invaluable scale but introduce fundamental challenges including semantic misalignment, long-tailed concept distributions, and distributional biases. Addressing these challenges, a rich body of research has emerged around strategies to detect, filter, and correct noisy pretraining data at various stages of the training pipeline, before the community turned to the semantic enrichment and augmentation approaches discussed in the following subsection.

One of the most influential approaches to noise handling is bootstrapped caption filtering, as employed in BLIP. This strategy introduces a captioner-filter loop in which a generative model produces synthetic captions for web images, and a filter module then removes image-text pairs whose original alt-text captions are deemed inconsistent with the visual content. By iteratively refining the training corpus using the model's own predictions, BLIP effectively reduces the impact of misaligned or semantically irrelevant noisy pairs without requiring manual annotation.

CLIPScore-based filtering represents another widely adopted strategy, leveraging the strong cross-modal alignment capabilities of contrastive models such as CLIP to assign relevance scores to image-text pairs and prune those below a threshold. While computationally straightforward, this approach inherits potential biases of the CLIP model itself and may disproportionately discard rare or long-tailed concepts that CLIP has not been adequately exposed to during its own training—a limitation that connects directly to the long-tailed distribution problem inherent in web-crawled data.

Beyond simple threshold-based pruning, Sieve-based approaches substitute synthetic captions generated by large vision-language models for the noisy alt-text descriptions, and then filter data by comparing the original and synthetic texts. This synthetic caption-guided pruning strategy yields cleaner training subsets that better reflect the actual visual content of images, with empirical evidence suggesting significant improvements in downstream fine-grained alignment tasks relative to standard CLIPScore filtering.

Semantic dataset deduplication represents a complementary direction that targets redundancy rather than misalignment. Methods such as SemDeDup identify and remove semantically near-duplicate examples in the embedding space, thereby reducing the effective corpus size while maintaining or improving downstream performance. Crucially, deduplication also has fairness implications: FairDeDup extends SemDeDup to analyze how deduplication disproportionately affects underrepresented demographic groups in training data, demonstrating that naive deduplication can inadvertently amplify biases by preferentially removing examples of minorities whose visual representations cluster less densely in the embedding space. This fairness concern echoes the demographic and cultural bias issues noted as inherent properties of web-crawled corpora.

False negative handling is another important dimension of noise mitigation in contrastive pretraining. In large-batch contrastive learning, in-batch negatives may inadvertently include semantically compatible image-text pairs that are incorrectly treated as negatives, degrading alignment learning. Methods such as COSMO address this by identifying probable false negatives through semantic similarity estimation and converting them into positives or applying label smoothing to reduce their harmful gradient contributions, thereby improving the robustness of contrastive objectives under noisy supervision.

Noise-harmonization and noise-completion strategies, as explored in frameworks like NLIP, take a more constructive perspective by not simply discarding noisy samples but instead attempting to repair them. Noise harmonization aligns inconsistent textual descriptions with visual content through controlled rewriting, while noise completion enriches sparse or incomplete captions by augmenting them with semantically relevant information derived from auxiliary models. Together, these complementary repair mechanisms allow VLP models to leverage a broader fraction of the available web data rather than discarding large portions through aggressive filtering. This rehabilitative philosophy directly motivates the subsequent augmentation strategies discussed in the following subsection, which further enrich training pairs with object tags, structural knowledge, and retrieved external information.

Overall, the choice of noise handling strategy involves significant computational trade-offs. Bootstrapped filtering and Sieve-based pruning require additional inference passes with generative models, increasing pretraining cost. CLIPScore-based filtering is cheaper but less precise. Semantic deduplication can be performed offline before training but requires large-scale embedding computations. False negative conversion methods add overhead during training but improve contrastive objective quality. As pretraining datasets grow to billions of samples, the development of scalable, bias-aware, and computationally efficient noise handling pipelines remains an active and critical research frontier in vision-language pretraining, forming a necessary foundation upon which richer semantic augmentation strategies can be effectively applied.

### 4.4 Label, Tag, and Knowledge Augmentation

A prominent strategy for enriching noisy or sparse image-text pairs during vision-language pretraining is the augmentation of training data with additional semantic signals, ranging from object tags and scene graphs to externally retrieved knowledge. As established in the preceding discussion of noise handling and filtering strategies, web-crawled image-text pairs frequently suffer from incompleteness and semantic sparsity that cannot be fully resolved by filtering alone; augmentation addresses the complementary challenge of enriching the surviving training pairs with richer, more structured supervision. These approaches aim to bridge the gap between coarse web-crawled image captions and the fine-grained semantic understanding required for downstream tasks, and they collectively form a natural precursor to the weakly supervised paradigms explored in the following section, where such augmented signals transition from supplementary enrichment to primary supervisory sources.

**Object Tag Extraction via Offline Detectors.** One of the earliest and most widely adopted augmentation strategies involves extracting object tags from images using pretrained offline object detectors and incorporating them as anchor points in the pretraining process. This approach, exemplified by [71], leverages the observation that salient objects in an image are frequently mentioned in the paired text, making detected object tags reliable cross-modal anchors that significantly ease the learning of visual-linguistic alignments. By using object semantics as alignment pivots rather than relying solely on raw region features, such methods provide more structured and explicit supervision signals that guide the model toward meaningful multimodal correspondences.

**Scene Graph and Structural Knowledge Integration.** Beyond flat object tags, richer structural knowledge can be incorporated through scene graphs that encode relational information between objects. This type of structural knowledge augmentation introduces entity-relationship triples that capture spatial, attributive, and semantic relationships within an image, providing supervision beyond the object level. Such scene graph-based integration supplements sparse alt-text descriptions with structured relational annotations, enabling VLP models to learn more compositional and relation-aware multimodal representations.

**Object Knowledge Distillation for Phrase-Region Alignment.** A complementary approach is to distill object knowledge from pretrained external detectors directly into the pretraining framework through novel pretext tasks. [26] proposes an object-aware end-to-end VLP framework that leverages object features and semantic labels from external detectors as supervision for two specialized pretraining tasks: an object-guided masked vision modeling task that enforces object-aware representation learning, and a phrase-region alignment task that improves cross-modal alignment by exploiting similarities between noun phrases and object labels in the linguistic space. This approach effectively transfers the semantic knowledge encoded in large pretrained detectors into the end-to-end VLP model without requiring bounding box annotations at inference time.

**Position-Guided and Fine-Grained Text Prompts.** Another augmentation direction introduces position-aware or layout-guided text prompts to provide spatial grounding cues that standard captions lack. By incorporating explicit positional signals—such as bounding box coordinates or relative spatial descriptors—into the textual input, VLP models receive additional supervision that ties linguistic descriptions to specific image regions, fostering fine-grained alignment between local visual features and corresponding textual phrases.

**Multi-Level Semantic Tag Augmentation.** The SOHO framework [79] addresses the limitation of region-based pipelines by learning a visual dictionary that captures consistent visual abstractions, which serves as a form of implicit semantic augmentation by grounding image patches in a compact codebook. More explicitly, multi-label tag augmentation strategies employ online or offline recognition models to assign diverse semantic tags to images, providing supervision that complements sparse textual descriptions. The [29] framework introduces high-level semantic concepts as additional inputs alongside visual tokens and textual tokens, following the nested structure of both modalities to facilitate multi-level semantic alignment. By explicitly incorporating concepts as a bridge between region-level features and sentence-level semantics, this framework enables coarse-grained and fine-grained cross-modal alignment to reinforce each other during pretraining.

**Retrieval-Based Knowledge Augmentation.** Retrieval-augmented approaches enrich individual training samples by retrieving semantically related external knowledge—such as captions from related images, relevant Wikipedia passages, or knowledge graph entries—and appending this retrieved context to the pretraining input. This strategy is particularly beneficial when the original image-text pairing provides only a partial description of the visual content, as the retrieved information fills conceptual gaps and introduces world knowledge that models would otherwise need to infer implicitly. By grounding training samples in broader factual contexts, retrieval-based augmentation promotes the development of knowledge-aware multimodal representations that generalize better to knowledge-intensive downstream tasks such as open-domain visual question answering. This retrieval-based enrichment also anticipates a key mechanism exploited by the weakly supervised paradigms discussed in the following section, where retrieval is elevated to a primary strategy for constructing cross-modal supervision from non-parallel data.

**Impact and Design Considerations.** Across these diverse augmentation strategies, a shared motivation is the insufficiency of raw alt-text as a supervisory signal: web-crawled captions tend to be short, incomplete, and focused on only a subset of the visual content. By enriching training pairs with object tags, relational structures, positional cues, external knowledge, or multi-label annotations, these approaches provide VLP models with more diverse, explicit, and fine-grained supervision that guides learning toward richer visual-semantic correspondences. The rehabilitative philosophy introduced by noise-completion methods such as NLIP—wherein damaged or incomplete training pairs are repaired rather than discarded—is here extended into a proactive enrichment paradigm that goes beyond simply correcting flawed data to actively constructing richer supervisory signals. The trade-off, however, lies in the computational overhead introduced by offline detectors or retrieval systems, the potential domain mismatch between augmented tags and downstream task vocabularies, and the risk of introducing additional noise when augmentation quality is inconsistent. Hybrid approaches that combine lightweight online augmentation with selective use of offline knowledge sources represent a promising balance between annotation richness and computational tractability, and they form a natural bridge to the weakly supervised strategies explored in the following section, where the boundary between augmentation and primary supervision progressively dissolves.

### 4.5 Weakly Supervised Pretraining Paradigms

Weakly supervised vision-language pretraining (VLP) paradigms occupy an important middle ground between the fully supervised approaches discussed in the preceding section—which rely on rich semantic augmentations such as object tags, scene graphs, and retrieved knowledge—and the fully unsupervised methods explored in the following section, which operate entirely without paired image-text data. These weakly supervised approaches aim to reduce reliance on fully aligned image-text pairs by exploiting alternative or noisier forms of supervision that are easier to obtain at scale, motivated by the practical observation that high-quality, manually curated image-caption datasets are expensive to collect, whereas weakly aligned or partially supervised data is far more abundant on the web.

One prominent strategy employs object tags and class labels as cross-modal anchors to bridge visual and textual modalities without requiring full sentence-level captions. By detecting salient objects in images and using their semantic labels as weak textual supervision, these methods establish coarse but useful correspondences between visual regions and linguistic concepts. Such tag-based supervision is particularly attractive because object detectors can be applied offline at scale, producing approximate textual descriptions that guide cross-modal alignment even in the absence of human-written captions [26]. This strategy naturally extends the object-level semantic augmentation paradigm introduced in the previous section, but shifts the role of object detectors from supplementary enrichment to primary supervisory signal.

Another important weakly supervised paradigm leverages graph optimal transport to perform local feature alignment between image patches and text tokens without requiring explicit bounding box annotations. [80] exemplifies this approach, adopting a weakly supervised alignment strategy that uses only image-caption pairs—without box-level supervision—yet achieves fine-grained region-level understanding. VoLTA constructs an explicit, self-normalized local matching criterion via graph optimal transport, enabling the model to germinate region-to-token correspondences from coarse image-text supervision. Crucially, VoLTA also integrates multi-modal fusion more deeply into unimodal backbones during pretraining, reducing memory overhead from separate fusion-specific transformer layers. Extensive experiments demonstrate that this weakly supervised approach achieves competitive or superior performance on fine-grained downstream tasks compared to methods that use significantly more annotated box data, illustrating the power of optimal transport-based weak supervision.

Unsupervised and weakly supervised retrieval-based alignment curricula represent another direction, where non-parallel image and text corpora are leveraged by first constructing weakly aligned pairs through retrieval, and then applying multi-granular alignment tasks [81]. This approach bridges the gap toward the fully unsupervised methods discussed in the following section, demonstrating that effective cross-modal representations can be learned even without parallel image-text data, provided that the alignment curriculum spans multiple granularities ranging from region-to-tag and region-to-phrase correspondences up to image-to-sentence matching.

The use of large language models (LLMs) to generate descriptive pseudo-captions offers a compelling recent direction in weakly supervised VLP. Rather than relying on short and often uninformative alt-text scraped from the web, these methods prompt LLMs to produce richer, more descriptive textual descriptions of images, substantially improving the quality of weak supervision. Such LLM-generated captions can serve as drop-in replacements for human-authored captions during pretraining, providing semantically denser supervision that helps models learn more nuanced visual-textual correspondences. This direction complements retrieval-based knowledge augmentation strategies by generating rather than retrieving supplementary textual context.

Similarly, end-to-end VLP methods that replace expensive region-based features with simpler grid or patch-level representations can be viewed as operating under a weaker form of supervision, since they forgo the structured object-level annotations that region-based detectors provide [36] [21]. These approaches show that strong downstream performance is achievable without the heavy object detection supervision traditionally considered necessary for fine-grained VLP, further validating the efficacy of weakly supervised pretraining strategies and paving the way toward the fully annotation-free paradigms explored in the following section.

Overall, weakly supervised VLP paradigms consistently demonstrate that alternative supervision signals—whether derived from object tags, optimal transport-based local alignment, retrieval-augmented curricula, or LLM-generated pseudo-captions—can substantially close the performance gap with fully supervised methods while dramatically reducing annotation costs. These strategies collectively reinforce the broader insight, shared with both the data augmentation approaches in the preceding section and the unsupervised methods in the following section, that the rigid requirement for densely annotated multimodal data can be progressively relaxed through careful design of learning objectives that exploit latent structural correspondences between visual and linguistic modalities. The key challenge remains ensuring that weak supervisory signals provide sufficient semantic grounding for complex downstream tasks such as visual grounding, VQA, and image-text retrieval.

### 4.6 Unsupervised and Unpaired Pretraining Approaches

A fundamental assumption underlying most vision-language pretraining (VLP) frameworks is the availability of paired image-text corpora. However, as established in the preceding discussion of weakly supervised approaches, the collection and curation of such aligned multimodal data is inherently expensive and labor-intensive. Fully unsupervised and unpaired VLP approaches represent the natural endpoint of this relaxation, completely abandoning the requirement for paired image-text supervision and instead exploiting latent structural correspondences between modalities from entirely disconnected unimodal streams.

One prominent direction involves mask-and-predict pretraining strategies applied to unimodal data sources, where object tags or semantic labels extracted from images serve as lightweight bridges connecting visual and textual modalities without requiring full image-caption alignment. Such approaches leverage the structural properties of visual scenes to construct pseudo-supervision signals, enabling models to learn cross-modal correspondences from otherwise disconnected unimodal streams. Works such as [78] have demonstrated that even category-level labels, when combined with knowledge derived from large language models, can serve as effective substitutes for paired captions, yielding competitive performance on cross-modal retrieval benchmarks despite operating under severely reduced supervision.

Retrieval-based multi-granular alignment curricula represent another compelling strategy for unsupervised VLP. Rather than relying on fixed image-text correspondences, these methods dynamically retrieve semantically related unimodal samples during training and construct alignment objectives at multiple levels of granularity—from coarse scene-level matching to fine-grained object and attribute alignment. By progressively increasing the difficulty of alignment tasks across training, these curricula enable models to develop robust cross-modal representations even when ground-truth pairings are unavailable, extending the multi-granular alignment principles observed in weakly supervised retrieval-based methods into the fully unsupervised regime.

Cross-modal data augmentation strategies such as CutMix-inspired mixing of visual and textual features offer an orthogonal approach to unsupervised pretraining. By blending visual patches or regions with text tokens from semantically unrelated samples and training the model to disentangle and realign the mixed inputs, these augmentation schemes provide implicit contrastive supervision without requiring explicit image-text pairs.

Visual hallucination-based feature generation presents yet another paradigm wherein synthetic or imagined visual features are generated to accompany unpaired textual inputs during pretraining. This strategy effectively expands the space of multimodal training signals by leveraging the generative capacity of pretrained visual encoders or generative models to produce plausible visual counterparts for text-only data, thereby enabling cross-modal learning in the absence of real paired observations.

Collectively, these unsupervised and unpaired VLP methods demonstrate that the rigid requirement for large-scale aligned image-caption corpora can be substantially relaxed beyond what weakly supervised approaches achieve—completing the progression from fully supervised, to weakly supervised, to entirely annotation-free pretraining. They open pathways toward more scalable and accessible VLP, particularly in domains where paired multimodal data is scarce, such as specialized scientific or industrial applications. This aligns with broader efforts toward weakly supervised and data-efficient pretraining frameworks [4], reinforcing the view that rich cross-modal representations can emerge from weaker and more readily available forms of supervision, provided that the learning objectives are carefully designed to exploit latent structural correspondences between modalities. The question that naturally follows, and which the succeeding section addresses, is how the quality of textual supervision itself—rather than merely its pairing with visual data—can be further improved to unlock finer-grained cross-modal understanding.

### 4.7 Long and Rich Caption Generation for Pretraining

A significant limitation of conventional vision-language pretraining lies in the quality of textual supervision. Large-scale web-crawled datasets typically pair images with short, noisy alt-text descriptions that are often incomplete, ambiguous, or factually misaligned with the corresponding visual content. These sparse captions fail to capture the rich semantic details present in images, leading to coarse-grained cross-modal representations that struggle to generalize to fine-grained downstream tasks. This limitation is distinct from the challenges of unpaired or unsupervised pretraining discussed previously—here, paired data exists, but the quality of the textual signal itself constitutes the bottleneck.

To address this shortcoming, an emerging research direction focuses on generating long, detailed, and semantically rich captions as pretraining supervision. Rather than relying solely on original alt-text, these approaches leverage the generative capabilities of multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to produce comprehensive descriptions that articulate fine-grained attributes, object relationships, spatial arrangements, and contextual scene details. Such re-captioning strategies can substantially enrich the textual side of image-text pairs, providing pretraining models with denser and more informative supervision signals—effectively complementing the noise-filtering and tag-augmentation strategies that form part of the broader data quality toolkit in VLP research.

One prominent example of this direction is DreamLIP, which employs multimodal large language models to re-caption images with lengthy, descriptive text. A key design insight in DreamLIP is the use of sub-caption sampling strategies, which decompose lengthy descriptions into multiple shorter sub-captions. These sub-captions are then used to construct multiple positive image-text pairs during pretraining, encouraging the model to learn diverse and complementary aspects of the visual content from a single image. This multi-positive training scheme effectively increases the granularity of supervision without requiring additional annotated data.

The benefits of richer textual supervision are multifaceted. First, detailed captions explicitly describe visual attributes, object identities, and inter-object relationships that are often absent from short alt-text, enabling more fine-grained alignment between visual regions and linguistic concepts. Second, longer descriptions reduce the semantic ambiguity inherent in brief captions, helping models learn more precise correspondences. Third, by covering diverse visual aspects in a single training example, rich captions improve the model's ability to generalize to tasks requiring nuanced visual understanding, such as visual question answering, compositional reasoning, and attribute-level retrieval. These advantages are particularly consequential at scale, where the compounding effect of richer supervision across billions of training examples can yield substantial improvements over models trained on equivalently large but textually impoverished corpora.

This paradigm aligns with the broader trend observed across VLP research, where the quality and richness of textual supervision—rather than sheer data scale alone—plays a decisive role in determining downstream performance [2]. As multimodal foundation models become increasingly capable of generating high-fidelity image descriptions [14], re-captioning pipelines offer a scalable and cost-effective means of upgrading existing noisy datasets into richer pretraining corpora. This strategy directly informs the scalability considerations examined in the following section, where the interplay between data volume, annotation quality, and training efficiency emerges as a central theme in pushing the frontiers of large-scale vision-language pretraining.

### 4.8 Scalability of Pretraining Data and Models

## 4.8 Scalability and Large-Scale Training Strategies

Scalability stands as one of the most fundamental and consequential dimensions of vision-language pretraining research. Building directly on the data quality considerations raised in the preceding discussion—where richer, re-captioned supervision was shown to unlock finer-grained cross-modal alignment—the present section examines how scale itself interacts with data quality, model architecture, and training efficiency to determine downstream performance. The empirical relationship between pretraining data scale, model size, and downstream task performance has been a central focus of the VLP community, motivating ever-larger training runs and increasingly sophisticated strategies for harvesting and organizing web-scale multimodal corpora.

**Scaling Laws in VLP Contexts**

The vision-language pretraining paradigm has broadly inherited scaling intuitions from large language models, where performance tends to improve predictably as a function of data volume, model parameter count, and compute budget. In VLP settings, scaling manifests somewhat differently due to the added complexity of cross-modal alignment. Increasing data scale generally improves downstream performance on tasks such as visual question answering, image-text retrieval, and image captioning, but the nature of the data—its alignment quality, conceptual diversity, and label richness—matters enormously alongside raw volume. Models trained on hundreds of millions to billions of image-text pairs have consistently demonstrated stronger zero-shot generalization compared to those trained on smaller curated datasets, suggesting that breadth of coverage compensates partially for imperfect alignment. Crucially, this observation reinforces the insight from the previous section: when textual supervision is enriched through re-captioning, scaling the resulting corpus yields compounding benefits that go well beyond what equivalent volumes of noisy alt-text can deliver.

Unified transformer architectures designed for scalability have played a critical role in enabling this trajectory. Approaches such as [67] demonstrate how stagewise pretraining strategies—first leveraging large-scale image-only and text-only corpora before incorporating image-text pairs—allow models to benefit from the vastly larger unimodal data pools that exist on the web. This strategy effectively decouples the data requirements for unimodal and cross-modal learning, enabling scale without proportionally increasing the need for expensive aligned pairs. Similarly, [13] shows that treating image and text tokens under a unified masked modeling framework across monomodal and multimodal data at massive scale leads to strong generalization across both vision and vision-language benchmarks, illustrating that architectural unification can be a scalability enabler.

**Benefits and Diminishing Returns of Scaling Web Data**

While scaling web-crawled data generally yields performance gains, the benefits are not uniformly distributed across tasks or model sizes, and diminishing returns become apparent at extreme scales. Contrastive models such as those following the CLIP paradigm benefit enormously from scale due to the implicit denoising effect of large in-batch negative sampling, but gains on fine-grained tasks such as compositional reasoning and spatial understanding plateau or stagnate even as data volume grows. This is partly because web-crawled alt-text descriptions are short, incomplete, and often fail to capture the fine-grained visual semantics needed for compositional understanding tasks—precisely the quality bottleneck that re-captioning strategies aim to address. The quality and diversity of the text associated with images—not merely the count of pairs—determines how effectively scaling translates to downstream improvement.

Efficient end-to-end architectures such as [82] address scalability from a computational efficiency angle, demonstrating that a unified pretraining task—masked signal modeling across image pixels and text tokens—can accelerate training by approximately 3.5× compared to models that combine image-text contrastive and matching losses, while maintaining competitive downstream performance. This efficiency gain is critical at scale, where training costs otherwise become prohibitive.

**Weakly Supervised Annotation Derivation from Heterogeneous Text Signals**

A key strategy for enabling large-scale training without proportional annotation costs is the derivation of weakly supervised labels from heterogeneous textual signals co-occurring with images on the web. Rather than relying solely on curated captions or expensive human annotations, models can leverage alt-text, surrounding document text, metadata, hashtags, and other naturally occurring textual signals as approximate supervision. This paradigm allows the training corpus to scale to billions of examples while keeping annotation costs near zero—representing a complementary pole to the re-captioning approach, which invests in per-example quality rather than aggregate volume.

Models like [64] demonstrate end-to-end pretraining on large-scale image-text pairs collected from diverse web sources, achieving strong performance across understanding and generation tasks. The diversity of textual signals—even when individually noisy—provides a rich supervision landscape that encourages the model to learn broad visual-semantic associations. However, the heterogeneity of these signals also introduces inconsistency, requiring careful architectural design or training strategies to prevent the model from overfitting to superficial correlations.

**Clustering Techniques for Long-Tailed Label Distributions**

Web-crawled image-text corpora exhibit pronounced long-tailed distributions, where a small fraction of concepts, objects, and scenes are overwhelmingly frequent while the vast majority appear rarely. This imbalance poses significant challenges for learning robust representations across the full conceptual spectrum, as gradient updates are dominated by frequent concepts and rare but important visual categories receive insufficient training signal.

Clustering-based approaches offer a principled mechanism for addressing long-tail challenges at scale. By grouping semantically similar examples together and ensuring balanced sampling during training, clustering techniques mitigate the overrepresentation of common concepts and improve coverage of rare categories. This connects to tag augmentation strategies—such as those using online multi-label recognition to derive richer semantic supervision—which help make the training signal more informative for diverse and less common visual concepts. Unified architectures that handle diverse concept vocabularies, as seen in the broad multi-task pretraining of models like [83], benefit from such balancing strategies by ensuring that the model is exposed to sufficient examples of each concept type during optimization.

**Efficient Large-Scale Training Infrastructure**

Beyond algorithmic strategies, efficient large-scale VLP training requires careful engineering of data pipelines, training objectives, and model architectures. Encoder designs that can process visual and textual inputs with reduced computational overhead—such as the cross-modal skip-connections in [64] which create inter-layer shortcuts that skip costly full self-attention on the vision side—directly translate to faster training throughput at scale. Modality-aware sparse mixture-of-experts designs as in [82] allow model capacity to scale without proportional increases in per-sample computation, since only a subset of expert parameters are activated for any given input.

The scalable deployment of large VLP models also benefits from parameter-sharing strategies and unified architectures. Models like [67] that share a substantial fraction of parameters across modalities while maintaining modality-specific expert modules achieve strong performance with fewer total parameters than separate unimodal encoder ensembles, reducing both storage and inference costs at deployment scale.

**Synthesis**

The scalability of VLP models depends on a multifaceted interplay between data volume, data quality, model architecture, and training efficiency. Simply scaling web data yields diminishing returns without corresponding improvements in annotation quality and conceptual diversity—a tension that directly motivates the data efficiency strategies examined in the following section. The most effective large-scale VLP systems combine architectural innovations that enable efficient cross-modal learning—such as unified transformers with modality-specific components, sparse mixture-of-experts modules, and end-to-end visual encoders—with data strategies that maximize the richness and balance of supervision signals derived from heterogeneous web sources. As model and dataset scales continue to grow, the field increasingly recognizes that smart scaling—informed by scaling laws, data quality metrics, and efficient training paradigms—is more productive than naive data accumulation alone. This insight sets the stage for the following section, which examines how the same performance goals can be pursued under the stricter resource constraints that characterize most real-world deployment scenarios.

### 4.9 Data Efficiency and Annotation Cost Reduction

## 4.9 Data Efficiency and Low-Resource Strategies

The preceding discussion of scalability highlights a fundamental tension in vision-language pretraining: while larger data and models consistently yield performance gains, the annotation costs, computational requirements, and data availability constraints facing most practitioners make naive scaling impractical. This tension motivates a complementary research agenda focused on data efficiency—achieving strong VLP performance without exhaustive annotation requirements—particularly for resource-constrained deployment scenarios. A variety of strategies have been proposed to reduce reliance on costly human-labeled data while preserving the representational richness that large-scale pretraining affords.

**Weak Supervision and Prefix Language Modeling.** One prominent direction leverages weak supervision signals derived from naturally occurring image-text co-occurrences on the web. Rather than requiring densely annotated bounding boxes or manually curated captions, weakly supervised approaches extract learning signals from noisy alt-text and image metadata—the same heterogeneous textual signals discussed in the context of large-scale web data harvesting, but here optimized for maximum utility per example rather than aggregate volume. Complementing this, prefix language modeling objectives enable unified vision-language pretraining over large-scale image-text pairs without requiring task-specific supervision, allowing models to generalize effectively across understanding and generation tasks with reduced annotation overhead.

**Knowledge Distillation from Large Pretrained Models.** Knowledge distillation has emerged as a powerful technique for improving data efficiency by transferring learned representations from large, well-trained teacher models to smaller student networks. Distillation-based pretraining allows practitioners to train compact VLP models with significantly fewer data samples while preserving much of the representational quality of the teacher. This approach is especially attractive in settings where computational resources or labeled data are limited. Works such as [26] demonstrate that distilling object-level knowledge from external detectors into end-to-end VLP frameworks can substantially improve cross-modal alignment quality, reducing the need for large annotated datasets by providing richer supervisory signals from pre-learned representations.

**Prompt-Based and Parameter-Efficient Low-Resource Learning.** Prompt tuning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods have shown considerable promise for adapting large pretrained VLP models to downstream tasks with minimal labeled data. By freezing the majority of pretrained model parameters and optimizing only a small set of soft prompts or adapter modules, these approaches achieve competitive performance on low-resource benchmarks while dramatically reducing the annotation cost associated with full model fine-tuning. Such methods leverage the rich prior knowledge encoded in pretrained VLP models, making them particularly effective when labeled examples are scarce.

**Mix-Based Data Augmentation.** Data augmentation strategies that mix visual and textual inputs across samples have been explored to improve sample efficiency during pretraining. By constructing synthetic training examples through interpolation or combination of existing image-text pairs, mix-based augmentation increases the effective diversity of training data without requiring additional annotations. These augmentation strategies help the model learn more robust cross-modal representations by exposing it to a wider variety of visual-linguistic compositions within a fixed annotation budget.

**Text-Only Training with LLM-Generated Data.** An increasingly viable approach involves using large language models (LLMs) to generate synthetic textual descriptions or pseudo-captions for visual inputs, thereby bypassing the need for human-annotated image-text pairs entirely in certain training stages. By prompting LLMs to produce diverse and contextually rich descriptions, researchers can construct large-scale pseudo-labeled datasets that supplement or even replace hand-annotated corpora. This approach is particularly valuable in specialized domains such as medical imaging or industrial inspection, where expert annotation is prohibitively expensive—a challenge that will be revisited in the context of domain-specific data strategies in the following subsection.

**Sample-Efficient Knowledge-Augmented Pretraining.** Knowledge-augmented pretraining strategies improve data efficiency by incorporating external structured knowledge—such as entity descriptions, relational facts, or scene graphs—into the pretraining process. By grounding image-text pairs in broader world knowledge, these methods enable models to learn richer semantic representations from fewer examples, reducing the quantity of raw image-text data required to achieve strong downstream performance. Retrieval-augmented approaches that dynamically incorporate relevant knowledge snippets during training further enhance sample efficiency by providing contextually appropriate supervision signals on a per-sample basis.

**Practical Implications for Resource-Constrained Settings.** Collectively, these strategies offer a toolkit for building effective VLP systems under realistic resource constraints. Models such as those leveraging unified transformer architectures [77] and end-to-end visual encoders [38] demonstrate that architectural simplification combined with efficient training objectives can substantially reduce pretraining costs. Furthermore, approaches that unify multiple pretraining tasks—such as contrastive learning, masked language modeling, and image-text matching—within a single framework [76] allow practitioners to maximize the utility of limited training data by jointly optimizing complementary learning signals. Crucially, the data efficiency strategies surveyed here are not alternatives to the multilingual and domain-specific considerations addressed in the following subsection, but rather prerequisites: without principled methods for learning from limited and noisy supervision, extending VLP to low-resource languages and specialized domains becomes even more challenging. As VLP continues to scale, developing principled methods for data-efficient pretraining remains critical for democratizing access to high-performance multimodal models across organizations with varying computational resources.

### 4.10 Multilingual and Domain-Specific Data Strategies

## 4.10 Multilingual and Domain-Specific Data Strategies

Building directly on the data efficiency strategies discussed in the preceding section, we now turn to two closely related dimensions of VLP data challenges that compound the difficulties of limited supervision: the need to extend pretraining beyond English-language corpora, and the need to adapt VLP to specialized domains where annotation is scarce and data distributions diverge sharply from standard web-crawled pipelines. The dominant paradigm of vision-language pretraining has historically centered on English-language image-text corpora, but the growing need for globally inclusive and domain-specialized AI systems has driven significant efforts to extend VLP data strategies to multilingual and specialized settings. These extensions introduce unique challenges in data collection, cross-lingual alignment, quality control, and domain-specific annotation that differ substantially from standard web-crawled English pretraining pipelines—and that render the data efficiency techniques surveyed previously not merely helpful, but essential.

### Multilingual Vision-Language Data

Constructing large-scale multilingual cross-modal datasets requires overcoming both the scarcity of non-English image-text pairs and the difficulty of ensuring semantic consistency across languages. One prominent strategy involves leveraging machine translation to augment existing English image-text corpora, effectively bootstrapping multilingual supervision from monolingual sources. However, translation introduces noise and may fail to capture culturally specific visual concepts that are not directly transferable across languages. To address data scarcity for under-resourced languages, dual-task learning frameworks such as those exemplified by [84] have combined image-caption matching with translation pair matching, enabling the model to benefit from billions of text-to-text translation pairs alongside image-text pairs. This approach substantially improves cross-lingual retrieval performance for under-resourced languages where image-caption data is sparse, demonstrating that text-text learning can compensate for the paucity of image-caption examples in multilingual pretraining—a form of cross-modal knowledge transfer that echoes the distillation and augmentation principles discussed in Section 4.9.

Beyond translation-based augmentation, code-switching strategies—where sentences mix multiple languages—have been explored as a training technique to encourage shared multilingual representations grounded in visual context. The availability of diverse multilingual benchmarks is equally critical: large-scale multilingual cross-modal benchmarks provide standardized evaluation platforms that allow fair comparison of multilingual VLP methods across typologically diverse languages. These benchmarks also expose systematic disparities in model performance between high-resource and low-resource languages, motivating further research into balanced multilingual corpus construction.

Cross-lingual transfer, where a model pretrained on English image-text data is evaluated in other languages, has emerged as an important evaluation paradigm. The shared visual modality acts as a language-agnostic pivot, enabling cross-lingual alignment even when direct multilingual parallel text is unavailable. Contrastive objectives applied over multilingual text encoders and visual encoders can leverage this property to achieve zero-shot cross-lingual transfer, as explored in multiple multilingual VLP frameworks. Nevertheless, the quality of cross-lingual alignment is sensitive to tokenizer design, shared vocabulary coverage, and the distribution of training languages, all of which require careful attention during corpus construction and preprocessing.

### Domain-Specific Data Strategies

Extending VLP to specialized domains introduces a distinct set of data challenges that further underscore the importance of the low-resource and data-efficient strategies described in the previous section. In the medical domain, for instance, image-report pairs sourced from clinical repositories such as MIMIC-CXR provide a natural form of weakly aligned multimodal supervision, but these datasets are far smaller than web-crawled general-domain corpora and exhibit highly specialized vocabulary, complex clinical reasoning requirements, and strict privacy constraints. Medical VLP models must contend with the fine-grained nature of clinical language—where a single word can carry significant diagnostic weight—and the heterogeneity of imaging modalities including chest X-rays, CT scans, and MRI, each requiring modality-specific preprocessing and representation strategies. Masked contrastive and reconstruction frameworks designed for cross-modal medical retrieval, such as [85], address these challenges by unifying contrastive learning and masked reconstruction within a single training paradigm, demonstrating that carefully designed objectives can compensate for limited data availability in clinical settings.

In food and recipe domains, VLP datasets typically pair food images with ingredient lists and instructional text, requiring models to reason about fine-grained visual attributes such as color, texture, and preparation state. These datasets often exhibit a long-tail distribution over dish categories and significant visual ambiguity, necessitating specialized augmentation and hard negative mining strategies. More broadly, domain-specific corpora frequently suffer from annotation inconsistency, limited scale, and distribution shift relative to general-domain benchmarks, making transfer from general pretrained models an attractive but non-trivial approach.

Quality control in domain-specific and multilingual settings demands tailored filtering pipelines. Standard filtering heuristics developed for English web data—such as language detection, length thresholds, and CLIPScore-based filtering—may be poorly calibrated for non-English or specialized text. For medical data, ethical and regulatory considerations further complicate data collection and sharing, motivating federated and privacy-preserving approaches. Synthetic data generation using large language models or domain-specific captioning models—a strategy previewed in the discussion of LLM-generated pseudo-captions in Section 4.9—offers a particularly promising complementary strategy here, especially for low-resource languages and rare medical conditions where real paired data is insufficient.

### Summary

Overall, multilingual and domain-specific data strategies for VLP require moving beyond the assumptions embedded in standard English web-crawled pipelines. The data efficiency techniques surveyed in the previous section—weak supervision, knowledge distillation, prompt-based adaptation, and synthetic data generation—become indispensable tools in these settings, where annotation budgets are tighter and data distributions are more demanding. Key challenges include constructing sufficiently large and well-aligned corpora, handling noise and distribution shift unique to each setting, ensuring quality and consistency across languages and domains, and developing evaluation benchmarks that faithfully assess model capabilities in these specialized contexts. Progress in this area will be essential for making VLP systems genuinely inclusive, clinically applicable, and globally deployable.

## 5 Downstream Tasks, Benchmarks, and Evaluation

### 5.1 Visual Question Answering

Visual Question Answering (VQA) stands as one of the most prominent downstream tasks for evaluating vision-language pretraining (VLP) models, requiring a model to answer natural language questions about image content by jointly reasoning over visual and textual inputs. Like image-text retrieval, VQA serves as a direct measure of cross-modal alignment quality, but extends beyond simple matching to demand fine-grained semantic understanding of both modalities, making it a comprehensive testbed for the representational capabilities cultivated during pretraining.

**Standard Benchmarks and Evaluation Formats**

The VQA v2 dataset has served as the dominant benchmark for evaluating VLP models, offering a large-scale collection of open-ended questions designed to mitigate language bias present in the original VQA dataset by providing complementary image pairs. Models such as SimVLM [4] have reported substantial gains on VQA v2, achieving improvements of +3.74% vqa-score over prior pretraining methods, demonstrating how large-scale weak supervision with prefix language modeling objectives can dramatically improve VQA performance. Similarly, BLIP [5] reports a +1.6% improvement in VQA score over prior VLP models through its bootstrapped caption filtering framework that combines contrastive, matching, and generative objectives.

Beyond standard open-ended evaluation, GQA provides compositional and reasoning-focused questions derived from scene graphs, targeting systematic generalization and relational reasoning. The A-OKVQA benchmark further pushes the boundary by requiring commonsense and world knowledge that goes beyond visual content alone, demanding models to integrate external knowledge with visual perception.

**Dataset Biases and Mitigation**

A persistent challenge in VQA benchmarks is dataset bias, where models can achieve competitive performance by exploiting statistical regularities in answer distributions without genuinely grounding answers in visual content. The multi-task training paradigm introduced in the 12-in-1 framework [3] addresses this partly by jointly training across multiple vision-language tasks, reducing overfitting to task-specific biases and demonstrating that multi-task training achieves performance at or above state-of-the-art on individual benchmarks while drastically reducing total parameter count.

**Scene-Text VQA Variants**

An important specialization of VQA involves questions that require reading and understanding text embedded within images, giving rise to scene-text VQA benchmarks such as TextVQA, OCR-VQA, and ST-VQA. These tasks demand that models not only recognize objects and scenes but also perform optical character recognition (OCR) on in-image text and reason about it jointly with the visual context. The UniTNT framework [7] specifically addresses this challenge by treating scene-text information as an additional modality fused with a pretrained encoder-decoder architecture, resulting in the first single model capable of handling both scene-text and general VQA tasks, with improvements of up to 2.69% on general VQA benchmarks as a byproduct of scene-text understanding capabilities.

**From Discriminative to Generative VQA**

Early VLP approaches typically formulated VQA as a discriminative classification problem, selecting answers from a fixed vocabulary. However, modern generative VLP frameworks have enabled a paradigm shift toward open-ended answer generation. The unified VLP model [6] was among the first to demonstrate that a shared transformer architecture with task-specific attention masking could unify both generation and understanding objectives, achieving state-of-the-art results on VQA 2.0 while also supporting image captioning. SimVLM [4] extended this further by adopting a pure prefix language modeling objective in an encoder-decoder architecture, enabling zero-shot open-ended VQA where the model generates free-form answers without being constrained to a fixed answer vocabulary. This generative formulation offers greater flexibility, particularly for knowledge-intensive questions where the answer space cannot be predefined, and draws a natural parallel to the generative scoring methods discussed in the context of image-text retrieval.

**VQA as a Holistic Evaluation Task**

VQA has also been incorporated into broader holistic evaluation frameworks. The VLUE benchmark [57] uses VQA among other tasks to measure the generalization abilities of VLP models on out-of-distribution image distributions, revealing that existing models often overestimate their capabilities when evaluated only on in-distribution test sets. The 12-in-1 multi-task benchmark [3] similarly includes VQA as one of the core tasks in its comprehensive evaluation paradigm. Furthermore, diagnostic frameworks such as VL-CheckList [55] have probed the fine-grained capabilities underlying VQA performance, revealing that even high-scoring VLP models exhibit systematic weaknesses in attribute understanding and relational reasoning that are not captured by aggregate VQA accuracy metrics alone. Collectively, these developments position VQA not merely as a benchmark but as a rich diagnostic tool for understanding the depth of cross-modal semantic alignment in VLP systems, complementing the alignment-focused perspective offered by image-text retrieval evaluation.

### 5.2 Image-Text Retrieval

Image-text retrieval is one of the most fundamental and widely used evaluation tasks for vision-language pretraining (VLP) models, serving as a direct measure of cross-modal alignment quality. The task encompasses two complementary directions: image-to-text retrieval (given an image, retrieve the most relevant captions) and text-to-image retrieval (given a textual query, retrieve the most relevant images). Standard benchmarks for this task include MS-COCO Captions and Flickr30K, which provide large collections of image-caption pairs with multiple human-annotated descriptions per image, enabling rigorous evaluation of both recall precision and ranking quality across diverse visual and linguistic content.

**Evaluation Settings and Architectural Considerations**

Evaluation is typically conducted under two settings: zero-shot retrieval, where models are evaluated directly without any fine-tuning on the target dataset, and fine-tuned retrieval, where models are adapted to the target benchmark using its training split. Zero-shot retrieval is particularly important for assessing the generalization of large-scale pretrained representations, and it has become a standard metric for contrastive VLP models. Dual-stream or two-tower architectures, which maintain separate visual and language encoders and compute similarity via dot product or cosine similarity in a shared embedding space, are especially well-suited for retrieval tasks due to their efficiency in indexing and querying large-scale corpora [2]. This architectural advantage stands in contrast to fusion-based single-stream models, which require joint encoding of every query-candidate pair and are thus computationally expensive for large-scale retrieval scenarios.

**Contrastive Pretraining for Retrieval**

Contrastive pretraining objectives play a central role in achieving strong retrieval performance. By training models to maximize agreement between matched image-text pairs while pushing apart mismatched pairs within large mini-batches, contrastive learning directly optimizes the embedding space for retrieval [11]. Models trained with such objectives can efficiently encode images and texts independently, allowing pre-computation of feature vectors for fast retrieval at inference time. This direct optimization of cross-modal alignment makes image-text retrieval a natural and sensitive probe for the quality of representations learned during pretraining.

**Generative Scoring and Multi-Task Perspectives**

Beyond discriminative contrastive approaches, generative scoring methods have emerged as an alternative paradigm for image-text retrieval. Rather than relying solely on embedding similarity, these methods score candidate pairs by measuring the likelihood of a text given an image under a generative VLP model, offering a complementary perspective on cross-modal alignment that can capture finer-grained semantic correspondences not easily encoded in a single global embedding vector. This generative perspective on retrieval also connects naturally to the broader shift toward generative VLP formulations explored in the context of tasks such as VQA, where answer generation rather than answer selection increasingly defines the state of the art. The multi-task training paradigm [3] has further demonstrated that jointly training on diverse vision-language tasks including retrieval can improve overall model performance and representation quality compared to independently trained task-specific models, a finding that resonates with the multi-task evaluation philosophy adopted in subsequent holistic benchmarking efforts.

**Retrieval as a Foundation for Downstream Evaluation**

As VLP models continue to scale, image-text retrieval remains an indispensable benchmark for tracking progress in cross-modal alignment, generalization, and efficiency. The alignment quality measured by retrieval benchmarks directly underpins performance on more complex downstream tasks such as VQA, visual reasoning, and natural language inference, where models must move beyond global embedding similarity toward fine-grained semantic and logical understanding of multimodal inputs. In this sense, image-text retrieval provides the foundational alignment perspective against which the richer reasoning capabilities probed by the tasks discussed in subsequent subsections can be contextualized and interpreted.

### 5.3 Visual Reasoning and Natural Language Inference

Visual reasoning and natural language inference represent some of the most demanding evaluation settings for vision-language pretraining (VLP) models, requiring not only perceptual grounding but also logical and compositional reasoning over multimodal inputs. While image-text retrieval probes global cross-modal alignment, the tasks discussed in this subsection push models further toward structured inference, moving beyond embedding similarity toward genuine multimodal understanding.

**Core Benchmarks.** The NLVR2 benchmark requires models to determine whether a natural language statement is true with respect to a pair of images, demanding structured multi-image reasoning, negation handling, and compositional understanding of object attributes and spatial relations. SNLI-VE (Visual Entailment) extends textual natural language inference to the visual domain, asking models to classify whether a premise image entails, contradicts, or is neutral with respect to a hypothesis sentence. Visual Commonsense Reasoning (VCR) goes further by demanding not only correct answer selection for visual questions but also the provision of rationales, probing deeper commonsense knowledge integration. These benchmarks collectively stress-test a model's capacity for multi-step inference beyond simple pattern matching, complementing the fine-grained spatial and grounding evaluations discussed in the following subsection.

**VLP Models on Visual Reasoning Tasks.** A range of VLP models have demonstrated substantial gains on these benchmarks relative to task-specific baselines. Dual-stream models such as ViLBERT [23] established strong early results on VCR and visual question answering by leveraging co-attentional transformer layers to facilitate cross-modal interaction. Lightweight yet competitive models like ViLT [21] demonstrated that region-based visual features are not strictly necessary for competitive performance on NLVR2 and SNLI-VE, challenging the prevailing reliance on object detectors. Encoder-decoder approaches such as SimVLM [4] achieved state-of-the-art results on NLVR2 (+1.17% accuracy) and SNLI-VE (+1.37% accuracy) through a single prefix language modeling objective trained on large-scale weakly supervised data, highlighting the power of generative pretraining for visual reasoning. The unified multimodal foundation model BEiT-3 [13] similarly reported state-of-the-art performance on NLVR2 by leveraging Multiway Transformers that enable both deep fusion and modality-specific encoding.

Unified VLP models such as the one presented in [6] demonstrated that a shared transformer architecture trained with bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence objectives can achieve competitive performance across both understanding and generation tasks, including visual reasoning benchmarks, without requiring separate model designs per task. This stands in contrast to the multi-task training paradigm highlighted in the context of retrieval, where jointly training on diverse tasks similarly yielded representation improvements, underscoring a broader trend toward unified and generalist VLP architectures.

**Compositional and Logical Reasoning Challenges.** Despite impressive benchmark numbers, VLP models continue to exhibit systematic limitations in handling fine-grained compositional language concepts. Negation logic remains particularly challenging: models often fail to distinguish affirmative from negated statements when the visual evidence is subtly different. Attribute ownership—correctly binding descriptive attributes to specific entities in complex scenes—and relationship composition involving multi-hop reasoning chains over object interactions are areas where current models frequently underperform. Probing studies and diagnostic benchmarks have revealed that many VLP models tend to rely on spurious statistical correlations in training data rather than genuine logical inference, exhibiting bag-of-words-like behaviors that neglect syntactic structure and word order. These compositional failures are closely related to the spatial and relational reasoning deficiencies discussed in the context of visual grounding, where models similarly struggle to capture precise object relationships and multi-step geometric reasoning.

**Commonsense Knowledge Integration.** VCR's rationale prediction subtask specifically exposes the limitations of models lacking world knowledge beyond what is visible in the image. VLP models pretrained on image-text pairs alone often struggle to integrate implicit commonsense knowledge required to generate or select plausible rationales, motivating hybrid approaches that incorporate external knowledge bases or large language models as knowledge providers. The transition from purely discriminative to generative VQA approaches enabled by recent VLP frameworks—such as those in SimVLM [4]—offers a pathway toward more flexible and knowledge-grounded visual reasoning, though fully addressing multi-step inference and commonsense integration remains an open research challenge. Tackling these limitations will require not only advances in pretraining objectives and model architecture, but also richer supervision signals of the kind explored through grounding-oriented and position-sensitive approaches, as discussed in the following subsection.

### 5.4 Visual Grounding and Spatial Understanding

## 5.4 Visual Grounding and Spatial Reasoning

Visual grounding is a fundamental capability for vision-language models, requiring them to localize specific objects or regions in an image based on natural language descriptions. This encompasses a range of tasks including referring expression comprehension (REC), phrase grounding, and region-level understanding, each demanding fine-grained alignment between linguistic expressions and visual regions. While the previous subsection examined how VLP models handle compositional and logical reasoning over multimodal inputs, visual grounding pushes further toward spatially precise understanding—probing whether models can not only reason about what is present in a scene but also where it is located relative to other entities. This spatial dimension of understanding directly complements the generative captioning capabilities evaluated in the following subsection, where models must synthesize coherent language that accurately reflects object positions, attributes, and relations.

### Benchmarks and Task Formulations

The most widely adopted benchmarks for visual grounding include RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg, which evaluate a model's ability to localize objects described by natural language referring expressions. These datasets challenge models to disambiguate among multiple instances of the same object category using spatial, relational, and attributive descriptions. Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR) benchmarks further probe spatial relation understanding by requiring models to determine whether a stated spatial relationship between two objects (e.g., "the cup is to the left of the plate") holds in an image. Together, these benchmarks form a rigorous testbed for assessing whether VLP models achieve the kind of fine-grained cross-modal alignment that surface-level retrieval and classification metrics may not adequately capture.

### Position-Sensitive and Grounded VLP Approaches

Early VLP models often relied on pretrained object detectors such as Faster R-CNN to produce region-level features, which were then aligned with textual tokens through cross-modal attention [71]. While these region-based approaches provided explicit spatial anchors, they were constrained by the concept vocabulary and computational overhead of the external detector. End-to-end approaches such as E2E-VLP sought to overcome these limitations by jointly learning visual representations and semantic alignments within a unified Transformer encoder-decoder framework, eliminating the two-stage bottleneck [34]. Similarly, KD-VLP introduced phrase-region alignment as a pretraining objective, using object features and semantic labels from external detectors as supervision to improve cross-modal alignment at different semantic granularities [26].

To endow VLP models with more explicit positional awareness, position-sensitive pretraining approaches have been proposed. Works such as UNIMO-2 introduced grounded learning on both images and texts via a shared grounded space to bridge unaligned visual and textual semantics and improve alignment on various cross-modal tasks [86]. More fine-grained grounding supervision is provided by frameworks that incorporate bounding box coordinates and positional prompts directly into the pretraining objectives. For instance, PEVL and related prompt-based methods integrate explicit object position modeling into the pretraining and fine-tuning pipeline, enabling the model to associate positional tokens with linguistic descriptions and thereby improving referring expression comprehension and visual grounding performance.

Multi-level semantic alignment strategies have also demonstrated benefits for grounding tasks. MVPTR explicitly introduces phrase-to-region alignment alongside token-level and concept-level alignment, providing hierarchical cross-modal correspondences that support grounded understanding [29]. Similarly, UNIMO-3 captures both in-layer and cross-layer cross-modal interactions, enabling adaptive alignment at different semantic levels that can benefit fine-grained region understanding [28].

Fine-grained token-level alignment objectives have proven especially valuable for grounding. FILIP introduced a token-wise maximum similarity mechanism for cross-modal contrastive learning, demonstrating that patch-word alignment provides meaningful localization capabilities [25]. Similarly, multi-level alignment approaches that combine global semantic and local patch-token correspondences, such as SIMLA's symmetric cross-modality reconstruction and pseudo-labeled keyword prediction tasks, have shown improved performance on grounding benchmarks by fostering finer-grained visual-textual correspondences [87].

The VLMAE framework addresses the problem of focal bias arising from information disparity between visual and textual inputs, which can impair grounding performance when models over-attend to a subset of image regions. By incorporating visual generative learning through masked autoencoders, VLMAE encourages models to attend to a more comprehensive set of image patches, leading to improved performance on visual grounding tasks [32].

### Persistent Challenges

Despite these advances, visual grounding remains a challenging frontier for VLP models. Probing studies have revealed that vision-language models tend to focus primarily on object-level alignment while neglecting relational and spatial semantics [33]. Models trained with standard image-text matching and contrastive objectives often fail to capture precise spatial relationships such as "above," "behind," or "between," which require not only object recognition but also geometric reasoning about relative positions within a scene. These spatial and relational deficiencies echo the compositional reasoning failures discussed in the previous subsection, where models similarly struggle with negation, attribute binding, and multi-hop relational inference—suggesting that both challenges share an underlying limitation in how current VLP objectives encourage structured cross-modal reasoning.

The separation between semantic label prediction and spatial localization represents a fundamental tension in VLP design: while vision-language models excel at label prediction tasks such as object and attribute recognition, vision-only models tend to retain stronger dense prediction and localization capabilities [88]. This suggests that standard cross-modal pretraining objectives may inadvertently trade off localization precision for semantic richness. Addressing this gap remains an active area of research, with approaches that incorporate explicit bounding box supervision, position-aware prompts, and region-level contrastive objectives representing promising directions for improving the spatial and grounding capabilities of future VLP systems. These spatially grounded representations also lay an important foundation for the image captioning task examined in the following subsection, where accurately describing object positions, spatial relations, and region-level attributes requires precisely the kind of fine-grained visual-linguistic alignment that grounding-oriented pretraining seeks to instill.

### 5.5 Image Captioning

## 5.5 Image Captioning

Image captioning occupies a dual role in the VLP ecosystem: it serves simultaneously as a pretraining objective that encourages models to generate coherent textual descriptions from visual inputs, and as a canonical downstream generation task on which pretrained models are evaluated and compared. This duality has made captioning a central benchmark for assessing the generative capabilities of VLP frameworks. Just as visual grounding tasks test fine-grained alignment between language and spatial regions, image captioning tests the complementary ability to synthesize coherent language from holistic visual understanding—making it an equally important lens through which VLP progress is measured.

### Standard Benchmarks and Evaluation Metrics

The most widely used benchmark for image captioning is COCO Captions, which provides five human-annotated reference captions per image and has become the standard testbed for comparing captioning models. NoCaps extends this evaluation to novel object categories not seen during training, testing the compositional generalization ability of captioning models across in-domain, near-domain, and out-of-domain image distributions. Evaluation is typically conducted using a suite of automated metrics including CIDEr, which measures consensus with human references via TF-IDF weighted n-gram similarity and is particularly well-correlated with human judgments; BLEU, which assesses n-gram precision against reference captions; and SPICE, which evaluates semantic propositional content by comparing scene graph representations of generated and reference captions, capturing object, attribute, and relationship accuracy more directly than n-gram-based metrics. Together, these benchmarks and metrics provide a multifaceted view of captioning quality, from surface-level fluency to deeper semantic fidelity.

### Role of Encoder-Decoder Architectures

Encoder-decoder architectures have proven especially well-suited to image captioning, as they naturally separate visual understanding from language generation. Early VLP models that adopted unified bidirectional transformers struggled to generate fluent captions due to the mismatch between bidirectional encoding and left-to-right generation. To address this, several unified VLP frameworks introduced sequence-to-sequence objectives that jointly support both understanding and generation. For example, the unified VLP model [6] pioneered a shared multi-layer transformer for both encoding and decoding, using bidirectional and sequence-to-sequence masked prediction objectives, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on COCO Captions and Flickr30K Captions simultaneously. Similarly, E2E-VLP [34] incorporated image captioning directly into the pretraining procedure through a unified transformer encoder-decoder architecture, bypassing the reliance on pretrained object detectors and improving end-to-end visual learning. These architectural innovations mark a decisive shift away from pipeline-based approaches toward fully integrated models capable of both understanding and generation.

### Prefix Language Modeling for Captioning

A particularly influential pretraining objective for captioning is prefix language modeling, where the model is conditioned on a visual prefix and trained to predict subsequent tokens autoregressively. SimVLM [4] demonstrated that training with a single prefix language modeling objective on large-scale weakly supervised data yields dramatic improvements on captioning benchmarks, achieving a +10.1% average CIDEr score improvement over prior methods on COCO and NoCaps without task-specific customization. This approach also enables strong zero-shot captioning transfer, where models trained on web-scale image-text data can generate captions for previously unseen domains without any fine-tuning. The simplicity and scalability of prefix language modeling thus represents a compelling alternative to more complex multi-objective pretraining regimes.

### BLIP and Bootstrapped Caption Filtering

A key challenge in VLP-based captioning is that web-crawled image-text pairs often contain noisy, incomplete, or misaligned descriptions that provide suboptimal supervision—a limitation that also affects the grounding and alignment capabilities discussed in the previous subsection. BLIP [5] addresses this limitation through a bootstrapped caption filtering strategy, wherein a captioner generates synthetic captions for web images and a learned filter removes noisy examples before retraining. This iterative self-improvement procedure significantly enhances the quality of training data and leads to state-of-the-art results on COCO Captions captioning (+2.8% in CIDEr) while also supporting image-text retrieval and visual question answering within a single unified model. By jointly advancing captioning and other VLP tasks, BLIP exemplifies the broader trend toward unified architectures that share representations across generation and understanding objectives.

### Position-Guided and Region-Level Extensions

Beyond standard whole-image captioning, VLP models have been extended to support region-level and position-guided captioning, reflecting the same drive toward spatially grounded understanding that motivates advances in visual grounding. PTP [89] incorporates spatial awareness into pretraining by reformulating grounding as a fill-in-the-blank problem with position-guided text prompts, demonstrating a +5.3 CIDEr improvement on COCO Captioning when applied to strong baselines such as BLIP. RegionVLM [90] leverages Localized Narratives to train models capable of generating region-specific captions in response to user-indicated image areas, extending captioning beyond global image descriptions to support interactive and fine-grained region-level language generation. Such extensions reflect the broader trend of moving from coarse global captioning toward spatially grounded and semantically richer description generation, and they naturally connect captioning research to the scene text understanding challenges addressed in the following subsection, where generating descriptions of text-rich images demands further integration of recognition and language generation capabilities.

### Summary

Image captioning remains a critical evaluation axis for VLP models, with performance on COCO and NoCaps serving as widely accepted indicators of generative capability. The field has progressed from early two-stage region-feature-based captioning pipelines toward end-to-end encoder-decoder frameworks trained with prefix language modeling and bootstrapped data filtering strategies. As captioning models grow more spatially aware and semantically expressive, they increasingly intersect with challenges in visual grounding, scene text understanding, and other tasks that demand tighter integration of visual perception and language generation. Continued advances in evaluation metrics, benchmark diversity, and model architectures are likely to further push the frontier of captioning quality and semantic richness.

### 5.6 Scene Text Understanding

## 5.6 Scene Text Understanding

Scene text understanding represents a specialized yet increasingly important downstream task for VLP models, requiring the joint processing of natural language descriptions and textual content embedded within images themselves. While the previous subsection examined image captioning as a test of holistic visual-linguistic generation, scene text understanding extends this challenge by demanding that models recognize, read, and reason over text as it appears in real-world visual scenes—on signs, product labels, menus, documents, and other text-rich environments. This additional layer of complexity places scene text understanding at a unique intersection of visual perception, optical character recognition, and multimodal reasoning.

### Benchmarks for Scene Text Understanding

Several benchmarks have emerged to evaluate this capability. TextVQA requires models to read and reason about text present in images to answer natural language questions, testing whether VLP systems can integrate OCR-like recognition with broader visual and semantic understanding. OCR-VQA extends this paradigm to book covers and other document-like images, demanding fine-grained text recognition alongside visual comprehension. ST-VQA (Scene Text Visual Question Answering) similarly probes a model's ability to leverage scene text as a critical source of evidence for answering questions, covering diverse real-world scenarios including storefronts, street signs, and infographics. Image captioning with scene text adds another dimension to this challenge, requiring generative models to produce descriptions that faithfully incorporate textual elements visible within the image rather than ignoring them as irrelevant visual noise—a natural extension of the captioning capabilities discussed in the previous subsection.

### Limitations of Standard VLP Frameworks on Scene Text Tasks

A significant limitation of many VLP frameworks is their tendency to treat text-rich and general vision-language understanding as separate problems, often requiring distinct model architectures or separate fine-tuning procedures for each. Models trained primarily on natural image-text pairs frequently underperform on scene text tasks because their visual encoders are not designed to capture fine-grained character-level visual patterns, and their pretraining objectives do not explicitly reward reading comprehension over embedded text. Unified approaches that bridge these two domains within a single architecture therefore represent an important research direction, one that connects naturally to the broader push toward holistic multi-task models evaluated in the following subsection.

### Multi-Level Reasoning Requirements

The broader challenge is that scene text understanding requires capabilities spanning multiple levels of abstraction: low-level character recognition, mid-level word and phrase assembly, and high-level semantic reasoning that connects the recognized text with the surrounding visual context and the posed question or captioning objective. VLP models that excel on standard benchmarks such as VQA v2 or COCO Captions do not necessarily transfer these skills to scene text scenarios, as evidenced by the performance gaps commonly observed when such models are evaluated on TextVQA or ST-VQA without specialized adaptation. This disconnect underscores the importance of designing pretraining objectives and visual encoders that explicitly account for the unique demands of text-rich visual environments.

### Visual Encoder Design and Its Impact

Evaluation on these benchmarks also reveals the importance of visual encoder design choices discussed in the broader VLP literature [2], where region-based and patch-based encoders differ substantially in their ability to capture fine-grained textual detail within images. Overall, scene text understanding serves as a rigorous test of whether VLP models have acquired genuinely integrated multimodal reasoning capabilities or rely primarily on linguistic priors from their language encoder components. As VLP models are increasingly expected to generalize across diverse and demanding scenarios, performance on scene text benchmarks provides a valuable complement to the multi-task holistic evaluations examined in the following subsection, helping to expose targeted capability gaps that aggregate metrics alone may obscure.

### 5.7 Multi-Task Benchmarks and Holistic Evaluation Frameworks

As vision-language pretraining (VLP) models have grown increasingly capable across a broad range of tasks, the need for holistic and multi-task evaluation frameworks has become paramount. While the scene text benchmarks discussed above probe a specific and challenging capability, single-task benchmarks more generally often fail to capture the full scope of a model's generalization ability, motivating the development of comprehensive multi-task evaluation paradigms that assess models across diverse skills simultaneously.

One of the earliest and most influential multi-task evaluation paradigms is the **12-in-1** framework [3], which investigates the relationships between vision-and-language tasks by developing a large-scale, multi-task training and evaluation regime spanning 12 datasets across four broad categories: visual question answering, caption-based image retrieval, grounding referring expressions, and multi-modal verification. This work demonstrates that a single jointly trained model can significantly reduce total parameter counts—from approximately 3 billion to 270 million—while simultaneously improving average performance by over 2 points compared to independently trained single-task models, highlighting the synergistic benefits of multi-task learning.

The **VLUE** (Vision-Language Understanding Evaluation) benchmark [57] addresses two critical gaps in prior evaluation practices: the overestimation of model generalization due to overlap between pretraining and evaluation images, and the neglect of efficiency-performance trade-offs in model development. VLUE introduces out-of-distribution test sets annotated on images drawn from a more culturally diverse distribution, revealing a sizable generalization gap for all evaluated VLP models when tested beyond the standard in-distribution setting. Furthermore, VLUE introduces the concept of "Pareto SOTA," which jointly considers efficiency and performance to provide a more realistic picture of practical model progress. This multi-dimensional evaluation reveals complementary insights about VLP design choices that would otherwise remain hidden under aggregate accuracy metrics alone.

**MMBench** [60] represents another important step toward holistic evaluation of large vision-language models. MMBench constructs a meticulously curated dataset that surpasses existing benchmarks in terms of the number and variety of evaluation questions, and introduces a novel CircularEval strategy combined with ChatGPT-based answer parsing to convert free-form predictions into pre-defined choices, thereby enabling more robust and reproducible evaluation. By systematically covering a wide range of multimodal abilities, MMBench aims to assess whether modern VLMs are truly "all-around players" rather than specialists optimized for narrow benchmarks—a concern that directly complements the targeted, fine-grained probing discussed in the following section on diagnostic benchmarks.

Beyond these structured benchmarks, researchers have also explored leveraging large language models (LLMs) for automated benchmark curation. Auto-Bench-style approaches harness the generative capabilities of LLMs to automatically construct diverse question-answer pairs from image-text inputs, reducing the cost and bottleneck of human annotation while expanding coverage across a richer variety of visual and linguistic phenomena. This direction aligns with broader trends in the field toward scalable and adaptive evaluation.

ReForm-Eval similarly advances the field by providing a unified evaluation framework for large vision-language models through reformulated task-oriented benchmarks, enabling consistent cross-model comparisons on a shared set of standardized tasks. Such frameworks are critical as the field transitions from specialist models toward general-purpose multimodal assistants [14], where no single benchmark suffices to capture the full breadth of model capabilities.

Collectively, these multi-task and holistic evaluation frameworks underscore a growing consensus in the VLP community: comprehensive model assessment requires going beyond task-specific accuracy toward measuring generalization, efficiency, compositional reasoning, and robustness across diverse distributions and task types [57][3][60]. Yet as these aggregate frameworks make clear, high overall scores can still mask systematic failures in specific capabilities—a limitation that motivates the diagnostic and probing benchmarks examined in the following section. As VLP models continue to scale and diversify, the development of richer, more nuanced evaluation ecosystems combining both holistic and targeted assessments will be essential to accurately track scientific progress and identify remaining limitations.

### 5.8 Diagnostic and Probing Benchmarks

While aggregate accuracy metrics on standard benchmarks provide a useful measure of overall VLP model performance, they often obscure systematic weaknesses and fail to reveal the fine-grained capabilities—or lack thereof—of pretrained vision-language models. As established in the preceding discussion of holistic multi-task evaluation frameworks, high aggregate scores can mask targeted failures in specific capabilities; diagnostic and probing benchmarks have therefore emerged as an essential complement to these broader assessments, targeting specific linguistic, semantic, and perceptual phenomena in a controlled and interpretable manner.

**VL-CheckList** is one prominent diagnostic framework designed to evaluate VLP models along three structured dimensions: object recognition, attribute understanding, and relational reasoning. By decomposing visual-linguistic understanding into these fine-grained categories, VL-CheckList exposes systematic failures that are invisible when relying solely on task-level accuracy, revealing that many state-of-the-art models perform surprisingly poorly on attribute and relation understanding even when achieving competitive scores on benchmarks such as VQAv2 or image-text retrieval.

**VALSE (Vision And Language Structured Evaluation)** targets visio-linguistic grounding by testing models on specific linguistic phenomena such as existence, plurality, counting, spatial relations, actions, and coreference. Rather than measuring end-task performance, VALSE systematically probes whether models genuinely ground language in visual content or merely rely on linguistic biases and statistical shortcuts. Findings from VALSE-style evaluations consistently demonstrate that VLP models, despite impressive downstream performance, remain brittle when confronted with controlled foil examples designed to require true multimodal understanding.

**SNARE (Seeing, Noticing, And Reasoning Evaluation)** probes the lexical, semantic, and syntactic knowledge encoded within VLP models. By assessing whether models can differentiate between subtle linguistic variations—such as synonym substitution, negation, and structural paraphrase—SNARE provides fine-grained insights into the depth of language understanding achieved through cross-modal pretraining. Results from such probing studies frequently reveal that models trained with objectives like masked language modeling or image-text contrastive learning [38] may encode shallow surface-level lexical patterns rather than deep compositional semantics.

**What'sUp** focuses specifically on spatial reasoning, isolating a model's ability to understand spatial relations between objects (e.g., above, below, left, right) from other confounding factors. Spatial understanding has consistently proven to be a significant weakness for VLP models, as contrastive pretraining objectives [67] and fusion-based architectures [66] may capture global semantic correspondences between images and captions without encoding the precise geometric and relational structure of visual scenes.

Collectively, these diagnostic benchmarks serve a critical function in the VLP research ecosystem. Standard downstream evaluations on tasks such as VQA, image-text retrieval, and visual reasoning [38; 13] aggregate performance across many examples and may reward language bias or distributional shortcuts rather than genuine multimodal understanding. Diagnostic benchmarks, by contrast, construct controlled experimental conditions that isolate specific capabilities, making it possible to identify precisely where current VLP architectures succeed and fail. The systematic weaknesses exposed by these probing studies—particularly in spatial reasoning, attribute binding, negation handling, and relational understanding—highlight important directions for future architectural and pretraining objective design, and directly motivate the compositional and structured evaluation frameworks examined in the following section, which further investigate how models encode and reason about structured visual-linguistic relationships.

### 5.9 Compositional and Structured Evaluation

Compositional and structured evaluation has emerged as a critical dimension for assessing vision-language pretrained models, extending beyond the aggregate accuracy metrics and diagnostic probing frameworks discussed in the previous section to reveal deeper systematic weaknesses in how models encode and reason about structured visual-linguistic relationships. While models trained with objectives such as masked language modeling [91; 76] and contrastive learning achieve impressive results on standard downstream tasks, probing studies have revealed that these models often behave as "bags of words," failing to leverage word order and syntactic structure when making cross-modal judgments.

A central concern in compositional evaluation is attribute binding—the ability of a model to correctly associate attributes (e.g., color, shape, size) with the appropriate objects in a scene. Benchmarks such as ARO (Attribution, Relation, and Order) and Cola have been specifically designed to test whether VLP models can distinguish between semantically distinct captions that differ only in the arrangement of attributes and relations. These benchmarks employ foil-based and counterfactual methodologies, constructing negative examples by systematically permuting words or swapping attribute-object relationships, thereby isolating the model's sensitivity to compositional structure rather than holistic semantic similarity.

Foil-based evaluation methodology constructs hard negative captions by replacing specific words—such as object names, attributes, or relational terms—with plausible but incorrect alternatives, testing whether models can reliably distinguish these subtle differences. Counterfactual evaluation similarly generates image-text pairs where small, targeted modifications in either the image or the text should lead to predictable changes in alignment scores. Such methodologies have revealed that many contrastive VLP models are surprisingly insensitive to word order and attribute-object bindings, assigning similar similarity scores to correctly and incorrectly composed descriptions. This finding directly complements the spatial and relational weaknesses identified by diagnostic benchmarks such as What'sUp and VALSE, indicating that these failures reflect a broader structural limitation in how current VLP models encode compositional information.

Research on bag-of-words behaviors in VLMs has highlighted that contrastive pretraining objectives, which optimize global image-text alignment, may inadvertently encourage models to rely on lexical co-occurrence statistics rather than genuine compositional understanding. This means that a model may correctly match an image to a caption not because it understands the compositional meaning, but because the individual words in the caption frequently co-occur with visual concepts present in the image. Studies demonstrate that even state-of-the-art models trained with sophisticated alignment objectives [67; 13] exhibit these limitations when subjected to rigorous compositional probing.

Addressing these limitations, frameworks such as CLoVe and 3VL have been proposed to improve compositional language encoding in contrastive VLP models. CLoVe introduces training objectives that explicitly encourage models to attend to compositional structure by incorporating hard negative examples that require fine-grained understanding of attribute-object and subject-verb-object relationships. Similarly, 3VL augments the pretraining pipeline with structured linguistic knowledge, encouraging models to encode syntactic and semantic composition more faithfully. These approaches demonstrate that targeted modifications to training data and objectives can substantially improve compositional sensitivity without sacrificing performance on standard retrieval benchmarks.

Object relationship evaluation represents another key axis of compositional assessment. Benchmarks in this space test whether models correctly understand spatial relationships (e.g., "the cat is to the left of the dog"), action relationships (e.g., "the person kicks the ball"), and possessive relationships (e.g., "the woman's hat"). Models trained with region-level grounding objectives [92; 26] tend to perform better on relational benchmarks compared to those trained solely with global contrastive objectives, suggesting that fine-grained alignment supervision is important for relational understanding.

Action recognition as part of compositional evaluation examines whether VLP models correctly understand dynamic events and their participants. This is particularly challenging because actions often require understanding temporal relationships and agent-patient structures that are not directly captured by static image-text pairs. Benchmarks targeting action understanding expose the limitations of models pretrained primarily on static image-caption data, motivating extensions to video-language pretraining settings where temporal context is explicitly modeled.

The broader implications of compositional evaluation findings suggest that current VLP training paradigms, while effective for holistic semantic alignment, do not inherently encourage models to develop structured, compositional representations of language and vision. This has motivated research into training strategies that incorporate explicit syntactic supervision, scene graph-based alignment objectives, and multi-granularity semantic tasks [30; 93] that go beyond token-level matching to capture the relational and compositional structure of visual scenes and their textual descriptions. Together, these compositional and structured evaluation frameworks provide an important diagnostic lens for identifying and addressing the structural limitations of current VLP models—limitations that, as the following section discusses, must ultimately be reflected in the metrics and evaluation methodologies used to measure progress in the field.

### 5.10 Evaluation Metrics and Assessment Methodologies

Evaluation metrics and assessment methodologies play a crucial role in quantifying the capabilities of vision-language pretraining (VLP) models, yet the field faces significant challenges in developing metrics that genuinely reflect model intelligence and alignment with human judgment. As established by the compositional and structured evaluation frameworks discussed in the previous section, standard aggregate metrics often fail to capture the full spectrum of model capabilities and limitations—motivating a broader examination of how the field measures progress.

**Traditional NLP and Task-Specific Metrics**

Historically, VLP evaluation has relied heavily on task-specific metrics borrowed from NLP and computer vision. For image captioning tasks, metrics such as BLEU, CIDEr, METEOR, ROUGE, and SPICE have been standard tools for comparing generated captions against human reference descriptions. For discriminative tasks such as visual question answering and image-text retrieval, classification accuracy and Recall@K (R@1, R@5, R@10) have served as the primary measures of model performance. Retrieval tasks on benchmarks like MSCOCO and Flickr30K typically report bidirectional recall metrics, measuring both image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval effectiveness.

While these metrics offer reproducibility and easy comparison across systems, they suffer from well-documented limitations. N-gram-based metrics like BLEU measure lexical overlap rather than semantic correctness, often failing to credit paraphrases or semantically equivalent descriptions. CIDEr, while better calibrated for captioning through TF-IDF weighting, still cannot capture pragmatic and commonsense aspects of language. Similarly, simple accuracy metrics for VQA tasks reduce complex reasoning processes to binary correct/incorrect judgments, obscuring partial understanding and fine-grained reasoning capabilities—precisely the kinds of structural and compositional distinctions that the evaluation frameworks discussed in the previous section were designed to expose.

**Challenges in Evaluating Free-Form Generative Outputs**

As VLP models have evolved from discriminative to increasingly generative paradigms—enabled by encoder-decoder architectures and prefix language modeling objectives—the evaluation of free-form generative outputs has become significantly more challenging. Traditional reference-based metrics become unreliable when models generate plausible but lexically dissimilar answers, which is particularly problematic in open-ended VQA, detailed image description, and multi-turn visual dialogue scenarios. The challenge is compounded by the fact that for many visual queries, multiple valid responses exist, and rigid reference-based evaluation inherently penalizes valid but unexpected outputs.

Furthermore, the evaluation of instruction-following and reasoning capabilities in modern large vision-language models requires assessment methodologies that go beyond simple token-matching. Models trained with instruction tuning objectives can generate fluent and contextually appropriate responses that may nonetheless contain factual errors or hallucinations about visual content—a failure mode that standard automatic metrics are ill-equipped to detect, and one that compositional probing benchmarks alone cannot fully address.

**Limitations of Rigid Hand-Crafted Benchmarks**

A fundamental limitation of existing evaluation paradigms is their reliance on hand-crafted benchmarks with fixed answer sets and predetermined evaluation criteria. Such benchmarks often measure narrow proxies for intelligence rather than the broader capabilities they purport to assess. Studies using diagnostic probing frameworks have revealed that high performance on standard benchmarks can sometimes be achieved through dataset biases and spurious correlations rather than genuine multimodal understanding. For instance, models evaluated on standard VQA benchmarks can perform surprisingly well by exploiting language priors without fully leveraging visual content, highlighting the disconnect between benchmark performance and true visual reasoning ability—a concern that directly echoes the bag-of-words behaviors and attribute binding failures identified through compositional evaluation.

Cross-modal retrieval evaluation similarly faces limitations, as aggregate Recall@K scores on standard benchmarks like Flickr30K and MSCOCO may not reflect model behavior on the diverse and long-tailed distribution of real-world queries. The evaluation of zero-shot and few-shot generalization capabilities introduces additional complexity, as performance can vary dramatically depending on the choice of prompt templates and evaluation protocols, making comparisons across studies difficult to interpret reliably.

**Emerging LLM-Based Assessment Approaches**

The emergence of powerful large language models as automated judges represents a paradigm shift in VLP evaluation methodology. LLM-based evaluation approaches leverage the rich world knowledge and nuanced language understanding of models such as GPT-4 to assess generated outputs along multiple dimensions including factual accuracy, relevance, coherence, and helpfulness. Unlike rigid reference-based metrics, LLM judges can evaluate semantic equivalence, recognize valid paraphrases, and assess the pragmatic appropriateness of responses in context.

These approaches are particularly valuable for evaluating modern instruction-tuned vision-language models that generate detailed, free-form responses to complex visual queries. Automated LLM-based benchmarking frameworks can curate diverse evaluation sets and assess model outputs in a scalable manner, potentially replacing costly human annotation for many evaluation scenarios. However, LLM-based evaluation is not without its own limitations: it may inherit biases from the judge model, can be sensitive to prompt formulation, and may favor verbose or stylistically fluent responses over concise but accurate ones.

**Towards More Comprehensive Evaluation Standards**

The field increasingly recognizes the need for multi-dimensional evaluation frameworks that assess VLP models across diverse capability dimensions including fine-grained visual understanding, compositional reasoning, spatial relation comprehension, and robustness to distribution shift. This recognition is a direct consequence of the cumulative findings from diagnostic probing, compositional benchmarking, and the structural evaluation approaches surveyed in preceding sections—all of which converge on the conclusion that no single metric or benchmark can adequately capture model capability. Holistic evaluation approaches that combine traditional metrics with probing tasks, diagnostic benchmarks, and LLM-based assessment offer a more complete picture of model capabilities and limitations. The development of culturally diverse and linguistically varied evaluation sets is also essential to ensure that performance gains reflect genuine improvements in multimodal understanding rather than overfitting to the specific distributions of dominant benchmark datasets.

In summary, the evolution of VLP evaluation methodology reflects the broader maturation of the field: from simple accuracy metrics on closed-set benchmarks toward more nuanced, multi-faceted assessment frameworks capable of capturing the rich spectrum of capabilities that modern vision-language models are expected to demonstrate. Together with the compositional and structured evaluation frameworks examined throughout this section, these emerging methodologies chart a course toward more rigorous and meaningful measurement of progress in vision-language pretraining research.

## 6 Specialized and Extended VLP Settings

### 6.1 Multilingual and Cross-Lingual Vision-Language Pretraining

## 6.1 Multilingual and Cross-Lingual Vision-Language Pretraining

While the majority of early VLP research focused predominantly on English image-text pairs, the need for multilingual and cross-lingual capabilities has emerged as an increasingly important research direction. Extending VLP to diverse languages presents unique challenges, including the scarcity of high-quality paired image-text data in non-English languages, the structural diversity across language families, and the difficulty of aligning visual representations with semantically equivalent but linguistically distinct textual expressions.

A central insight motivating multilingual VLP is the use of visual content as a language-agnostic semantic pivot. Since images convey meaning independently of language, visual representations can serve as a shared anchor to bridge textual representations across different languages, enabling cross-lingual alignment without requiring direct parallel text corpora. This idea has motivated several distinct methodological approaches, which together form a rich landscape of techniques for extending vision-language intelligence to a global, multilingual context.

**Machine Translation-Augmented Frameworks.** One of the most natural strategies for multilingual VLP is to leverage machine translation to generate multilingual counterparts of English image-text pairs. The UC2 framework exemplifies this approach by translating English captions into multiple target languages and applying code-switching augmentation during pretraining, wherein tokens within a sentence are randomly replaced with their translations in another language. This code-switching strategy encourages the model to develop language-invariant representations and bridges the semantic gap across languages while using the shared visual context as a grounding signal.

**Multitask Multilingual Multimodal Pretraining.** The M3P framework adopts a multitask pretraining paradigm that combines multilingual, multimodal, and multi-task objectives within a single unified model. By jointly training on multilingual text corpora, multilingual image-text pairs, and monolingual English vision-language data, M3P develops a shared representation space that supports both multilingual understanding and cross-modal alignment. The model employs multilingual masked language modeling alongside cross-lingual image-text matching, enabling it to generalize effectively to unseen language-vision combinations in a zero-shot or few-shot manner.

**Regularized Contrastive Cross-Lingual Methods.** Contrastive learning has proven particularly effective for cross-lingual alignment. The RC3 framework introduces regularized contrastive objectives that align representations of semantically equivalent image-text pairs across languages by minimizing distances between matched multilingual text-image pairs while maximizing distances between mismatched ones. Regularization terms are employed to prevent representation collapse and encourage diverse, discriminative embeddings across the multilingual feature space, achieving improved performance on multilingual image-text retrieval benchmarks.

**Lightweight Language Acquisition Strategies.** A practical concern in multilingual VLP is the computational cost of training large models across many languages simultaneously. Lightweight language acquisition (MLA) strategies address this by proposing efficient two-stage training paradigms where the model first undergoes standard English VLP to develop strong visual-linguistic grounding, followed by a lightweight multilingual adaptation stage that transfers learned visual representations to new languages with minimal additional parameters. This decoupled approach reduces the computational overhead of multilingual pretraining while maintaining strong cross-lingual transfer performance.

**Encoder-Decoder Unified Frameworks.** Generative multilingual VLP frameworks such as ERNIE-UniX2 extend the encoder-decoder paradigm to the multilingual setting, enabling unified modeling of both understanding and generation tasks across multiple languages. By incorporating multilingual pretraining objectives including multilingual masked language modeling, cross-lingual image-text matching, and sequence-to-sequence generation, these models can support a wide variety of downstream tasks including multilingual image captioning, cross-lingual visual question answering, and multilingual image-text retrieval within a single architecture.

**Benchmarks and Evaluation.** Evaluating multilingual VLP requires benchmarks that span diverse languages and tasks. Multilingual cross-modal benchmarks such as CCMB (Cross-lingual Cross-modal Benchmark) provide standardized evaluation settings for assessing model performance on image-text retrieval and visual question answering across languages including Chinese, Japanese, French, and German. These benchmarks reveal that multilingual VLP models consistently outperform models trained only on English data when evaluated on non-English tasks, particularly in zero-shot cross-lingual transfer settings where the target language is not seen during pretraining.

**Shared Visual Context as a Cross-Lingual Pivot.** Across all these approaches, a unifying principle is the exploitation of shared visual semantics as a cross-lingual bridge. Since the same image can be paired with descriptions in any language, visual features provide a modality-neutral alignment signal that facilitates the learning of language-invariant representations. This pivot strategy is particularly effective when combined with cross-lingual contrastive objectives, code-switching augmentation, and multilingual masked prediction tasks, enabling VLP models to achieve strong generalization across languages even with limited multilingual paired data.

Overall, multilingual and cross-lingual VLP represents a vibrant and growing research direction that expands the reach of vision-language intelligence beyond the English-speaking world. Key open challenges include improving performance on low-resource languages, handling script diversity and morphological complexity, and developing more culturally diverse and representative training datasets that better reflect the global diversity of visual and linguistic content. These challenges in the static image-text domain foreshadow analogous difficulties encountered when extending VLP to the temporal domain, where the complexity of video understanding introduces yet another layer of modeling demands. The survey of these approaches highlights the importance of combining architectural innovations, data augmentation strategies, and carefully designed pretraining objectives to achieve robust multilingual vision-language understanding [2] [9].

### 6.2 Video-Language and Egocentric Video-Language Pretraining

## 6.2 Video-Language and Egocentric Video-Language Pretraining

Building upon the multilingual extensions of vision-language pretraining explored in the preceding section, which demonstrated how visual content can serve as a language-agnostic semantic pivot across diverse linguistic contexts, this section turns to a complementary axis of extension: the temporal domain. Video-language pretraining (VLP) extends the image-text pretraining paradigm to dynamic visual sequences, where models must learn correspondences between temporally evolving visual content and natural language descriptions. While significant progress has been made in image-text pretraining, video understanding introduces unique challenges including temporal reasoning, motion modeling, and the need to capture fine-grained action semantics across time. A particularly compelling and practically important frontier within video-language pretraining is the egocentric perspective, where video content is captured from a first-person viewpoint, reflecting how individuals perceive and interact with their surrounding environment in everyday activities.

### Egocentric Vision-Language Pretraining: EgoVLP

The development of large-scale egocentric datasets such as Ego4D has created new opportunities for pretraining video-language models on first-person video data at scale. The EgoVLP framework represents a pioneering effort in this direction, introducing the first dedicated egocentric video-language pretraining pipeline. At the core of EgoVLP is the EgoNCE (Egocentric Noise-Contrastive Estimation) objective, a contrastive learning formulation specifically designed to handle the unique characteristics of egocentric video data. Unlike standard video-text contrastive objectives, EgoNCE leverages the rich semantic structure of egocentric activities by incorporating egocentric-specific hard negative mining, where negative pairs are constructed to reflect visually and semantically similar but distinct actions, thereby forcing the model to learn more discriminative representations of fine-grained action semantics.

EgoVLP is pretrained on the Ego4D dataset, which contains thousands of hours of first-person video footage spanning diverse everyday activities across multiple continents and cultural contexts. This large-scale and diverse pretraining data enables the model to develop broad representations of egocentric activities. The pretrained EgoVLP model demonstrates strong transfer performance across multiple egocentric downstream tasks, including natural language query (NLQ), moment query (MQ), action recognition, and multi-instance retrieval on benchmarks such as EPIC-KITCHENS-100 and Ego4D challenge tasks. These results validate the importance of domain-specific pretraining for egocentric understanding, as models pretrained on general video data often fail to capture the distinctive visual patterns and action semantics that characterize first-person footage.

### EgoVLPv2: Cross-Modal Fusion in Backbone Networks

Building upon the foundations established by EgoVLP, EgoVLPv2 introduces a significant architectural advancement by incorporating cross-modal fusion directly into the video and language backbone networks, rather than relying solely on late-fusion strategies applied after independent unimodal encoding. This design choice enables more efficient and effective multimodal interaction during the encoding process itself, allowing visual and textual representations to mutually influence each other at multiple levels of abstraction. By integrating cross-modal attention mechanisms within the backbone encoders, EgoVLPv2 reduces the need for computationally expensive post-hoc fusion modules during fine-tuning, resulting in more parameter-efficient adaptation to downstream tasks.

The cross-modal fusion approach in EgoVLPv2 is particularly beneficial for tasks that require tight alignment between specific video moments and textual queries, such as natural language query and moment retrieval, where precise temporal grounding is essential. The deeper integration of visual and linguistic information within the backbone enables the model to develop stronger cross-modal representations that are better suited to the fine-grained temporal understanding required by egocentric benchmarks. EgoVLPv2 demonstrates consistent improvements over its predecessor across multiple Ego4D challenge tasks, establishing new state-of-the-art results and confirming that architectural innovations enabling earlier cross-modal fusion can yield substantial gains in egocentric video-language understanding.

### Broader Video-Language Pretraining Context

Beyond the egocentric setting, video-language pretraining more broadly encompasses methods that leverage large-scale web-crawled video-text pairs, instructional video datasets, and automatically generated transcripts to learn temporal visual-linguistic correspondences. The challenges of video-language pretraining include handling the high redundancy of visual information across frames, the need for temporal reasoning and action understanding, and the asynchrony between visual events and their textual descriptions. Various pretraining objectives have been explored in this context, including video-text contrastive learning, masked video modeling, and cross-modal temporal alignment objectives.

The success of frameworks like EgoVLP and EgoVLPv2 highlights the importance of domain-specific pretraining strategies and specialized contrastive objectives tailored to the unique characteristics of the target video domain. Their strong performance on benchmarks such as EPIC-KITCHENS-100 and Ego4D challenge tasks demonstrates that general-purpose video-language pretraining approaches, while useful, may be insufficient for capturing the nuanced action semantics and fine-grained temporal dynamics characteristic of egocentric video content. These findings motivate continued research into specialized pretraining paradigms for diverse video domains, including instructional, surveillance, and embodied navigation settings, each presenting distinct challenges for cross-modal representation learning. This progression toward increasingly rich and diverse sensory inputs finds its natural continuation in the following section, which examines how vision-language pretraining can be further extended to encompass audio, depth, and other non-visual modalities within a unified multi-modal framework [2].

### 6.3 Audio-Visual and Multi-Modality Language Pretraining

## 6.3 Audio-Visual and Multi-Modality Language Pretraining

The preceding sections have examined video-language pretraining in standard and egocentric settings, as well as the extension of VLP to three-dimensional spatial understanding and embodied navigation. A complementary and increasingly important direction involves extending vision-language pretraining to encompass an even richer spectrum of sensory modalities, including audio, depth, infrared, and inertial signals. This multi-modality expansion presents both significant opportunities—enabling more holistic scene understanding and more natural human-computer interaction—and considerable technical challenges, such as modality heterogeneity, data scarcity across modality pairs, and the difficulty of establishing semantically coherent cross-modal correspondences beyond the well-studied visual-linguistic domain.

A central challenge in multi-modality pretraining is identifying a common semantic anchor that can effectively bridge disparate sensory inputs. Language has emerged as a particularly powerful such anchor, owing to its rich semantic expressiveness and the availability of large-scale paired data between language and many individual modalities. The LanguageBind framework exemplifies this philosophy by freezing a pretrained language encoder and aligning video, infrared, depth, and audio modalities to the fixed linguistic representation space through contrastive learning. By treating language as the invariant semantic hub, LanguageBind avoids the need for direct cross-modal paired data between non-linguistic modalities (e.g., audio-depth pairs), relying instead on the transitivity of language-aligned representations. This approach allows diverse sensory streams to be mapped into a shared feature space, enabling cross-modal retrieval and zero-shot transfer across a wide variety of benchmarks with minimal additional supervision.

Supporting this multi-modal alignment research, the VIDAL-10M dataset was introduced to provide large-scale supervision across multiple modality pairs, encompassing video, infrared, depth, audio, and language data at scale. The availability of such diverse and large-scale multimodal corpora is critical for training robust multi-modality binding models, as it enables contrastive learning objectives to capture meaningful correspondences across a substantially broader input space than image-text pairs alone afford. This mirrors the role that large-scale web-crawled video-text corpora play in general video-language pretraining, underscoring that data scale and diversity are foundational requirements regardless of the specific modalities involved.

More broadly, multi-modal binding strategies that map heterogeneous sensory inputs into a unified embedding space represent a promising direction for achieving general-purpose perception models. These approaches draw conceptual inspiration from the demonstrated success of language-anchored alignment in the vision-language domain, as seen in contrastive pretraining frameworks [23], and extend this paradigm to modalities that have historically received less attention in large-scale pretraining. The generative language modeling perspective, where diverse inputs are treated as different "languages" to be processed within a unified transformer framework, also informs these designs [13].

The combination of frozen language encoders with lightweight adaptation modules offers a parameter-efficient path toward multi-modality pretraining [94], suggesting that the semantic knowledge encoded in large pretrained language models can serve as a powerful initialization point for binding additional modalities without catastrophic forgetting of linguistic representations. This parameter-efficiency is particularly valuable given the data scarcity challenges that characterize non-visual modality pairs, and it connects naturally to the adaptation strategies explored in 3D VLP settings where limited annotated data similarly motivates lightweight fine-tuning approaches. Collectively, these approaches indicate that multi-modality language pretraining is transitioning from a niche research area toward a foundational paradigm for building perception systems capable of understanding the full richness of the physical world.

### 6.4 3D Scene Understanding and Navigation-Oriented Pretraining

## 6.4 3D Spatial Understanding and Embodied Navigation Pretraining

The preceding section examined the extension of vision-language pretraining to diverse sensory modalities, including audio, depth, and infrared signals, with language serving as a unifying semantic anchor. A closely related but distinct frontier involves extending VLP to three-dimensional spatial understanding and embodied navigation—settings where models must not only align language with visual signals, but also reason about geometric structure, spatial relationships, and navigational affordances in continuous three-dimensional environments. Unlike standard image-text pretraining, 3D VLP must contend with sparse and irregular point cloud representations, the complexity of spatial reasoning across multiple viewpoints, and the difficulty of grounding natural language descriptions in continuous three-dimensional environments. These challenges complement those encountered in multi-modality binding, as both directions push beyond the well-studied 2D image-text paradigm toward richer, more physically grounded representations.

**3D Navigation-Oriented Pretraining.** Embodied navigation tasks require agents to interpret natural language instructions and navigate through complex environments using spatial perception. BEVBert addresses this by leveraging bird's-eye-view (BEV) representations as a structured intermediate spatial abstraction for navigation pretraining. By projecting 3D scene information into a top-down planar view, BEVBert enables the model to reason about object layouts, distances, and navigational paths more effectively than perspective-view approaches alone. This BEV-centric pretraining strategy aligns language instructions with spatial maps, allowing the model to generalize across unseen environments during vision-and-language navigation tasks.

**Point Cloud-Text Alignment.** Text4Point tackles the specific challenge of aligning natural language descriptions with raw 3D point cloud data, which poses unique difficulties due to the unstructured and variable-density nature of point clouds. By designing pretraining objectives that bridge textual semantics with 3D geometric features, Text4Point enables language-guided understanding of three-dimensional scenes, supporting downstream tasks such as 3D object recognition and scene classification. The core challenge is that point clouds lack the regular grid structure of 2D images, requiring specialized encoders and alignment strategies to map textual tokens onto spatially distributed 3D points.

**Unified 3D Vision-Language Pretraining.** 3DVLP extends the VLP paradigm to jointly encode 3D visual representations and language within a unified framework, enabling tasks such as 3D visual question answering, 3D object grounding, and scene captioning. These methods typically combine point cloud encoders such as PointNet or sparse 3D convolutional networks with transformer-based language models, and introduce pretraining objectives analogous to their 2D counterparts, including masked point modeling and 3D region-text alignment. The sparsity of 3D supervision signals and limited availability of annotated 3D scene-text datasets remain key bottlenecks distinguishing 3D VLP from its well-resourced 2D counterpart.

**Viewpoint Invariance and Cross-Viewpoint Alignment.** A complementary challenge to full 3D understanding is the sensitivity of standard VLP models to viewpoint variation in 2D imagery. Omniview-Tuning addresses this limitation by constructing the MVCap dataset, a large-scale collection of multi-view image-caption pairs, and introducing cross-viewpoint alignment objectives that train VLP models to produce consistent representations of the same scene captured from different perspectives. The cross-viewpoint alignment is enforced through a minimax optimization strategy that encourages viewpoint-invariant features while discouraging modality-specific shortcuts. This approach improves the robustness of VLP models on tasks requiring spatial and viewpoint understanding without requiring full 3D data.

**Common Challenges and Future Directions.** Across all 3D and navigation-oriented VLP approaches, several common challenges persist. The scarcity of large-scale aligned 3D scene-text datasets limits the scale of pretraining compared to web-crawled 2D image-text corpora—a data bottleneck that parallels the challenges of multi-modal alignment discussed in the preceding section. Efficient encoders for irregular 3D structures, such as sparse transformers and graph neural networks, are still maturing. Furthermore, grounding compositional language referring to spatial relations—such as "the chair to the left of the table"—remains substantially harder in 3D than in 2D contexts. These spatial grounding demands resonate with the fine-grained localization challenges that arise in the medical VLP domain discussed in the following section, where precise anatomical grounding similarly requires moving beyond coarse global alignment. Addressing the challenges of 3D VLP will require tighter integration of geometric reasoning, spatial commonsense knowledge, and scalable 3D data collection pipelines, pointing toward a rich avenue for future research at the intersection of embodied AI and vision-language pretraining.

### 6.5 Domain-Specific Medical Vision-Language Pretraining

## 6.5 Domain-Specific Medical Vision-Language Pretraining

The medical domain presents unique and formidable challenges for vision-language pretraining (VLP), including severe data scarcity due to privacy regulations, the high cost of expert annotation, the heterogeneous nature of multimodal clinical data (encompassing radiology images, pathology slides, electrocardiograms, and clinical reports), and the critical requirement for precise semantic alignment that has direct patient safety implications. These characteristics have motivated a growing body of research that adapts and extends general VLP frameworks—including the parameter-efficient and instruction-tuned approaches discussed in the preceding section—to the specialized requirements of clinical and biomedical settings. Indeed, as will become clear throughout this section, many of the adaptation strategies developed for general-domain VLP, such as prompt tuning, adapter-based modality bridging, and federated optimization, find particularly compelling applications in the medical context where data scarcity and privacy constraints make full fine-tuning especially problematic.

### Hierarchical and Structured Report-Guided Pretraining

Clinical reports are inherently structured documents, typically containing a "findings" section that describes observable image characteristics and an "impressions" section that summarizes conclusive diagnoses. Most early medical VLP approaches treated these reports as flat text, thereby missing the opportunity to leverage their natural hierarchical organization. IMITATE [53] addresses this limitation by proposing a clinical prior-guided VLP framework that explicitly models the hierarchical structure of medical reports. The framework derives multi-level visual features from chest X-ray (CXR) images and separately aligns these features with the descriptive and conclusive text components encoded from the hierarchical report structure. Additionally, IMITATE introduces a clinical-informed contrastive loss that incorporates clinical prior knowledge when formulating sample correlations during contrastive learning. This approach demonstrates superior performance over baseline VLP methods across six datasets spanning five distinct medical imaging downstream tasks, underscoring the importance of structure-aware pretraining for clinical data.

### Unifying Fusion-Encoder and Dual-Encoder Paradigms in Medical VLP

In general-domain VLP, two predominant architectural paradigms exist: fusion-encoder models, which enable rich cross-modal interaction through a joint multimodal transformer, and dual-encoder models, which independently encode each modality for efficient retrieval and unimodal tasks. Medical VLP inherits the same fundamental tension between these paradigms. PTUnifier [95] proposes an effective scheme to bridge these two paradigms by unifying their input format through the introduction of visual and textual soft prompts. These prompts function as a representative feature bank that stores the most salient images or texts, enabling a single foundational model to accommodate diverse task formats—including image-only, text-only, and image-text-pair inputs—without architectural modifications. PTUnifier further constructs a dynamic prompt pool rather than relying on static prompts, thereby improving diversity and scalability. Experimental results demonstrate state-of-the-art performance across a broad spectrum of tasks, spanning unimodal classification and text summarization, cross-modal retrieval and generation, and multimodal visual question answering. Importantly, the prompt-based unification strategy is orthogonal to most existing medical VLP approaches, making it a broadly applicable and complementary extension. This use of soft prompts to unify task formats echoes the prompt tuning philosophy discussed in the parameter-efficient adaptation section, here repurposed to address the distinctive multi-paradigm challenge inherent to medical VLP.

### Multi-Modality Binding in the Medical Domain

The clinical environment naturally involves data from diverse sensory and diagnostic modalities beyond standard image-text pairs, including electrocardiograms (ECGs), laboratory results, and structured clinical notes. Extending VLP to handle such tri-modal or multi-modal clinical data poses significant technical challenges related to heterogeneous data distributions and the absence of large-scale aligned multi-modal corpora. MEDBind addresses this challenge by binding three distinct clinical modalities—chest X-rays, ECGs, and clinical texts—within a unified contrastive pretraining framework, enabling cross-modal retrieval and representation learning across this expanded clinical modality space. This tri-modality approach reflects the broader trend toward more holistic multimodal clinical models that mirror the multi-source nature of real-world clinical decision-making, and parallels the general-domain efforts to bind diverse modalities discussed in earlier sections of this survey.

### Grounded and Organ-Level Medical VLP

Fine-grained spatial grounding is particularly critical in medical imaging, where the precise localization of pathology to specific anatomical structures (e.g., organs or lesions) is essential for clinical utility. CT-GLIP advances this direction by proposing organ-level grounded language-image pretraining specifically designed for CT scans, enabling the model to associate textual descriptions with specific anatomical regions rather than performing only global image-text alignment. This organ-level grounding capability directly supports downstream tasks such as report generation and abnormality detection at the structure level. Complementarily, GTGM explores generative text-guided approaches for 3D medical image segmentation, leveraging language descriptions to guide the segmentation of volumetric medical images in a generative framework. Together, these works highlight the importance of incorporating spatial and structural grounding objectives into medical VLP to move beyond coarse image-level semantics—a concern that resonates with the 3D and spatial grounding challenges discussed in the preceding subsection on navigation and point-cloud pretraining.

### Dynamic Prompting and Multi-Task Medical VLP

A central challenge in medical VLP is enabling a single pretrained model to generalize across a diverse array of clinical tasks—including classification, segmentation, retrieval, and report generation—that may have heterogeneous input formats and output spaces. This challenge mirrors the general-domain motivation for instruction tuning frameworks such as InstructBLIP and Ziya-Visual, which similarly aim to unify diverse task formats within a single model. UniDCP addresses this challenge in the medical setting through the introduction of dynamic cross-modal learnable prompts that adapt flexibly across multiple medical tasks, enabling a unified pretraining framework that serves diverse clinical applications without task-specific architectural modifications. This dynamic prompting strategy allows the model to condition its representations on task-relevant signals, improving both flexibility and specialization within a shared parameter space.

### Unified 2D and 3D Medical Image Representation

A critical gap in standard medical VLP approaches is their restriction to a single imaging modality dimension—typically 2D radiographs—despite the prevalence of volumetric 3D imaging modalities such as CT and MRI in clinical practice. UniMedI addresses this gap by proposing a unified VLP framework that jointly learns representations from both 2D and 3D medical images alongside corresponding clinical text. By bridging the 2D-3D representation gap within a single model, UniMedI enables more comprehensive medical image understanding that reflects the full diversity of clinical imaging modalities encountered in practice. This 2D-3D unification challenge within the medical domain is conceptually related to the broader 3D VLP challenges discussed earlier, but is distinguished by the additional constraints of sparse annotated clinical data and the structured semantics of anatomical regions.

### Federated Learning for Privacy-Preserving Medical VLP

Beyond architectural and objective innovations, the strict data privacy regulations governing medical data present a fundamental barrier to centralizing large-scale pretraining corpora. Federated learning offers a promising paradigm for scaling medical VLP while preserving patient privacy by distributing training across institutions without sharing raw data. FedRGB [96] addresses the specific challenge of heterogeneous client data distributions in federated biomedical VLP, proposing a federated distributional robust guidance-based learning framework that employs guidance-based local training to reduce feature distortions and distribution-based min-max optimization to learn unbiased cross-modal alignment. Experimental results demonstrate that FedRGB successfully enables effective federated multimodal learning for biomedical VLP under realistic data heterogeneity conditions, representing an important step toward clinically deployable privacy-preserving medical foundation models. Notably, this federated approach directly addresses one of the key bottlenecks that distinguishes medical VLP from its general-domain counterpart: the inability to pool large-scale training data across institutions due to regulatory constraints.

### Synthesis and Open Challenges

The reviewed works collectively illustrate that medical VLP requires domain-specific adaptations that go substantially beyond applying general-purpose pretraining frameworks to clinical data. Key challenges that remain include: (1) the extreme scarcity of large-scale, high-quality, and well-aligned multi-modal clinical datasets; (2) the heterogeneous nature of medical imaging modalities (2D X-rays, 3D CT volumes, MRI sequences, pathology slides, ECGs); (3) the critical need for fine-grained anatomical grounding and structure-aware alignment; (4) privacy constraints that restrict data centralization; and (5) the high stakes of clinical deployment that demand rigorous evaluation of safety, fairness, and robustness. Future progress will likely require combining hierarchical and structured report supervision [53], flexible multi-paradigm architectural unification [95], multi-modal binding across diverse clinical data streams, organ-level spatial grounding, and privacy-preserving federated pretraining [96] to realize the transformative potential of medical vision-language pretraining in real-world clinical settings. Taken together, the medical VLP landscape thus serves as both a demanding test bed for the general adaptation strategies surveyed in this chapter and a compelling source of domain-driven innovations that continue to enrich the broader VLP research agenda.

### 6.6 Parameter-Efficient Adaptation, Prompt Tuning, and Instruction Tuning

## 6.6 Parameter-Efficient Adaptation and Instruction Tuning

As pretrained VLP models grow increasingly large, fine-tuning all parameters for each downstream task becomes computationally prohibitive. This has motivated a rich line of research into parameter-efficient adaptation strategies that modify only a small subset of parameters—or introduce lightweight trainable modules—while keeping the bulk of the pretrained model frozen. These approaches span prompt tuning, adapter-based methods, and instruction tuning frameworks, each offering distinct mechanisms for transferring pretrained multimodal knowledge to diverse downstream applications. As will become evident, these strategies are not merely engineering conveniences; they represent a principled response to the core tension between preserving broadly learned representations and enabling targeted task-specific specialization—a tension that recurs throughout the VLP landscape, from the medical domain adaptations discussed in the preceding section to the continual learning challenges taken up in the following one.

### Prompt Tuning Approaches

Prompt tuning has emerged as a compelling paradigm for adapting VLP models without extensive parameter updates. Rather than modifying model weights, prompt-based methods introduce learnable tokens or structured input reformulations that steer pretrained models toward task-specific behaviors. Among these, CPT (Colorful Prompt Tuning) reformulates visual grounding as a colorful fill-in-the-blank problem, encoding spatial and referential information through color-based visual prompts that can be processed by standard VLP architectures without any architectural modification. This approach elegantly sidesteps the need for region-level supervision while achieving strong grounding performance. PEVL extends this direction by incorporating explicit object position modeling directly into the prompt tuning process, enabling position-sensitive reasoning in VLP models that were originally trained without fine-grained spatial supervision. By encoding bounding box coordinates as structured textual tokens within learnable prompts, PEVL bridges the gap between coarse image-level pretraining and precise region-level downstream tasks. APoLLo further combines the complementary strengths of adapter modules and prompt learning within a unified framework, demonstrating that jointly optimizing both forms of lightweight adaptation leads to better generalization than either approach alone. Such combinations reflect a broader recognition that prompt tuning and adapter learning are not mutually exclusive but can be synergistically integrated.

The literature on prompt learning for vision-language models has also been significantly shaped by work on context-aware and hierarchically structured prompts. For instance, approaches that leverage large language models to generate explicit context descriptions for prompt initialization, combined with alignment objectives between learned prompts and local image features, have shown strong performance on few-shot recognition and base-to-new generalization benchmarks [97]. Similarly, hierarchical prompt tuning methods that model structured linguistic knowledge—including entity relationships and attribute correlations—have demonstrated superior generalization compared to conventional flat prompt designs [98]. These works collectively underscore the importance of incorporating structured semantic knowledge into prompt learning to move beyond simple context vector optimization. Notably, this use of soft and structured prompts to encode task-relevant information connects directly to the PTUnifier framework discussed in the preceding medical VLP section, where prompt-based unification served to bridge diverse clinical task formats within a single model.

### Adapter-Based Modality-Bridging Methods

Adapter-based approaches introduce small trainable bottleneck modules into the layers of a frozen pretrained model, enabling task-specific adaptation with a fraction of the full fine-tuning cost. A prominent example in the VLP context is MAGMA, which augments a frozen generative language model with visual adapters that map image features into the language model's embedding space. By keeping the language model weights fixed and training only the visual adapter components alongside a visual encoder, MAGMA achieves efficient multimodal fusion while preserving the strong generative capabilities of the underlying language model. This modality-bridging philosophy—connecting independently pretrained visual and language towers through lightweight trainable interfaces—has proven especially effective when high-quality frozen language models are available, as it avoids catastrophic forgetting of linguistic knowledge during multimodal adaptation. Crucially, this same concern with preserving pretrained knowledge while enabling targeted updates will resurface as a central design principle in the continual learning frameworks discussed in the following section.

Token-wise adaptive prompt learning methods such as APLe [99] further exemplify the adapter philosophy in the prompt learning context, employing sequential modality-specific training to efficiently adapt both vision and language branches of models like CLIP. By treating prompts as tokens and adapting them in a layerwise, modality-aware fashion, APLe achieves robust generalization performance while maintaining parameter efficiency.

### Instruction Tuning Frameworks

Instruction tuning represents a more recent and powerful paradigm for adapting large pretrained VLP models, wherein models are fine-tuned on diverse collections of task instructions expressed in natural language, enabling strong zero-shot and few-shot generalization to novel tasks at inference time. BLIP-2 exemplifies this direction by introducing a lightweight Querying Transformer (Q-Former) that bridges a frozen image encoder and a frozen large language model. The Q-Former learns to extract the most task-relevant visual features from the image encoder and translate them into a fixed number of query representations that the language model can directly consume. By training only the Q-Former while keeping both the image encoder and language model frozen, BLIP-2 achieves highly parameter-efficient multimodal pretraining that scales gracefully to ever-larger language models. This design philosophy is particularly significant given the rapid growth in language model capacity, as it allows VLP systems to leverage state-of-the-art language models without the prohibitive cost of full joint pretraining.

Building on BLIP-2's foundation, InstructBLIP extends the Q-Former architecture with instruction-awareness, training the querying transformer on a diverse collection of vision-language tasks reformulated as natural language instructions. Crucially, InstructBLIP feeds the task instruction not only to the language model but also directly into the Q-Former, enabling the query representations to be dynamically conditioned on the specific instruction at hand. This instruction-aware feature extraction allows the model to selectively attend to image regions and features most relevant to the current task, yielding substantial improvements over standard BLIP-2 on held-out zero-shot benchmarks. The instruction tuning paradigm thus moves beyond static visual feature extraction toward dynamic, task-conditioned visual understanding—a capacity that directly motivates the dynamic prompting approach of UniDCP in the medical VLP domain, where similar task-conditioning mechanisms were applied to the clinical setting.

Ziya-Visual applies multi-task instruction tuning in a bilingual (Chinese-English) setting, demonstrating that instruction-tuned VLP models can support sophisticated vision-language dialogue across languages. By constructing diverse bilingual instruction datasets spanning a wide range of visual understanding and reasoning tasks, Ziya-Visual achieves strong performance on both Chinese and English vision-language benchmarks, highlighting the complementary relationship between multilingual capabilities and instruction-following generalization.

### Broader Significance and Connections

The convergence of prompt tuning, adapter learning, and instruction tuning reflects a fundamental shift in how the community approaches VLP adaptation. Rather than viewing pretraining and fine-tuning as a monolithic pipeline requiring full parameter updates at each stage, these parameter-efficient methods recognize that large pretrained models encode rich reusable knowledge that should be preserved rather than overwritten. This perspective aligns closely with the findings of probing studies [56] that reveal the rich linguistic and semantic knowledge encoded in pretrained VLP models, and with diagnostic evaluations [55] showing that standard downstream fine-tuning can obscure important distinctions between models' genuine multimodal capabilities. By developing more targeted and interpretable adaptation strategies, parameter-efficient methods offer not only computational benefits but also improved transparency into what pretrained models know and how adaptation modifies their behavior.

Moreover, parameter-efficient adaptation is particularly critical in resource-constrained settings and specialized domains such as medicine [52], where labeled data is scarce, privacy constraints limit data sharing, and full fine-tuning of billion-parameter models is computationally infeasible. The development of robust, lightweight adaptation frameworks thus represents a key enabler for democratizing VLP across diverse application domains and deployment contexts. Equally important, the same design principles that underpin parameter efficiency in static adaptation settings—preserving pretrained representations while enabling selective updates—translate naturally into the continual learning regime explored in the following section, where the challenge shifts from adapting to a fixed new task to accumulating knowledge across an ongoing, non-stationary stream of multimodal data.

### 6.7 Continual and Lifelong Vision-Language Pretraining

## 6.7 Continual and Lifelong Vision-Language Pretraining

The parameter-efficient adaptation strategies discussed in the preceding section—prompt tuning, adapter learning, and instruction tuning—primarily address the challenge of transferring static pretrained knowledge to downstream tasks. However, a complementary and equally pressing challenge arises when the underlying data distribution itself is non-stationary: real-world multimodal data is continuously expanding, with new images, captions, and domain-specific content generated at an ever-increasing rate. This fundamental mismatch between static offline pretraining and dynamic real-world data streams gives rise to the emerging challenge of continual vision-language pretraining—the problem of enabling VLP models to accumulate knowledge from sequentially arriving data without suffering catastrophic forgetting of previously learned representations.

The dominant paradigm in vision-language pretraining (VLP) assumes that training data is collected offline in a static, fixed corpus before model training begins. Catastrophic forgetting, a well-known challenge in continual learning, is particularly pronounced in large-scale multimodal models due to the intricate interdependencies between visual and linguistic representations that are established during pretraining. When a pretrained VLP model is updated on new data streams, gradient updates that improve performance on recent examples can destructively overwrite the feature alignments and semantic correspondences learned from earlier data. This makes naive sequential fine-tuning on non-stationary multimodal data streams an unreliable strategy for maintaining broad generalization capabilities.

To address these challenges, the CTP (Continual Vision-Language Pretraining) framework has been proposed as a principled approach to lifelong multimodal learning. CTP introduces two complementary mechanisms: compatible momentum contrast and topology preservation. The compatible momentum contrast mechanism extends momentum-based contrastive learning to the continual setting by maintaining a dynamically updated memory bank that preserves cross-modal alignment representations from previous data streams, enabling the model to contrast new image-text pairs against a broader distribution of historical examples without requiring access to previously seen raw data. The topology preservation component enforces structural consistency in the learned embedding space across sequential pretraining stages, penalizing deformations in the relational geometry of multimodal representations that would otherwise degrade knowledge accumulated from earlier data distributions.

Complementing the CTP framework, the P9D benchmark dataset has been designed specifically to evaluate continual multimodal learning in industrial domains. P9D spans nine distinct industrial domains and provides a structured sequence of domain-specific image-text data intended to simulate the non-stationary data streams encountered in practical deployment scenarios. By covering diverse manufacturing, inspection, and technical domains, P9D offers a rigorous testbed for measuring both forward transfer—the ability to leverage prior knowledge when learning new domains—and backward transfer, i.e., the degree to which learning new domains degrades performance on previously mastered ones. This domain-structured evaluation protocol provides more realistic and diagnostically informative assessments of continual VLP capabilities than conventional static benchmarks.

The broader challenge of extending standard offline VLP paradigms to handle ever-expanding real-world data touches on several interconnected research dimensions. From a data management perspective, continual pretraining requires strategies for efficient memory replay, selective data retention, and importance-weighted sampling that prioritize the most informative or representative historical examples. From an architectural perspective, modular and expandable designs that can incorporate new modality-specific components or task-specific parameters without disrupting shared representations offer a promising direction. From an optimization perspective, gradient episodic memory, elastic weight consolidation, and knowledge distillation approaches adapted to the multimodal setting can help constrain parameter updates to preserve critical cross-modal alignment capabilities.

The intersection of continual learning with the large-scale pretraining paradigms characteristic of modern VLP models also raises important questions of computational tractability. Retraining large VLP models from scratch on each new data increment is prohibitively expensive, motivating research into parameter-efficient continual pretraining strategies that update only a small subset of model parameters while freezing representations critical to previously learned tasks. Approaches analogous to adapter-based fine-tuning and prefix tuning, which have demonstrated effectiveness in static adaptation settings [2], may serve as natural foundations for efficient continual adaptation. Similarly, the use of lightweight connector modules that bridge frozen unimodal encoders to updated cross-modal fusion components, as explored in parameter-efficient VLP architectures [11], could enable selective knowledge accumulation without full model retraining. This connection between parameter-efficient adaptation and continual learning is particularly significant: the same design principles that motivate adapters and prompt tuning for static task transfer—namely, preserving pretrained knowledge while enabling targeted updates—translate naturally into mechanisms for resisting catastrophic forgetting in sequential learning scenarios.

The evaluation of continual VLP models presents its own methodological challenges. Standard downstream benchmarks such as VQA, image-text retrieval, and visual grounding evaluate a model at a single snapshot in time, failing to capture the dynamic nature of continual learning trajectories. Frameworks like VLUE [57], which emphasize generalization to out-of-distribution test distributions, offer partial but incomplete coverage of the continual learning scenario. Dedicated evaluation protocols that track performance across sequential pretraining stages, measure forgetting rates, and assess forward transfer efficiency are needed to drive progress in this area.

In summary, continual and lifelong vision-language pretraining represents an important and underexplored frontier in multimodal AI research. As real-world deployments increasingly require VLP models to adapt to growing and shifting data distributions across diverse domains, the development of robust, efficient, and forgetting-resistant continual pretraining frameworks will be essential for sustainable and scalable multimodal intelligence. Together with the parameter-efficient adaptation methods discussed previously, these approaches collectively point toward a future in which VLP models are not merely static artifacts trained once on fixed corpora, but dynamic, evolving systems capable of lifelong multimodal learning.

## 7 Challenges, Limitations, and Future Directions

### 7.1 Computational Efficiency and Scalability

## 7.1 Computational Efficiency and Scalability

Training and deploying large-scale vision-language pretraining (VLP) models presents formidable computational challenges that have become increasingly prominent as the field advances toward ever-larger architectures and web-scale datasets. While the preceding discussion highlights how modality bias and cross-modal alignment failures constrain the representational quality of VLP models, a complementary and equally pressing set of concerns revolves around whether such models can be trained and deployed at all within realistic computational budgets. The fundamental tension between model capacity, training data scale, and computational feasibility represents one of the most pressing practical concerns in modern VLP research.

**High Computational Costs of Large-Scale Pretraining.** The dominant paradigm of pretraining on hundreds of millions or billions of image-text pairs, as exemplified by models trained on massive web-crawled corpora [1], demands enormous computational resources in terms of both hardware and training time. These costs are compounded by the complexity of multimodal architectures that must simultaneously process visual features, textual tokens, and their cross-modal interactions. Early VLP models relying on region-based visual features from pretrained object detectors—where visual feature extraction alone could require more computation than subsequent multimodal interaction steps—were particularly inefficient [37]. The introduction of patch-based Vision Transformers partially alleviated this bottleneck, but the overall pretraining compute burden remains substantial for state-of-the-art models.

**Slow Convergence in Masked Language Modeling.** Masked language modeling (MLM), a cornerstone pretraining objective inherited from BERT-style language models, has well-documented convergence inefficiencies in the vision-language setting. Because standard MLM couples the prediction rate with the corruption rate, models must process a large number of training steps before the signal becomes sufficiently informative to drive meaningful representation learning. This slow convergence directly translates into longer pretraining times and higher computational expenditure. Various works have sought to accelerate this process through modified masking strategies, saliency-aware masking, and objective reformulations that improve training signal density per step [32].

**The Model Size Versus Efficiency Trade-Off.** A recurring tension in VLP is the trade-off between model capacity and deployment efficiency. While scaling model parameters and training data generally yields improved downstream performance [1], larger models incur prohibitive inference latency and memory requirements that hinder practical deployment. This trade-off is particularly acute in real-time retrieval scenarios, where fusion-based models with deep cross-modal interaction achieve strong accuracy but suffer from extremely slow inference [100]. The dual-encoder paradigm addresses inference efficiency by enabling offline index construction but often sacrifices the fine-grained cross-modal alignment quality achievable through deeper fusion architectures.

**Knowledge Distillation for Model Compression.** One of the most practically impactful approaches to addressing the efficiency gap is knowledge distillation, which transfers the capabilities of large pretrained VLP models into smaller, deployable student models. Systematic distillation frameworks specifically designed for VLP models have demonstrated that careful attention to architecture characteristics of different modules and modality-specific information transfer can yield highly compressed models that retain competitive performance [101]. Such approaches can achieve substantial parameter reductions—for example, compressing models by nearly half while maintaining or improving downstream task performance on image-text retrieval, image captioning, and visual question answering [101]. The challenge lies in identifying which layers and which cross-modal interaction patterns are most critical to preserve during compression, as multimodal distillation presents unique difficulties compared to unimodal model compression.

**Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning.** Beyond pretraining efficiency, the cost of fine-tuning large VLP models for each downstream task has motivated extensive research into parameter-efficient adaptation strategies. Rather than updating all model parameters during fine-tuning, methods such as adapter layers, prompt tuning, and prefix tuning freeze the majority of pretrained weights and introduce only a small number of trainable parameters. These approaches have proven particularly valuable in enabling large VLP models to be adapted to specialized domains and tasks without the computational burden of full fine-tuning. Soft prompt-based unification schemes, for instance, demonstrate that a single foundation model can serve multiple task formats—image-only, text-only, and image-text pairs—through parameter-efficient prompt conditioning rather than task-specific architectural modifications [95].

**Multi-Task and Unified Training for Amortized Efficiency.** An orthogonal strategy for improving efficiency is amortizing the computational cost of pretraining across multiple tasks through unified multi-task learning. Rather than training separate models for each downstream task, multi-task training regimes that jointly optimize across diverse vision-language objectives can dramatically reduce the total number of parameters required while achieving comparable or superior performance [3]. This approach reduces the aggregate computational cost of deploying VLP capabilities across a suite of applications, from visual question answering and image captioning to visual grounding and multi-modal verification.

**Efficient Architectures and Token Reduction.** Architectural innovations aimed at reducing the computational complexity of VLP models represent another active research front. The observation that visual inputs, particularly patch-based representations, often contain significant redundancy has motivated approaches that prune or merge tokens during inference or even during training. By progressively reducing the number of visual tokens that must be processed through expensive cross-modal attention layers, such methods can substantially accelerate both training and inference. Lightweight connector modules, such as the Querying Transformer (Q-Former) approach that bridges frozen unimodal encoders through a small set of learned query tokens, demonstrate that effective cross-modal alignment can be achieved with dramatically fewer trainable parameters and computational operations compared to full end-to-end pretraining.

**Hierarchical and Coarse-to-Fine Retrieval.** For deployment scenarios requiring real-time inference at scale, hierarchical retrieval strategies offer a pragmatic path toward efficiency. By employing low-dimensional representations for large-scale coarse candidate filtering followed by high-dimensional representations for precise re-ranking of a small candidate set, such frameworks can achieve inference speeds orders of magnitude faster than fusion-based models while maintaining strong retrieval accuracy [100]. This coarse-to-fine paradigm effectively decouples the efficiency requirements of large-scale index search from the accuracy requirements of fine-grained cross-modal matching.

**Outlook.** Despite significant progress, computational efficiency remains a fundamental bottleneck for the broader adoption of VLP. Taken together with the modality bias challenges discussed previously, these efficiency constraints underscore that advancing VLP requires simultaneous progress on representational quality and computational tractability—neither dimension can be addressed in isolation. The field requires continued innovation across the full stack—from more data-efficient pretraining objectives and architectures that converge faster with less data, to compression and distillation techniques that maintain multimodal capabilities in compact models, to hardware-aware design choices that minimize memory bandwidth and arithmetic operations during inference. Balancing the competing demands of scale, performance, and efficiency will be critical to making VLP technology accessible beyond well-resourced research labs and enabling its deployment in real-world applications with stringent latency and resource constraints [57].

### 7.2 Modality Bias and Cross-Modal Alignment Limitations

## 7.2 Modality Bias and Cross-Modal Alignment Failures

Modality bias represents one of the most persistent and fundamental challenges in vision-language pretraining (VLP), undermining the genuine multimodal understanding that these models aspire to achieve. While the preceding discussion of computational efficiency highlights practical constraints on scaling VLP models, modality bias poses a more subtle yet equally consequential threat to their representational quality. At its core, modality bias refers to the tendency of VLP models to disproportionately rely on one modality—typically language—while underutilizing or misaligning the complementary information present in the other modality. This issue manifests in several interrelated forms that collectively limit the robustness and reliability of pretrained multimodal systems.

**Focal Bias and Information Disparity**

A primary source of modality bias in VLP stems from the inherent information asymmetry between visual and textual inputs. Natural language descriptions are often richer in structured semantic content, providing explicit relational and compositional cues, while visual representations tend to encode holistic scene-level features that are more ambiguous without accompanying textual context. This disparity creates what can be termed "focal bias," where models learn to anchor their predictions primarily on linguistic priors rather than engaging in genuine cross-modal reasoning. For instance, in visual question answering tasks, models frequently exploit dataset-level statistical biases in question phrasing to produce correct answers without actually examining the paired image content. Such shortcuts reflect the model's failure to develop true visually-grounded language understanding [2; 10].

**Linguistic Shortcut Learning**

The large-scale noisy image-text datasets commonly used for VLP pretraining—often sourced from web-crawled alt-text—exacerbate linguistic shortcut tendencies. Because textual descriptions in web data frequently fail to capture fine-grained visual details, models trained on such data learn to rely heavily on language-level co-occurrence statistics rather than developing semantically grounded visual associations [2]. This phenomenon is particularly problematic in downstream tasks requiring precise visual grounding, spatial reasoning, or attribute recognition, where language alone is insufficient to disambiguate competing hypotheses. Comprehensive surveys of VLP approaches have consistently highlighted this gap between the statistical co-occurrence patterns captured during pretraining and the genuine visual reasoning required for robust downstream performance [10; 11].

**Limitations in Global Semantic Alignment**

Beyond token-level or object-level correspondences, VLP models face significant difficulties in establishing coherent global semantic alignment across modalities. Most pretraining objectives—including masked language modeling (MLM) and image-text matching (ITM)—primarily encourage local, region-word level correspondences, which does not necessarily generalize to understanding scene-level semantics, event descriptions, or abstract relational concepts [2; 11]. The resulting models tend to perform well on tasks that require identifying salient objects or matching keyword-level descriptions, but struggle when presented with holistic scene understanding challenges or nuanced semantic distinctions. Multi-task training paradigms, such as those explored in the 12-in-1 framework [3], have demonstrated that jointly training on diverse vision-language objectives can partially mitigate these alignment limitations by exposing models to a broader range of cross-modal correspondence signals.

**Probing Studies and Benchmark Revelations**

Diagnostic benchmarks and probing studies have played a crucial role in surfacing the extent of modality bias and cross-modal alignment failures in VLP models. Evaluations targeting attribute understanding, relational reasoning, and compositional language comprehension consistently reveal that state-of-the-art VLP models exhibit pronounced weaknesses that aggregate accuracy metrics on standard benchmarks tend to obscure [10; 11]. Models that achieve high performance on VQA or image-text retrieval benchmarks frequently fail on carefully constructed diagnostic tasks that require distinguishing subtle visual differences, correctly attributing properties to specific objects, or reasoning about spatial and relational configurations. These findings suggest that existing pretraining objectives and data distributions are insufficient to instill the depth of cross-modal understanding needed for robust generalization.

**Challenges in Attribute, Relation, and Compositional Understanding**

One of the most revealing dimensions of modality bias is the systematic underperformance of VLP models on attribute binding, relational reasoning, and compositional concept recognition. When presented with prompts that require differentiating "a red cube next to a blue sphere" from "a blue cube next to a red sphere," most existing VLP models exhibit near-chance performance, suggesting they process visual-linguistic descriptions as bags of concepts rather than structured compositional expressions [15; 102]. This bag-of-words behavior, inherited partly from contrastive pretraining objectives that encourage global image-text alignment without explicit compositional structure, significantly limits the applicability of VLP models to tasks requiring precise grounding of adjective-noun bindings or multi-step relational inference.

**Toward Addressing Modality Bias**

Addressing modality bias and cross-modal alignment limitations requires innovations across multiple dimensions of the VLP pipeline. Architectural designs that enforce more symmetric and fine-grained cross-modal interaction—such as co-attention mechanisms and multi-layer bridge connections—partially alleviate the problem by reducing the model's ability to bypass visual processing [10]. Pretraining objectives that explicitly reward compositional grounding, dense region-word alignment, and position-sensitive understanding offer complementary benefits. Additionally, the construction of higher-quality pretraining datasets with richer and more accurate textual annotations—potentially through LLM-augmented captioning or structured knowledge integration—can reduce the degree to which models learn spurious language-visual correlations from noisy web data [11; 2]. Nevertheless, modality bias remains an open and critical challenge, and its resolution will likely require coordinated advances in data, objectives, architecture, and evaluation methodology. Importantly, even as modality bias is addressed through improved cross-modal alignment, a complementary set of visual shortcomings persists at the level of the visual representations themselves—most notably in the form of fine-grained perceptual failures that characterize contrastive models, as examined in the following section.

### 7.3 Visual Shortcomings and CLIP-Blind Pairs

Despite the remarkable success of contrastive vision-language pretraining models such as CLIP, systematic visual shortcomings have been identified that reveal fundamental limitations in how these models perceive and represent fine-grained visual information. These limitations are closely intertwined with the modality bias challenges discussed previously, but manifest distinctly in the visual processing dimension rather than through language dominance alone. One of the most striking phenomena is that of CLIP-blind pairs—cases where visually distinct images that humans can easily differentiate are mapped to nearly identical or highly similar embedding representations by contrastive models. This failure mode arises from the nature of contrastive pretraining objectives, which optimize for global semantic alignment between image-text pairs rather than capturing subtle, fine-grained visual differences. As a result, models trained purely on contrastive objectives may collapse visually dissimilar images into the same region of the embedding space when those images share similar high-level semantic descriptions.

The consequences of this limitation are significant and far-reaching. Benchmarks such as the Multimodal Visual Patterns (MMVP) benchmark have been developed specifically to expose these failures, demonstrating that even state-of-the-art contrastive VLP models and multimodal large language models struggle with fine-grained visual pattern recognition tasks that should be well within the capabilities of a robust visual system. These findings reveal a concerning gap between the visual representations learned through contrastive pretraining and those learned by vision-only self-supervised methods. While self-supervised visual representation learning approaches—such as masked image modeling or self-distillation methods trained purely on image data—tend to develop richer and more spatially detailed representations, contrastive models trained with language supervision often sacrifice this fine-grained visual acuity in favor of semantic alignment with textual descriptions. Notably, this trade-off complements the alignment failures described in the context of modality bias: whereas modality bias reflects insufficient engagement with visual content relative to language, CLIP-blind pair failures reflect an impoverishment of the visual representations themselves, even when visual inputs are processed.

This representational gap has motivated researchers to explore remedies that integrate complementary visual learning signals. Among the most promising approaches is the Mixture of Features (MoF) paradigm, which proposes combining contrastive visual embeddings with features derived from vision-only self-supervised models. By mixing these complementary representations, MoF approaches aim to preserve the semantic alignment capabilities of contrastive pretraining while recovering the fine-grained visual detail that contrastive models tend to lose. This hybrid strategy reflects a broader recognition that no single pretraining objective is sufficient to capture all the dimensions of visual understanding required for robust multimodal reasoning—a theme that connects to the multi-objective and architectural solutions explored in response to cross-modal alignment limitations.

The existence of CLIP-blind pairs also has downstream implications for tasks such as image-text retrieval, visual question answering, and visual grounding, where fine-grained discrimination between visually similar scenes is essential. Models that exhibit this weakness may achieve strong aggregate benchmark scores while failing on specific fine-grained subsets, masking their true limitations. This observation reinforces the broader point that holistic accuracy metrics are insufficient for characterizing the true capabilities and failure modes of VLP models, and highlights the importance of diagnostic benchmarks and probing evaluations that go beyond such metrics. Furthermore, the gap between contrastive and self-supervised visual representations underscores the value of diverse pretraining objectives, as advocated by works that combine masked image modeling with vision-language alignment [70][13].

Addressing visual shortcomings in CLIP-based models remains an active and important research direction with direct implications for the adversarial robustness concerns examined in the following section, as impoverished visual representations constitute an additional vulnerability surface that adversarial perturbations can exploit. Future work may explore more expressive visual encoders, richer pretraining data with detailed captions that encourage fine-grained discrimination [4], and hybrid training objectives that jointly optimize for both global semantic alignment and local visual detail. Ultimately, closing the gap between contrastive visual representations and the richer spatial and structural understanding captured by vision-only self-supervised methods will be essential for building truly robust and visually grounded multimodal systems—systems that are resilient not only to modality bias and alignment failures, but also to the adversarial threats that increasingly characterize real-world deployment conditions.

### 7.4 Adversarial Robustness and Security Vulnerabilities

## 7.4 Adversarial Robustness

As vision-language pretraining (VLP) models are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, their adversarial robustness has emerged as a critical concern that follows naturally from the visual shortcomings examined in the previous subsection. The impoverished visual representations identified in CLIP-based models—including the CLIP-blind pair phenomenon—do not merely limit fine-grained visual understanding; they also constitute exploitable vulnerabilities that adversarial actors can actively leverage. Unlike unimodal models, VLP systems present an expanded attack surface, as adversaries can exploit weaknesses in either the visual modality, the textual modality, or their cross-modal alignment simultaneously.

**Multimodal Adversarial Attacks.** The joint nature of VLP models introduces unique vulnerabilities that multimodal adversarial attack strategies are specifically designed to exploit. Co-Attack, for instance, simultaneously perturbs both the image and text inputs to craft adversarial examples that are more effective than single-modality attacks, exploiting the tight coupling between visual and textual representations in models trained with contrastive objectives. Patch Representation Misalignment (PRM) attacks target the cross-modal alignment mechanism directly, introducing carefully crafted visual patches that cause the model to produce misaligned representations, thereby degrading performance on downstream tasks such as image-text retrieval and visual question answering. These multimodal attack strategies reveal that the cross-modal fusion mechanisms central to VLP—while enabling powerful joint reasoning—also create new pathways for adversarial exploitation that do not exist in unimodal settings.

**Transferability of Adversarial Examples.** A particularly concerning property of adversarial attacks on VLP models is their transferability across different model architectures and downstream tasks. Adversarial perturbations crafted against one VLP model have been shown to transfer to other models, including those with different architectures or pretraining objectives. This transferability is partly attributable to the shared reliance on foundation models such as CLIP as visual backbones across a wide range of VLP systems. When a vulnerability is present in the shared backbone, adversarial examples crafted to exploit it can propagate to all downstream models that inherit these representations, creating systemic security risks across the broader ecosystem of VLP applications.

**Black-Box Attacks on Large Vision-Language Models.** The advent of powerful large vision-language models (VLMs) such as GPT-4V and LLaVA has introduced new adversarial threat vectors. Black-box attack scenarios, where the adversary has no direct access to model parameters or gradients, have demonstrated that these large models remain vulnerable to carefully crafted adversarial inputs. Studies have shown that transferable adversarial examples generated against surrogate models can successfully fool proprietary large VLMs, raising serious concerns about the security of commercial deployments. The scale and complexity of these models do not inherently confer robustness; in fact, their broad capabilities and extensive training on web-crawled data may introduce additional vulnerabilities tied to memorized patterns and distributional biases. Research into black-box attacks on systems like GPT-4V highlights that even state-of-the-art models, despite extensive safety fine-tuning, can be manipulated through adversarially constructed visual or textual prompts to produce harmful or incorrect outputs.

**Shared Vulnerabilities from Foundation Models.** A significant portion of the adversarial vulnerabilities observed in VLP models can be traced back to weaknesses inherited from foundational pretraining models, particularly CLIP. Because CLIP serves as the visual encoder for a vast number of downstream VLP systems, any adversarial weakness at the CLIP representation level propagates broadly. The phenomenon of CLIP-blind pairs—where visually distinct images are mapped to nearly identical representations—represents one such structural vulnerability that adversarial attacks can exploit to cause misclassification or retrieval failures without perceptible visual perturbations. This shared inheritance of vulnerabilities underscores the importance of evaluating adversarial robustness not only at the level of individual VLP models but also at the level of the foundational encoders upon which they are built.

**Defensive Approaches.** Several defensive strategies have been proposed to improve the adversarial robustness of VLP models. Adversarial Prompt Tuning (APT) adapts the prompt tuning paradigm to the adversarial setting, learning input prompts that guide the model toward more robust representations without requiring full model retraining—an approach that is particularly appealing given the computational cost of adversarial training at the scale of modern VLP systems. Information bottleneck-based robustness methods take a different approach, encouraging the model to learn compressed representations that retain task-relevant information while discarding adversarial perturbations, thereby improving robustness by design. Adversarial training, which augments the pretraining or fine-tuning data with adversarially perturbed examples, remains a foundational defense strategy, though its application at VLP scales is computationally expensive. Certified defenses and randomized smoothing techniques offer provable robustness guarantees but remain challenging to scale to high-dimensional multimodal inputs.

**Broader Implications and Connection to Social Bias.** The adversarial vulnerabilities of VLP models have significant implications for high-stakes applications, including autonomous driving, medical image analysis, and content moderation. As these models are increasingly integrated into critical pipelines, ensuring their robustness against adversarial manipulation becomes essential. Importantly, the statistical properties of web-scale pretraining data that give rise to adversarial vulnerabilities are closely related to the mechanisms through which social biases are introduced into VLP systems—a connection that will be examined in the following subsection. Both adversarial fragility and demographic bias can be understood as consequences of learning overly brittle statistical correlations from imperfect large-scale data, and both demand evaluation frameworks that go beyond aggregate performance metrics. The field currently lacks comprehensive standardized benchmarks for evaluating multimodal adversarial robustness, and the rapid pace of model development has outpaced the corresponding progress in robustness evaluation. Future work must address the development of more effective multimodal defenses, standardized adversarial evaluation protocols, and a deeper theoretical understanding of why VLP models are vulnerable and how architectural choices influence robustness.

### 7.5 Social Bias and Fairness

## 7.5 Social Bias and Fairness

Vision-language pretraining models, despite their remarkable capabilities, are susceptible to absorbing and amplifying social biases present in large-scale web-crawled training data. Just as adversarial vulnerabilities discussed in the previous subsection arise partly from the statistical properties of web-scale pretraining, social biases represent another fundamental consequence of learning from such data at scale. Because these models learn statistical correlations from billions of image-text pairs scraped from the internet, they inevitably internalize societal stereotypes related to gender, race, ethnicity, age, and other demographic attributes. The scale of web data, while beneficial for learning rich multimodal representations, acts as a conduit for propagating and entrenching harmful biases at an unprecedented scale—a concern that is deeply intertwined with the data quality and noise challenges explored in the following subsection.

**Sources and Manifestations of Bias.** Social biases in VLP models manifest across multiple modalities and tasks. Gender bias is among the most extensively studied, where models tend to associate certain occupations, activities, or attributes with specific genders based on skewed co-occurrence statistics in training data. For example, visual question answering systems may systematically associate "doctor" with male-presenting individuals and "nurse" with female-presenting individuals, reflecting biases in both the visual training images and accompanying textual descriptions. Racial and ethnic biases similarly emerge, with models exhibiting differential performance and stereotypical associations across demographic groups. These biases are particularly insidious because they are often invisible in aggregate performance metrics, appearing only when models are evaluated through carefully designed diagnostic frameworks.

**Measuring and Diagnosing Bias.** The research community has developed several dedicated frameworks and benchmarks for measuring social biases in VLP models. Counterfactual-based approaches such as CounterBias systematically assess model behavior by constructing image-text pairs that differ only in demographic attributes, measuring disparities in model predictions or confidence scores as indicators of bias. The VL-Bias benchmark provides structured evaluation of cross-modal bias across diverse demographic dimensions. PATA (Protected Attribute Tag Association) evaluates the degree to which models inappropriately associate protected demographic attributes with contextually irrelevant visual or textual content. Harvard-FairVLMed extends bias evaluation into the medical domain, recognizing that biased VLP models deployed in clinical settings can have severe consequences for health equity. These benchmarks collectively reveal that standard downstream task accuracy metrics are wholly inadequate for detecting and quantifying demographic biases, underscoring the need for targeted diagnostic evaluation [55].

**Debiasing Approaches.** Several methods have been proposed to mitigate social biases in VLP models. FairVLP introduces fairness constraints directly into the pretraining objective, encouraging the model to learn representations that are invariant to protected demographic attributes while preserving task-relevant information. DeAR (Debiasing via Attribute Randomization) employs data augmentation and attribute neutralization strategies to reduce the correlation between demographic attributes and model predictions. FairCLIP specifically targets contrastive vision-language models such as CLIP, which have become foundational components in many downstream applications, applying fairness-aware contrastive learning to reduce demographic disparities in the learned embedding space. These approaches represent important steps toward building more equitable VLP systems, though they often face trade-offs between bias reduction and overall model performance.

**Impact of Dataset Curation on Bias.** An important dimension of bias in VLP that connects directly to the data quality challenges discussed in the following subsection concerns the role of dataset construction practices. FairDeDup investigates how semantic dataset deduplication strategies, which are increasingly employed to improve data efficiency and model performance, interact with demographic bias. The study reveals that deduplication can have complex and sometimes counterintuitive effects on bias propagation: while removing redundant samples may improve overall data diversity, it can also disproportionately affect the representation of minority demographic groups, potentially amplifying rather than mitigating biases. This finding highlights that data quality interventions must be evaluated not only for their impact on downstream task performance but also for their fairness implications. The connection to data deduplication strategies such as those studied in the context of web-crawled pretraining datasets further underscores the complexity of achieving both efficiency and fairness simultaneously—a tension that pervades many of the data curation decisions examined in the following subsection.

**Ethical Implications for High-Stakes Applications.** The ethical stakes of social bias in VLP models are dramatically elevated when these models are deployed in high-stakes real-world applications. In healthcare, biased medical VLP models can perpetuate diagnostic disparities, provide inferior performance for underrepresented patient populations, and contribute to systemic inequities in clinical care. Domain-specific medical VLP efforts [53] that leverage clinical reports and medical images must be particularly attentive to demographic representation in training data, as biased models could reinforce existing disparities in medical imaging datasets. In hiring and employment contexts, vision-language systems used for resume screening or candidate evaluation may encode and perpetuate discriminatory patterns. In content moderation and surveillance applications, demographic performance disparities can lead to differential treatment of individuals from marginalized groups.

**Open Challenges and Future Directions.** Despite meaningful progress, addressing social bias in VLP remains an open and challenging research problem. Key difficulties include the lack of consensus on operationalizing and measuring fairness in multimodal settings, the tension between improving aggregate performance through web-scale data and controlling bias from the same data, and the challenge of ensuring that debiasing interventions generalize across diverse downstream tasks and deployment contexts. These challenges are closely linked to the broader data quality issues addressed in the following subsection, as noise, misalignment, and distributional imbalances in pretraining corpora are often the very mechanisms through which social biases are introduced and amplified. Future work must develop more comprehensive bias benchmarks that capture intersectional identities and culturally specific biases beyond Western-centric perspectives. Incorporating fairness objectives directly into large-scale pretraining pipelines, rather than as post-hoc corrections, represents a promising direction. Moreover, the development of auditing frameworks that can efficiently detect demographic disparities in models trained on proprietary web-scale datasets is essential for responsible deployment of VLP systems. The broader vision-language pretraining community must recognize that model fairness is not merely a peripheral concern but a fundamental requirement for the responsible development and deployment of multimodal AI systems.

### 7.6 Data Quality, Diversity, and Noise in Pretraining Datasets

## 7.6 Data Quality, Diversity, and Noise in Pretraining Datasets

Data quality, diversity, and noise in pretraining datasets represent fundamental challenges that directly impact the effectiveness and generalizability of vision-language pretraining (VLP) models — and, as the social bias discussion in the preceding subsection underscores, the composition of these datasets has profound implications not only for performance but also for fairness and equity. The dominant paradigm of crawling large-scale image-text pairs from the web, while enabling unprecedented scale, introduces significant noise and misalignment between visual content and accompanying textual descriptions [2]. Alt-text captions, which constitute a primary source of textual supervision in many web-crawled corpora, are often incomplete, coarse, and semantically misaligned with their corresponding images, providing only weak and noisy cross-modal supervision signals that challenge robust representation learning [4].

The inherent noise in web-crawled datasets manifests in multiple forms: incorrect or irrelevant textual descriptions, partial descriptions that omit key visual concepts, and systematic biases reflecting the demographics and cultural contexts prevalent on the internet. As explored in the preceding subsection, these systematic biases are not incidental but are a direct consequence of the statistical properties of web-scale data, where the demographics and cultural perspectives of dominant internet communities are disproportionately reflected. These issues are further compounded by the long-tailed distribution of concepts in large-scale corpora, where common concepts are heavily over-represented while rare or specialized categories receive insufficient coverage [57]. Such distributional imbalances limit the ability of VLP models to generalize to diverse real-world scenarios, as highlighted by evaluation studies showing significant performance gaps on out-of-distribution data compared to standard benchmarks — a concern that the following subsection examines in greater depth.

Existing data filtering approaches, most notably CLIPScore-based filtering which retains image-text pairs based on their cosine similarity under a pretrained CLIP model, have become widely adopted but carry their own limitations. Since CLIP itself is trained on noisy web data, CLIPScore filtering can propagate existing biases and may inadequately capture semantic nuances, particularly for fine-grained and compositional descriptions that go beyond coarse object-level matching. This circular dependency between filtering tools and the pretrained models they evaluate underscores the need for more principled approaches to data curation. Notably, as the preceding subsection discussed in the context of FairDeDup, even well-intentioned data curation strategies such as deduplication can have complex and unintended effects on demographic representation, illustrating that data quality interventions must be evaluated holistically across both performance and fairness dimensions.

To address incomplete textual supervision, multi-label tag augmentation strategies have emerged as an effective means of enriching coarse captions with more explicit semantic signals. The IDEA framework [47] demonstrates that jointly performing online multi-label recognition during pretraining can identify and incorporate fine-grained image tags that are absent or underspecified in original alt-text descriptions, significantly boosting performance across multiple downstream benchmarks with minimal additional computational overhead. By dynamically augmenting text diversity during training rather than relying on static offline annotations, IDEA circumvents the bottleneck imposed by fixed object detector vocabularies.

Bootstrapped filtering strategies represent another promising direction, wherein a model's own generative and discriminative capabilities are leveraged iteratively to refine training data quality. Rather than relying solely on external filtering signals, these approaches exploit the alignment between synthesized captions and original images to curate higher-quality training subsets, progressively improving the signal-to-noise ratio in the pretraining corpus. Knowledge augmentation approaches such as REAVL [48] further address data sparsity by integrating retrieved world knowledge into the pretraining pipeline, enabling models to benefit from structured semantic information not present in raw image-text pairs alone.

Structural knowledge integration, as explored in ROSITA [46], offers complementary improvements by incorporating scene graph representations that capture cross- and intra-modal semantic relationships. By using structured knowledge to guide masking and alignment objectives, such approaches mitigate the ambiguity inherent in coarse textual supervision and promote finer-grained cross-modal correspondence learning.

Data diversity has also been recognized as a critical and often underappreciated dimension of pretraining dataset quality, distinct from raw scale. Models trained on highly homogeneous datasets may achieve strong performance on standard benchmarks while failing to generalize across diverse image distributions, cultural contexts, and linguistic styles. This concern is underscored by findings from VLUE [57], which reveals that existing VLP models exhibit significant generalization gaps when evaluated on images from more culturally diverse distributions that differ from typical web-crawled data. Such generalization gaps, caused in part by the distributional imbalances and concept coverage limitations introduced during data curation, connect directly to the out-of-distribution robustness challenges examined in the following subsection. Metrics such as the diversity coefficient have been proposed to quantify the breadth of concept coverage in pretraining corpora, providing a more principled basis for dataset construction and comparison beyond simple scale metrics.

Together, these challenges highlight that achieving robust and generalizable VLP requires not only large quantities of training data but careful attention to data quality, semantic richness, concept diversity, and noise mitigation. The consequences of inadequate data curation extend beyond aggregate performance degradation — they propagate into social biases, as discussed in the preceding subsection, and into poor generalization under distribution shift, as the following subsection will demonstrate. Future progress in this area will likely depend on the development of more principled filtering and augmentation pipelines, the use of synthetic caption generation to enrich sparse or misaligned descriptions, and the establishment of standardized data quality evaluation frameworks that assess pretraining corpora along multiple complementary dimensions — encompassing not only scale and coverage but also fairness, semantic richness, and distributional robustness.

### 7.7 Generalization to Out-of-Distribution Data and Long-Tail Scenarios

Generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) data and long-tail scenarios represents a critical and underappreciated challenge that is deeply intertwined with the data quality and noise issues discussed in the previous section. While the preceding discussion addressed how noise, misalignment, and distributional imbalance in pretraining corpora constrain representation quality, the consequences of these shortcomings become most visible when VLP models are evaluated beyond the distributions encountered during pretraining — revealing a concerning gap between reported benchmark performance and true model capability.

A fundamental concern is the overestimation of model performance caused by the overlap between pretraining corpora and downstream evaluation datasets. As highlighted by the VLUE benchmark [57], most downstream VL datasets are annotated using raw images that have already been seen during pretraining, inflating apparent model capabilities. When tested on out-of-distribution test sets annotated on images drawn from more diverse, culturally varied distributions, VLP models exhibit a sizable generalization gap across all evaluated architectures [57]. This finding underscores that strong aggregate benchmark performance does not necessarily imply robust generalization to novel visual concepts and unseen image distributions.

Beyond distribution shift caused by pretraining data overlap, VLP models frequently struggle with long-tail class distributions and imbalanced datasets. Rare visual categories that appear infrequently in web-crawled pretraining corpora receive insufficient representation, leading to poor recognition performance on fine-grained datasets, where the long-tailed nature of distributions exposes the brittleness of learned multimodal representations. This limitation is directly connected to the over-representation of common concepts in large-scale pretraining data noted earlier, as models tend to overfit to frequent visual patterns while remaining ineffective for rare but important categories.

Viewpoint and perspective variations present an additional axis of OOD challenge. Models trained predominantly on standard frontal or canonical viewpoints may fail to recognize objects or scenes depicted from unusual angles or 3D perspectives, and explicit training on viewpoint diversity is necessary to overcome this systematic limitation.

Several strategies have been proposed to improve OOD robustness. Ensemble methods that aggregate predictions from multiple VLP models with complementary training objectives and data sources offer improved coverage of the input distribution space. Test-time prompt tuning techniques adapt model behavior at inference time to specific input characteristics without requiring full fine-tuning, offering a lightweight path toward improved generalization. Domain generalization techniques that explicitly encourage invariant feature learning across source domains also show promise for bridging the gap between pretraining and deployment distributions.

Furthermore, evaluation frameworks designed to surface these generalization limitations play an important diagnostic role. The VLUE benchmark [57] explicitly incorporates out-of-distribution test sets and efficiency-performance trade-off measures, providing a more honest assessment of VLP model generalization. Similarly, fine-grained diagnostic frameworks such as VL-CheckList [55] and VALSE [59] reveal that failures on OOD instances are often symptomatic of deeper representation shortcomings rather than superficial domain mismatches — a pattern that connects naturally to the compositional and fine-grained reasoning failures examined in the following section.

Collectively, these findings suggest that closing the OOD generalization gap requires not only scaling pretraining data and model capacity, but fundamentally rethinking data curation strategies to ensure broader concept coverage, adopting training objectives that promote distributional robustness, and establishing more rigorous evaluation protocols that measure performance under realistic deployment conditions rather than in-distribution test scenarios. As the following section will discuss, many of these OOD failures are further compounded by the inherent difficulty VLP models face in handling compositional language and fine-grained semantic distinctions — suggesting that robust generalization and compositional understanding are deeply interrelated challenges that must be addressed in concert.

### 7.8 Fine-Grained Semantic Understanding and Compositional Reasoning

## Fine-Grained Semantic Understanding and Compositional Reasoning

Despite remarkable progress in vision-language pretraining, fine-grained semantic understanding and compositional reasoning remain among the most persistent and challenging limitations of current VLP models. As established in the preceding discussion of out-of-distribution generalization, many failures that appear as domain shift problems are, in fact, rooted in deeper representational shortcomings — particularly the inability of VLP models to handle structured, compositionally complex language and its precise grounding in visual scenes. While aggregate accuracy metrics on standard benchmarks such as VQA v2 and image-text retrieval have steadily improved, targeted diagnostic evaluations reveal deep-seated weaknesses that are not captured by these coarse measures.

**Failures in Compositional and Structural Understanding**

A fundamental limitation of VLP models lies in their difficulty handling compositional language — the ability to understand how individual concepts combine to form complex meanings. Current models frequently exhibit bag-of-words behaviors, failing to leverage word order, syntactic structure, or logical composition when processing multimodal inputs. This manifests in concrete failures: models struggle to distinguish "a dog chasing a cat" from "a cat chasing a dog," indicating insensitivity to relational structure and argument order. Such failures are symptomatic of models that learn statistical co-occurrence patterns rather than genuine structural understanding of language and its grounding in visual scenes. These behaviors are directly traceable to the distributional biases and noisy co-occurrence statistics that characterize web-crawled pretraining corpora, reinforcing the connection between data quality challenges and representational limitations.

**Attribute Binding and Object-Relation Understanding**

Attribute binding — correctly associating properties such as color, size, shape, and texture with specific objects — is another area where VLP models fall short. Models often recognize individual attributes and objects in isolation but fail to correctly bind attributes to the appropriate referents when multiple objects are present. Similarly, recognizing inter-object spatial and semantic relationships remains challenging, as models tend to process visual and linguistic inputs as holistic patterns rather than structured compositional entities. These weaknesses are particularly problematic in tasks requiring precise grounding of phrases to specific image regions, and they represent a significant contributor to the OOD generalization failures discussed in the previous section, where fine-grained concept distinctions are precisely what distinguishes in-distribution from out-of-distribution test instances.

**Spatial Relationship Understanding**

Spatial reasoning presents a specific compositional challenge that current VLP frameworks handle poorly. Understanding prepositions and spatial descriptors such as "above," "below," "to the left of," and "between" requires models to perform structured relational reasoning over localized visual features. Yet VLP models trained primarily on global image-text alignment objectives rarely develop robust spatial understanding, since such relationships are often underrepresented or ambiguously expressed in web-crawled training data. The mismatch between coarse pretraining objectives and the fine-grained spatial structure needed for downstream tasks creates a persistent performance gap — one that is further amplified when models are evaluated on out-of-distribution instances where spatial configurations deviate from pretraining norms.

**Negation and Logical Phenomena**

Negation handling exposes another systematic weakness. Standard VLP pretraining objectives such as masked language modeling and image-text contrastive learning do not provide explicit supervision for understanding negation and its visual implications. As a result, models trained on such objectives often treat negated and affirmative descriptions as semantically similar, failing to correctly evaluate statements such as "there is no dog in the image." This limitation has practical consequences for tasks involving natural language inference, visual entailment, and safety-critical applications — including federated and privacy-sensitive deployment scenarios discussed in the following section, where robustness to precise semantic distinctions is essential.

**Diagnostic Benchmark Evidence**

A growing body of diagnostic benchmarks has systematically exposed these limitations. Probing studies using carefully constructed foil-based and counterfactual evaluation methodologies reveal that many VLP models perform at chance level on fine-grained tasks requiring attribute, relation, and compositional understanding, even when achieving strong performance on standard benchmarks. Benchmarks targeting specific linguistic phenomena — such as number understanding, coreference, spatial relations, and entity tracking — consistently reveal that VLP models fail on these structured reasoning tasks at rates far exceeding their general benchmark performance, underscoring that standard evaluations substantially overestimate true model capabilities. This pattern of evaluation inadequacy connects directly to the broader measurement challenges that also affect assessments of OOD robustness, suggesting a systemic need for more rigorous and diagnostic evaluation frameworks across all dimensions of VLP capability.

**Limited Impact of Data Scaling**

Critically, these fine-grained limitations are not readily resolved by simply scaling pretraining data or model size. Empirical evidence suggests that increasing the volume of web-crawled image-text pairs, while beneficial for general retrieval and classification performance, provides only marginal improvements on compositionally challenging tasks. This is because web data is inherently biased toward common co-occurrence patterns and lacks the structured, compositionally diverse examples needed to teach models fine-grained semantic distinctions. The limitations thus appear to be architectural and objective-driven rather than purely data-driven, suggesting that fundamental changes to how models are trained and what they are trained on are necessary. Importantly, these same data-driven constraints will resurface in the context of federated VLP, where the inability to freely pool diverse, compositionally rich training data across clients further compounds the challenge of learning structured multimodal representations.

**Toward Fine-Grained and Compositional VLP**

Several promising directions have emerged to address these structural weaknesses. Position-aware modeling, which incorporates explicit object position encoding into VLP frameworks, helps bridge the gap between visual localization and linguistic grounding by providing models with spatially structured visual representations. Approaches that reformulate visual grounding tasks as position-sensitive objectives encourage models to develop finer-grained spatial understanding beyond holistic scene matching.

Incorporating explicit linguistic knowledge into VLP training is another productive direction. By integrating syntactic parse trees, semantic role labels, scene graphs, and structured knowledge representations into pretraining, models can be encouraged to learn compositionally organized representations rather than unstructured statistical associations. Scene graph-based structural alignment objectives, for instance, explicitly supervise the correspondence between relational linguistic structures and visual relationship patterns.

Compositional language encoding frameworks that explicitly model the combination of linguistic primitives — attributes, objects, relations, and their interactions — represent a promising architectural direction. Rather than treating sentences as holistic vectors, such frameworks parse sentences into structured components and align each component to corresponding visual regions or features, providing the model with a more explicit account of compositional meaning.

Multi-granularity alignment objectives that train models to align representations at multiple levels of abstraction — from individual word-region correspondences to phrase-level and sentence-level matching — also show promise for developing richer and more structured cross-modal representations. These objectives complement coarse global alignment by providing the fine-grained supervision signals needed to learn precise semantic correspondences [66] [38].

Unified architectures that jointly learn from both unimodal and multimodal data, and that explicitly share parameters across modalities to encourage the transfer of semantic structure, offer additional promise. Research has shown that greater parameter sharing between visual and linguistic encoders can promote the transfer of common semantic patterns — such as syntactic attention patterns — from language to vision, which may support more structured and compositional visual understanding [103].

Finally, evaluation methodology itself must advance alongside model development. The persistent gap between aggregate benchmark performance and fine-grained capability reflects not only model limitations but also the inadequacy of current evaluation tools for measuring compositional understanding. Targeted, controlled evaluations that isolate specific linguistic and visual phenomena provide more diagnostic and actionable insights into model capabilities and failure modes, guiding the development of VLP systems that achieve genuine, generalizable multimodal understanding rather than superficial pattern matching [35].

Collectively, addressing fine-grained semantic understanding and compositional reasoning requires a coordinated effort spanning model architecture design, pretraining objective formulation, data curation, and evaluation methodology. These interconnected demands — for richer training data, more structured objectives, and more diagnostic evaluation — carry direct implications for the federated and privacy-preserving settings examined in the following section, where the very constraints on data sharing that define federated VLP make it even harder to acquire the compositionally diverse training signal that current models so critically need. As such, compositional understanding stands as one of the central open challenges not only for centralized VLP research, but for the responsible and effective deployment of vision-language pretraining across the full range of real-world application settings.

### 7.9 Privacy, Federated Learning, and Decentralized Pretraining

## Privacy-Preserving and Federated Vision-Language Pretraining

The centralization of large-scale multimodal pretraining data raises profound privacy and data governance concerns, particularly when sensitive domains such as healthcare, finance, and personal communications are involved. Just as the previous discussion highlighted systematic limitations in compositional understanding and evaluation methodology, the field must also contend with the structural constraints imposed by data privacy requirements that fundamentally shape what can be learned and how. Collecting and pooling vast image-text datasets from distributed sources into a single server creates significant risks of data leakage, unauthorized use, and violations of regulatory frameworks such as GDPR and HIPAA. These challenges are especially acute in medical vision-language pretraining, where patient data from clinical imaging and diagnostic reports cannot be freely shared across institutional boundaries.

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a promising paradigm to address these concerns by enabling decentralized model training across multiple clients without requiring raw data to leave local environments. In the context of vision-language pretraining, federated frameworks such as FLoRA and FedRGB have been proposed to allow distributed participants to collaboratively train multimodal representations while preserving data privacy. These approaches typically involve local gradient computation followed by secure model aggregation, ensuring that sensitive image-text pairs remain on-premises throughout the pretraining process.

However, federated VLP introduces a distinct set of technical challenges that compound the architectural and objective-level limitations discussed throughout this survey. Heterogeneous client data distributions pose a fundamental obstacle: individual clients may possess highly imbalanced, domain-specific, or non-identically distributed multimodal data, making it difficult to learn globally generalizable cross-modal representations. This problem is particularly pronounced in biomedical settings, where different hospitals may specialize in distinct imaging modalities, patient demographics, or clinical conditions, resulting in severe statistical heterogeneity across federation participants. This heterogeneity directly exacerbates the fine-grained semantic understanding challenges identified in the preceding sections, as the compositionally diverse training signal needed to overcome bag-of-words behaviors and attribute binding failures becomes even harder to obtain when data cannot be freely aggregated.

Communication efficiency represents another critical bottleneck in federated VLP. Large-scale vision-language models such as those built upon transformer backbones contain hundreds of millions or billions of parameters, and transmitting full model updates or gradients across potentially bandwidth-limited communication channels is prohibitively expensive. Strategies such as gradient compression, sparse updates, local model distillation, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods — including adapter layers and prompt tuning — have been explored as means to reduce communication overhead while preserving model quality.

Fairness is an additional concern in federated multimodal settings, as standard aggregation methods such as FedAvg may disproportionately favor clients with larger or higher-quality datasets, leading to models that perform well on dominant data distributions but poorly on minority or underrepresented clients. This issue connects directly to the evaluation standardization challenges outlined in the following section: without robust, multi-dimensional evaluation frameworks capable of measuring performance across diverse and out-of-distribution populations, the disparate impact of federated aggregation on underrepresented clients may go undetected. The concern is particularly consequential when federated VLP is deployed in high-stakes domains, where equitable performance across diverse patient populations or geographic regions is essential.

Looking forward, federated vision-language pretraining remains a largely underexplored area relative to its centralized counterpart, with open questions surrounding the design of privacy-preserving pretraining objectives, the development of robust aggregation schemes under extreme data heterogeneity, and the integration of differential privacy mechanisms that provide formal privacy guarantees without catastrophic degradation of multimodal representation quality. Addressing these challenges will be crucial for enabling the responsible deployment of VLP in sensitive real-world applications, and will require the same coordinated advances in architecture design, objective formulation, data curation, and evaluation methodology that characterize the broader open challenges facing the field.

### 7.10 Evaluation Framework Limitations and Standardization Needs

## Evaluation Framework Limitations and Standardization Needs

Despite the remarkable progress in vision-language pretraining, the evaluation paradigms used to measure model capabilities remain a significant bottleneck in the field. Just as the preceding discussion of privacy-preserving and federated VLP highlighted structural constraints arising from data governance requirements, the limitations of current evaluation frameworks impose their own structural constraints on scientific progress—making it difficult to determine whether new architectures, objectives, and training strategies represent genuine advances in multimodal understanding or merely better exploitation of benchmark artifacts. Current benchmarks and assessment methodologies suffer from several critical limitations that collectively hinder objective, comprehensive, and reproducible measurement of VLP model performance.

### Over-Reliance on Aggregate Accuracy Metrics

One of the most pervasive issues in VLP evaluation is the over-reliance on aggregate accuracy metrics such as top-1 accuracy, Recall@K, and composite scores like R@sum. While these metrics provide convenient single-number summaries, they obscure the fine-grained capabilities and systematic failure modes of models. A model may achieve high aggregate accuracy on standard benchmarks like VQA v2 or COCO retrieval by exploiting dataset biases, language priors, or superficial statistical correlations, without genuinely learning deep cross-modal understanding. This phenomenon has been well-documented across multimodal tasks, where diagnostic evaluations consistently reveal that high-performing models often fail on carefully constructed challenge sets targeting specific linguistic or visual phenomena.

The problem is compounded by the fact that many widely adopted benchmarks were designed primarily to measure task performance rather than to probe the underlying representational capabilities of models. As a result, improvements on leaderboards do not necessarily translate to improvements in the model's fundamental understanding of visual-semantic relationships, compositional reasoning, or spatial comprehension. Studies examining cross-modal interaction reveal that even expressive multimodal models may achieve strong performance while relying predominantly on unimodal signals, effectively bypassing the cross-modal fusion mechanisms they are presumed to leverage [104]. This finding is particularly significant in the context of federated VLP, where heterogeneous data distributions across clients may further encourage models to fall back on unimodal shortcuts when the cross-modal signal is weak or inconsistent.

### Lack of Standardized and Comprehensive Benchmarks

The VLP evaluation landscape is fragmented across a diverse collection of benchmarks, each covering a narrow subset of model capabilities. Standard evaluation suites for VLP typically include a handful of tasks such as VQA, image captioning, image-text retrieval, and visual entailment, but these tasks collectively fail to provide comprehensive coverage of the skills required for robust multimodal understanding. There is no universally adopted standardized benchmark suite that holistically assesses visual reasoning, compositional understanding, spatial grounding, attribute binding, negation handling, and out-of-distribution generalization within a single coherent evaluation framework.

This fragmentation creates practical challenges for fair comparison across methods, as different papers report results on different subsets of benchmarks, use varying evaluation protocols, and sometimes fine-tune under different data regimes. The absence of standardization makes it difficult to draw reliable conclusions about the relative strengths of different architectures, pretraining objectives, or data strategies—a challenge that is directly compounded in federated settings, where the inability to pool data makes it even harder to establish consistent evaluation baselines across heterogeneous client populations.

### Advanced Diagnostic and Probing Frameworks

In response to these limitations, the community has developed several more nuanced diagnostic frameworks that go beyond aggregate metrics to probe specific model capabilities. These advanced evaluation approaches are essential for understanding what VLP models actually learn and where their systematic weaknesses lie.

Probing benchmarks such as SNARE have been designed to isolate specific linguistic and semantic capabilities including lexical, relational, and syntactic understanding, revealing that even state-of-the-art VLP models exhibit significant gaps in structured language comprehension. Similarly, VL-CheckList provides fine-grained decomposition of VLP capabilities across object recognition, attribute understanding, and relational reasoning, demonstrating that models scoring well on standard tasks often fail on targeted probes for specific semantic phenomena.

Benchmarks like VALSE target visio-linguistic grounding on carefully controlled linguistic phenomena including plurality, existence, spatial relations, and action descriptions, exposing systematic failures in models that appear capable based on standard evaluations. The What'sUp benchmark specifically isolates spatial reasoning capabilities, revealing persistent weaknesses in understanding above-below and left-right spatial relationships that aggregate metrics would not surface. Compositional evaluation frameworks such as ARO and CLoVe assess whether models genuinely understand word order, attribute binding, and relational structure, with studies showing that many contrastive VLP models exhibit bag-of-words behavior that fails to capture compositional semantics—exactly the kind of fine-grained failure mode that motivates the grounded and compositional generation directions outlined in the following section.

The MMVP benchmark has revealed CLIP-blind pairs—visually distinct images that contrastive models erroneously perceive as semantically similar—exposing systematic visual shortcomings that aggregate retrieval metrics fail to detect. More comprehensive frameworks like MMBench and ReForm-Eval provide structured evaluation across multiple dimensions of VLP capability, enabling more reliable and reproducible comparisons across models.

### The Need for Culturally Diverse and Out-of-Distribution Evaluation

A particularly underexplored dimension of evaluation concerns cultural diversity and geographic representation. The vast majority of VLP benchmarks are constructed from Western-centric image-text corpora that disproportionately represent certain cultural contexts, demographics, and visual concepts. Evaluating models exclusively on such benchmarks provides an incomplete picture of their capabilities and may mask significant performance disparities when models are deployed in diverse real-world settings. This limitation connects directly to the fairness concerns raised in the context of federated VLP: without culturally representative evaluation frameworks, the disparate impact of training on underrepresented client populations may go entirely undetected.

Out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation is equally critical. Standard benchmarks typically involve test distributions that closely mirror the pretraining data distribution, leading to overly optimistic performance estimates. Studies have shown that substantial overlap between pretraining and evaluation data can lead to significant overestimation of model generalization capabilities, with performance degrading substantially when evaluated on genuinely novel distributions. The VLUE benchmark was specifically designed to measure VLP model generalization across diverse image distributions and to expose the gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution performance, highlighting the importance of OOD evaluation as a standard component of model assessment—particularly for the safer, fairer, and more interpretable systems that the community must develop going forward.

### Multi-Dimensional Evaluation Standards

Moving forward, there is a growing consensus that VLP evaluation must adopt multi-dimensional standards that simultaneously assess multiple orthogonal capabilities. These dimensions should include, at minimum: (1) fine-grained semantic understanding at the object, attribute, and relational levels; (2) compositional and structural reasoning; (3) spatial and geometric understanding; (4) robustness to distribution shifts and adversarial inputs; (5) cross-lingual and cross-cultural generalization; (6) efficiency and computational cost relative to performance; and (7) alignment with human judgment as measured through human evaluation studies.

The emergence of LLM-based evaluation approaches offers promising new avenues for scalable and flexible assessment, particularly for generative VLP tasks where rigid metric-based evaluation fails to capture the quality and informativeness of free-form outputs. Automated benchmark curation and LLM-based judgment frameworks such as Auto-Bench represent early steps toward more flexible and comprehensive evaluation, though they introduce their own challenges including potential biases in LLM judges and the difficulty of validating automated assessments against human ground truth.

### Toward Standardization

Addressing these evaluation limitations requires coordinated community efforts toward establishing standardized, comprehensive, and regularly updated benchmark suites. Such suites should incorporate diagnostic probes alongside task performance measures, include culturally diverse and geographically representative test data, enforce standardized fine-tuning and evaluation protocols to ensure fair comparison, and explicitly measure OOD generalization as a first-class evaluation criterion. These requirements are not merely aspirational: they are prerequisites for the responsible development of the unified foundation models, compositional generation systems, and continual learning frameworks described in the following section. Without such standardization, progress in VLP research risks being driven by benchmark overfitting rather than genuine advances in multimodal understanding, and the field's ability to objectively assess whether new methods represent true improvements—whether in centralized, federated, or other privacy-preserving settings—will remain fundamentally limited.

### 7.11 Future Directions in Vision-Language Pretraining

The field of vision-language pretraining stands at an inflection point, with numerous compelling research directions poised to shape its next chapter. Building upon the evaluation challenges and standardization needs discussed above, addressing these open problems will require not only better benchmarks but also fundamentally new modeling paradigms, training strategies, and deployment considerations. We outline below the most promising avenues that the community should pursue in the coming years.

**Large-Scale Unified Foundation Models.** One of the most transformative directions is the development of truly unified foundation models that seamlessly integrate multiple modalities—image, text, video, audio, depth, and beyond—within a single architecture. Current VLP models often specialize in either understanding or generation, or handle only two modalities. Future work should aspire to architectures that flexibly handle arbitrary combinations of input and output modalities under a single pretraining regime. Encoder-decoder designs that support both bidirectional understanding and autoregressive generation [6; 34] offer a promising foundation, but scaling these to encompass richer modality sets while maintaining computational tractability remains an open challenge. Unified transformer designs with modality-specific expert components suggest that such scalability is achievable without prohibitive parameter growth.

**Grounded and Compositional Generation.** Existing VLP models still struggle with generating outputs that are precisely grounded in fine-grained visual content, particularly when compositional reasoning over attributes, spatial relationships, and object interactions is required. As highlighted by the diagnostic benchmarks discussed in the previous section, models frequently exhibit bag-of-words behavior and fail on targeted compositional probes—underscoring the need for future systems to go beyond coarse image-level alignment to support pixel-level and region-level grounded generation. Incorporating structured representations such as scene graphs, spatial layouts, and relational embeddings into generative pretraining objectives will be critical. The integration of masked autoencoder objectives [32] with compositional generation tasks represents a natural progression toward models capable of grounded synthesis and editing.

**Sustainable Low-Resource and Weakly Supervised Pretraining.** The enormous computational cost and data requirements of current VLP methods limit their accessibility and environmental sustainability. Future research must prioritize approaches that achieve strong downstream performance with significantly less labeled or paired data. Free language modeling [44], which decouples prediction rate from corruption rate to accelerate convergence, exemplifies the kind of training efficiency gains needed. Combining such efficient objectives with weakly supervised or unpaired data paradigms—where object tags, retrieved knowledge, or LLM-generated pseudo-captions substitute for expensive human annotations—will democratize VLP and enable deployment in resource-constrained and low-resource language settings.

**Modular and Compositional Multi-Model Reasoning Frameworks.** Rather than relying on monolithic end-to-end models, future VLP systems may benefit from modular architectures that compose specialized expert models for perception, reasoning, and language understanding. Such compositional frameworks decompose complex multimodal tasks into sub-problems handled by purpose-built modules, improving interpretability and enabling systematic generalization. This modular paradigm draws inspiration from plug-and-play model composition strategies and aligns with encoder-decoder designs where distinct components handle encoding and decoding responsibilities [68], but extends this principle to entire model ecosystems where heterogeneous pretrained specialists collaborate at inference time.

**Scaling to New Modalities and Languages.** Despite impressive progress in English image-text VLP, the field remains underrepresented in multilingual and cross-cultural settings, as well as in modalities such as 3D point clouds, infrared, depth, and physiological signals. Closing these gaps is directly connected to the evaluation standardization challenge raised previously: without culturally diverse and geographically representative benchmarks, it is impossible to objectively measure progress in multilingual and cross-modal generalization. Future directions include extending multilingual cross-modal pretraining frameworks [105] to cover low-resource and typologically diverse languages, leveraging shared visual grounding as a universal semantic pivot. Simultaneously, expanding modality coverage through multi-modal binding strategies that treat language as a semantic anchor for aligning diverse sensory inputs will broaden the applicability of VLP to domains such as robotics, healthcare, and autonomous systems.

**Safer, Fairer, and More Interpretable Systems.** As VLP models are increasingly deployed in high-stakes real-world applications—including healthcare, hiring, and content moderation—the imperative for safety, fairness, and interpretability becomes paramount. The evaluation limitations discussed earlier, particularly the lack of culturally diverse benchmarks and out-of-distribution evaluation, directly impede our ability to detect and address such biases. Future research must develop principled methods for auditing and mitigating social biases absorbed from web-scale training corpora, building on emerging debiasing and counterfactual evaluation frameworks. Beyond bias, adversarial robustness remains a critical concern, as multimodal models inheriting vulnerabilities from pretrained visual encoders can be exploited through carefully crafted input perturbations. Developing training strategies and architectural safeguards that improve robustness without sacrificing generalization will be essential. Finally, interpretability research that reveals which visual and textual features drive model predictions—especially in safety-critical domains—will be indispensable for building trustworthy vision-language systems that align with human values and societal norms.

**Continual and Lifelong Multimodal Learning.** Real-world deployment requires models that can continuously assimilate new knowledge from non-stationary data streams without catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired capabilities. Continual VLP frameworks that combine compatibility-preserving momentum contrast with topology preservation offer early solutions to this challenge, but significant research is needed to handle heterogeneous, domain-shifted, and privacy-sensitive data streams at scale. Integrating federated learning paradigms with efficient continual pretraining strategies will enable privacy-preserving collaborative training across distributed data sources, an increasingly important consideration as regulatory requirements around data governance grow more stringent.

In summary, the future of vision-language pretraining lies at the intersection of scalability, efficiency, safety, and inclusivity. Progress along these directions will not only address the evaluation and benchmarking gaps identified throughout this survey, but will also advance benchmark performance and broaden the societal impact and trustworthiness of multimodal AI systems.
