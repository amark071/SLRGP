# Text-to-Image Diffusion Models: A Comprehensive Survey of Architectures, Methods, and Applications

## 1 Introduction and Background

### 1.1 The Evolution of Text-to-Image Generation

The field of text-to-image generation has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past decade, driven by successive waves of architectural innovation and the availability of large-scale multimodal datasets. Understanding this evolution is essential for appreciating the capabilities and limitations of modern diffusion-based systems, as well as the theoretical foundations described in the sections that follow.

**Early GAN-Based Approaches**

The earliest neural approaches to text-to-image generation were rooted in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), a framework in which a generator and discriminator engage in adversarial training to produce realistic images [1]. Landmark models in this era include StackGAN, which introduced a multi-stage generation strategy that progressively refined images from low to high resolution by conditioning on text descriptions at each stage, and AttnGAN, which incorporated cross-modal attention mechanisms to focus generation on relevant image regions corresponding to specific words in the input description [2]. These attention-guided architectures represented a significant step forward in aligning visual content with textual semantics, and subsequent work such as GR-GAN further explored gradual refinement strategies that decomposed the generation process into coarse-to-fine stages with complementary sentence-level and word-level text constraints [3].

Despite these advances, GAN-based text-to-image models suffered from several fundamental limitations. Training instability, mode collapse, and the tendency to produce blurry or artifact-laden images at high resolutions constrained their practical utility [4]. Moreover, the expressivity of GAN generators was inherently limited by the adversarial objective, making it difficult to capture the full diversity of complex textual descriptions involving multiple objects, spatial relationships, and fine-grained attributes. Attempts to scale GANs to larger datasets and more expressive architectures, such as GigaGAN [5] and StyleGAN-T [6], partially addressed these issues but struggled to match the generation quality achievable by emerging alternative paradigms.

**Autoregressive Models**

The advent of large-scale transformer architectures opened a new chapter in text-to-image generation. Autoregressive models reframed image generation as a sequence prediction problem by representing images as sequences of discrete tokens produced by vector-quantized autoencoders. DALL-E, developed by OpenAI, was among the first to demonstrate that a single large transformer trained on billions of image-text pairs could generate coherent and creative images from arbitrary textual prompts. Shortly thereafter, CogView [7] introduced a four-billion-parameter transformer with a VQ-VAE tokenizer, achieving state-of-the-art FID scores on MS-COCO and demonstrating the feasibility of training competitive text-to-image models using a unified autoregressive framework. These models demonstrated a remarkable ability to generalize to novel concept combinations, benefiting from the rich linguistic representations encoded in large-scale language model pretraining.

However, autoregressive models also faced significant challenges. Their sequential token generation process incurred substantial computational costs at inference time, as generating a single high-resolution image could require thousands of autoregressive steps. Furthermore, errors introduced early in the generation sequence tended to propagate and compound through subsequent steps, limiting spatial coherence and image fidelity. Models such as EMAGE explored non-autoregressive generation strategies that produce image tokens in parallel to address this latency bottleneck [8], but these alternatives often sacrificed generation quality for speed.

**The Rise of Diffusion-Based Paradigms**

The limitations of both GAN-based and autoregressive approaches created fertile ground for a new class of generative models based on diffusion processes. Rather than relying on adversarial objectives or sequential token prediction, diffusion models formulate image generation as the iterative reversal of a noise corruption process, learning to progressively denoise a Gaussian noise signal into a high-fidelity image over many denoising steps. The introduction of Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) demonstrated that this framework could achieve image quality surpassing GANs on established benchmarks while avoiding training instabilities. The subsequent development of latent diffusion models, which perform the diffusion process in the compressed latent space of a pretrained autoencoder rather than in pixel space, dramatically improved computational efficiency without sacrificing visual quality [9]. The precise mathematical mechanisms underlying these processes—including the forward corruption process, the learned reverse denoising network, and the variational training objective—are described in detail in the following section.

The application of diffusion models to text-to-image synthesis quickly produced transformative results. Imagen [10] demonstrated that combining large pretrained language models, specifically T5, with cascaded diffusion architectures could achieve unprecedented levels of photorealism and language understanding. DALL-E 2 extended this paradigm by conditioning a diffusion decoder on CLIP image embeddings derived from text prompts. Stable Diffusion made high-quality text-to-image generation broadly accessible through its open-source release and efficient latent diffusion architecture. Models such as Kandinsky [11] further refined this approach by combining image prior models with latent diffusion techniques, while RAPHAEL [12] introduced mixture-of-experts designs to enhance text-image alignment. CogView3 [13] explored relay diffusion frameworks to reduce both training and inference costs while maintaining competitive generation quality.

The rapid ascendance of diffusion models can be attributed to several key advantages over their predecessors. Unlike GANs, diffusion models do not require adversarial training and thus benefit from greater training stability and coverage of the data distribution. Unlike autoregressive models, the iterative denoising process can be guided by rich conditioning signals including text embeddings, spatial layouts, and reference images, enabling fine-grained semantic control. These properties, combined with the growing scale of training data and model parameters, have established diffusion models as the dominant paradigm for state-of-the-art text-to-image generation [14], setting the stage for the formal theoretical treatment of denoising diffusion probabilistic models that follows.

### 1.2 Fundamentals of Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

## 1.2 Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

Building on the historical trajectory described in Section 1.1—where the limitations of GAN-based and autoregressive approaches motivated the search for more stable and expressive generative frameworks—Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have emerged as a powerful class of deep generative models that achieve state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of synthesis tasks [15]. The theoretical framework of DDPMs rests on two coupled stochastic processes: a forward diffusion process that systematically corrupts data with Gaussian noise, and a learned reverse process that reconstructs clean data from noise through iterative denoising. Understanding these processes in detail is essential both for appreciating the advantages of diffusion models over their predecessors and for following the score-based and SDE-based theoretical extensions presented in Section 1.3.

**Forward Diffusion Process.** Given a data sample $\mathbf{x}_0 \sim q(\mathbf{x}_0)$, the forward process defines a Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise over $T$ timesteps:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, \mathbf{x}_{t-1},\, \beta_t \mathbf{I}),$$

where $\{\beta_t\}_{t=1}^T$ is a variance schedule controlling the rate of noise injection. A key convenience of this formulation is that the marginal distribution at any timestep $t$ admits a closed-form expression [16]:

$$q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\, \mathbf{x}_0,\, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t) \mathbf{I}),$$

where $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t (1 - \beta_s)$. As $T \to \infty$ and the schedule is appropriately designed, $\mathbf{x}_T$ converges to an isotropic Gaussian distribution, providing a tractable starting point for generation [15].

**Reverse Denoising Process.** The generative process inverts the forward diffusion by learning a parameterized reverse Markov chain:

$$p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)),$$

where a neural network predicts the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta$ (and optionally the variance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_\theta$) of the denoising transition. In practice, the network is often reparameterized to predict the added noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)$, leading to a simplified mean estimate [16]:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}} \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)\right).$$

**Variational Lower Bound Training Objective.** Training is performed by maximizing a variational lower bound (ELBO) on the log-likelihood of the data. The ELBO decomposes into a sum of KL divergence terms at each timestep, measuring how closely the learned reverse transitions match the true posterior $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)$ [16; 17]. In the noise-prediction parameterization, this objective reduces to a weighted mean-squared error loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{simple} = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\left[18],$$

which is both simple to optimize and empirically effective for high-quality sample generation. As will be discussed in Section 1.3, this noise-prediction objective is also directly interpretable as a form of denoising score matching, underscoring the deep theoretical connections between the DDPM framework and score-based generative models.

**Learned Variance Schedules.** Early formulations fixed the reverse process variances to constants derived from the forward schedule, leaving potentially significant expressivity on the table. A key improvement introduced in [19] demonstrates that learning the reverse variances—parameterized via interpolation between lower and upper bounds—enables DDPMs to achieve competitive log-likelihoods while maintaining sample quality. Crucially, learned variances allow high-quality generation with significantly fewer sampling steps, an order-of-magnitude reduction compared to fixed-variance models [19]. The same work also established that sample quality and likelihood scale smoothly with model capacity and training compute, making DDPMs highly practical for the large-scale text-to-image applications described throughout this survey.

**Accelerated Sampling via DDIM.** A central limitation of the original DDPM framework is its requirement for hundreds to thousands of sequential network evaluations during inference, making generation prohibitively slow in practice [20]. Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) address this by constructing a family of non-Markovian diffusion processes that share the same forward marginals—and thus the same training objective—as DDPMs, but whose reverse processes can be evaluated with far fewer steps [20]. DDIM introduces a deterministic generative trajectory parameterized by an implicit probabilistic model, enabling $10\times$ to $50\times$ speedups in wall-clock time without retraining. Furthermore, DDIM supports semantically meaningful interpolation directly in the latent space, a property that Markovian formulations do not naturally exhibit.

Additional acceleration strategies have since been proposed, including treating the reverse process as solving a differential equation on a manifold [21], deriving optimal discrete-time sampling schedules via dynamic programming over the decomposed ELBO [17], and using backward error analysis to derive improved step schedules that maintain high sample quality with as few as 8–20 function evaluations [22]. Bilateral modeling objectives have also been explored to jointly learn the noise schedule and score network, enabling generation in as few as 3 sampling steps [23]. These acceleration techniques are of particular practical importance for text-to-image diffusion systems, where interactive and real-time generation scenarios demand efficient inference.

**Connections to Score Matching and SDEs.** The noise-prediction objective in DDPMs is intimately connected to score matching: predicting $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is equivalent to estimating the score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log q(\mathbf{x}_t)$ of the perturbed data distribution [15]. This connection, which positions the noise-prediction network as implicitly learning the gradient of the log-density of the noised data, provides a unifying theoretical perspective that links DDPMs with score-based generative models and stochastic differential equations. Section 1.3 elaborates on this relationship in depth, showing how the Markov chain formulation introduced here can be generalized to a continuous-time SDE framework that encompasses both variance-exploding and variance-preserving diffusion processes and enables rigorous convergence analysis.

Together, these foundations—the forward corruption process, the learned reverse denoising network, the ELBO training objective, improved variance learning, and accelerated sampling—establish the mathematical bedrock upon which the rich landscape of text-to-image diffusion architectures and applications described in subsequent sections is built.

### 1.3 Score-Based Generative Models and Stochastic Differential Equations

## 1.3 Score-Based Generative Models and Stochastic Differential Equations

As established in the previous section, the noise-prediction objective of DDPMs is intimately connected to score matching—predicting the added noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is equivalent to estimating the score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t} \log q(\mathbf{x}_t)$ of the perturbed data distribution. This connection motivates a complementary perspective on diffusion-based generation grounded in the idea of learning the *score function*—the gradient of the log-probability density of the data—rather than directly modeling the data distribution itself. This framework traces its origins to denoising score matching, where a neural network is trained to predict the score of a data distribution perturbed by noise at multiple scales. The key insight is that by learning these noise-conditioned score functions, one can simulate a reverse-time stochastic process that progressively denoises random noise into coherent data samples.

### Noise-Conditioned Score Networks

Early formulations of score-based models trained a single neural network, conditioned on the noise level, to estimate scores across a continuous range of perturbation magnitudes. This gave rise to noise-conditioned score networks (NCSNs), which laid the groundwork for modern diffusion models. A critical theoretical advance was the recognition that the denoising score matching objective—minimizing the expected squared difference between the network output and the true score—is equivalent to maximizing a variational lower bound on the data log-likelihood, thereby connecting score matching with probabilistic inference [24].

### The SDE Unification

A landmark contribution to the field was the unification of score-based generative models and DDPMs under the framework of stochastic differential equations (SDEs) [25]. In this framework, the forward process that gradually corrupts data into noise—as described in Section 1.2 via the Markov chain formulation—is described more generally as the solution to a forward-time SDE, while sample generation corresponds to simulating the corresponding reverse-time SDE. Crucially, the drift term of the reverse SDE depends precisely on the score function of the time-marginal distributions, establishing a rigorous mathematical connection between score estimation and generative sampling.

This SDE perspective encompasses both the variance-exploding and variance-preserving diffusion processes as special cases, providing a principled and flexible family of generative models. Furthermore, by deriving an equivalent probability flow ordinary differential equation (ODE), this framework enables deterministic sampling with exact likelihood computation—an important capability not present in purely stochastic samplers such as the original DDPM [25]. The probability flow ODE interpretation also facilitates efficient sampling, with provably fast convergence guarantees [26], complementing the accelerated sampling strategies such as DDIM discussed in Section 1.2.

### Theoretical Foundations and Convergence

The SDE framework stimulated a rich body of theoretical work aimed at rigorously justifying the empirical success of score-based models. Convergence analyses have established polynomial-time guarantees for sampling from complex distributions given sufficiently accurate score estimates [27; 28; 29]. These results do not require strong assumptions such as log-concavity or smoothness, making them applicable to the highly multimodal distributions encountered in image generation. Importantly, the requirement for score accuracy is formulated in terms of an $L^2$ norm with respect to the data distribution, which aligns naturally with the denoising score matching training objective.

Additional theoretical insights have emerged from studying the Fokker-Planck equation (FPE) associated with the diffusion process. The FPE governs the evolution of the probability density under the forward SDE, and satisfying its score-level analogue has been shown to improve both likelihood and the degree of conservativity of learned score functions [30]. Moreover, a systematic analysis of the gap between ODE- and SDE-based sampling dynamics has been provided through the lens of the Fokker-Planck residual [31], offering principled regularization strategies to reduce distributional discrepancies between these two sampling paradigms.

### Relationship to DDPMs and Broader Implications

A thorough theoretical treatment has demonstrated that DDPMs, variational diffusion models, and score-based generative models share a common mathematical skeleton [32]. In particular, optimizing a DDPM amounts to learning a neural network that predicts either the original clean data, the added noise, or the score function of the noised distribution—three objectives that are provably equivalent under the appropriate parameterization. This unification underscores that the score function is the central object of interest across all these model families, and provides the theoretical grounding for the simplified training objective $\mathcal{L}_\text{simple}$ introduced in Section 1.2.

The SDE framework has also expanded the design space for diffusion processes beyond standard Gaussian perturbations. For instance, the critically-damped Langevin diffusion (CLD) introduces auxiliary velocity variables, resulting in a joint diffusion over an extended state space that simplifies the denoising task by requiring the model to learn only conditional velocity scores rather than data scores directly [33]. This approach achieves superior sample quality at comparable computational budgets, illustrating how the SDE perspective motivates principled architectural and process design choices.

Beyond images, the SDE formulation has been extended to function spaces through the Denoising Diffusion Operators (DDO) framework, which generalizes score matching to infinite-dimensional settings and enables generation of functional data such as solutions to partial differential equations [34]. Collectively, these advances demonstrate that the score-based SDE perspective is not merely a theoretical reformulation but a productive generative framework that continues to inspire both new algorithms and deeper understanding of the diffusion process as the task of learning the gradient of the data log-density across noise scales. These theoretical foundations directly inform the architectural design choices explored in the following section, where latent diffusion models leverage the efficiency of compressed representations to scale score-based generation to high-resolution text-to-image synthesis.

### 1.4 Latent Diffusion Models and Efficient Image Synthesis

## 1.4 Latent Diffusion Models

Diffusion models trained directly in pixel space achieve impressive generative quality but incur prohibitive computational costs, as each denoising step must operate over the full high-dimensional image representation. Building upon the score-based SDE framework established in the previous section—where the denoising trajectory is governed by learned score functions—a natural question arises: must this trajectory operate in the original data space? This bottleneck motivated the development of **Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs)**, which relocate the diffusion process to the compressed latent space of a pretrained autoencoder, dramatically reducing memory and compute requirements while retaining high visual fidelity [35].

The core insight of LDMs is a two-stage design. In the first stage, a powerful autoencoder—typically a variational autoencoder (VAE) or a VQ-regularized variant—is trained to encode high-resolution images into a compact latent representation and reconstruct them with minimal perceptual loss. The encoder learns to discard perceptually redundant high-frequency details while preserving semantically meaningful structure, yielding a latent space that is far lower-dimensional than the original pixel space yet rich enough to support high-fidelity decoding. In the second stage, a diffusion model is trained entirely within this latent space, operating on latent codes rather than raw pixels [35]. The score-matching objectives and SDE formulations discussed in Section 1.3 apply directly within this latent space, inheriting all associated theoretical guarantees while benefiting from dramatically reduced dimensionality. Because the latent space is substantially smaller, the computational cost of both training and inference is reduced by orders of magnitude compared to pixel-space counterparts, as evidenced by Würstchen's training requirement of approximately 24,602 A100-GPU hours versus Stable Diffusion 2.1's 200,000 GPU hours [36].

Beyond computational efficiency, the latent space formulation opens natural pathways for flexible conditioning. By introducing cross-attention layers into the denoising backbone, LDMs can incorporate arbitrary conditioning modalities—such as free-form text, bounding boxes, or semantic maps—by attending over encoded condition representations at each denoising step [35]. This design cleanly separates the perceptual compression stage from the semantic generation stage, allowing text encoders, layout descriptors, or other external signals to steer the generation process without requiring retraining of the autoencoder. These cross-attention-based conditioning mechanisms prove especially significant in the context of vision-language alignment, as they provide the architectural interface through which semantic embeddings—such as those produced by CLIP, discussed in the following section—can be injected into the denoising process. Subsequent work has further shown that the quality and structure of the latent space critically determine downstream generative fidelity and semantic interpretability [36], and that differentially private fine-tuning of LDMs can be achieved by updating only the cross-attention modules, reducing trainable parameters by roughly 90% while preserving generation quality [37].

The LDM paradigm has also been successfully extended beyond image synthesis. Video generation frameworks, for instance, have adopted the same principle of learning temporal dynamics within a compact latent space obtained from pretrained image autoencoders, achieving significant reductions in memory and computational overhead while maintaining temporal coherence [38; 39]. Similarly, structured pruning methods developed specifically for LDMs exploit the latent representation to evaluate the impact of pruning decisions in a task-agnostic manner, enabling inference-time speedups without catastrophic quality degradation [40].

In summary, latent diffusion models represent a pivotal architectural innovation that reconciles the generative power of diffusion processes with practical deployment constraints. By confining the stochastic denoising trajectory to a semantically structured, low-dimensional latent space and coupling it with cross-attention-based conditioning mechanisms, LDMs established the foundation upon which most modern large-scale text-to-image systems are built. Critically, these cross-attention interfaces serve as the natural entry point for the vision-language representations explored in the following section, where CLIP embeddings provide the semantic conditioning signal that bridges natural language descriptions and the structured latent spaces in which diffusion models operate.

### 1.5 Vision-Language Models and Text Conditioning

Vision-language models have played a transformative role in enabling text-conditioned image synthesis, with Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP) emerging as the dominant paradigm for learning joint representations of images and text. Building naturally upon the latent diffusion framework discussed in the previous section, CLIP provides the semantic conditioning signal that bridges natural language descriptions and the structured latent spaces in which modern generative models operate. CLIP is trained on massive collections of image-text pairs using a contrastive objective that brings semantically aligned image and text embeddings close together in a shared latent space while pushing apart mismatched pairs [41]. This contrastive pretraining at web scale endows CLIP with rich, semantically meaningful representations that generalize remarkably well across diverse visual concepts and linguistic descriptions, making it exceptionally well-suited as a conditioning signal for generative models.

The use of CLIP embeddings as conditioning signals in diffusion models has become a foundational design choice in modern text-to-image systems. In the DALL-E 2 framework, a two-stage architecture is employed wherein a prior model first generates a CLIP image embedding from a given text caption, after which a diffusion-based decoder synthesizes the final image conditioned on this embedding [41]. This explicit generation of image representations in the CLIP embedding space improves image diversity while preserving photorealism and caption alignment, and also enables zero-shot image manipulation by interpolating or editing embeddings in the joint space. Crucially, this design complements the cross-attention-based conditioning mechanisms introduced by latent diffusion models, as CLIP embeddings provide semantically structured representations that can be directly injected into the denoising backbone.

Beyond direct embedding conditioning, CLIP has been leveraged for classifier guidance during the diffusion sampling process, where the gradient of the CLIP similarity score between the current noisy image and the target text prompt is used to steer denoising steps toward semantically aligned outputs. This CLIP-guided generation approach provides a flexible, training-free mechanism for improving prompt adherence. Models such as clip2latent further explore conditioning a diffusion model on CLIP embeddings to sample latent vectors of a pretrained generative model, demonstrating that CLIP's aligned image-text embedding space can bridge text-driven synthesis without requiring paired text-labeled training data [42].

CLIP's role extends beyond pure conditioning; its internal representations encode rich semantic structure that has been exploited for tasks including attribute binding, compositional understanding, and style transfer within generative pipelines [43]. The eDiff-I model, for instance, incorporates both CLIP text and CLIP image embeddings alongside T5 text embeddings as conditioning inputs, demonstrating that CLIP image embeddings uniquely enable intuitive style transfer from a reference image to generated outputs [44]. Scene-graph-guided diffusion approaches such as SceneGenie further leverage CLIP semantic features to enrich structural representations with semantic embeddings, guiding the generation process to produce geometrically and semantically accurate compositions [45].

Despite its widespread adoption, CLIP-based conditioning is not without limitations. CLIP text encoders have been shown to struggle with compositional visio-linguistic reasoning, often failing to correctly bind attributes to objects or to represent fine-grained relational structures in complex prompts [46; 43]. These shortcomings become particularly pronounced when handling dense, multi-object descriptions or nuanced relational prompts that demand deep linguistic understanding beyond the coarse semantic alignment that CLIP's contrastive objective provides. These limitations have motivated the integration of richer text encoders such as T5 and BERT alongside CLIP embeddings in state-of-the-art models — a direction explored in depth in the following section. Nonetheless, CLIP's joint embedding space remains a cornerstone of text-conditioned image synthesis, providing the semantic bridge that connects natural language descriptions to high-quality visual outputs and establishing the representational foundation upon which more expressive language model integrations are built.

### 1.6 Large Language Models as Text Encoders for Image Generation

While CLIP-based text encoders have served as the dominant conditioning backbone for early text-to-image diffusion models, their relatively shallow linguistic representations impose notable limitations on semantic understanding, compositional reasoning, and the faithful rendering of complex, multi-object textual descriptions. As discussed in the preceding section, CLIP's contrastive pretraining objective provides a powerful but coarse semantic alignment between vision and language — one that excels at bridging modalities at a high level yet struggles with the fine-grained linguistic structure demanded by dense and relationally complex prompts. This has motivated researchers to explore the integration of large language models (LLMs) — most prominently T5 and BERT — as richer and more expressive text encoders within diffusion-based image synthesis pipelines.

Models such as BERT [47] are pretrained via masked language modeling on large corpora, enabling them to capture deep bidirectional contextual representations of text. In contrast to the contrastive pretraining objective of CLIP, which aligns image and text embeddings at a coarse semantic level, BERT-style encoders excel at fine-grained linguistic understanding including syntactic structure, entity relationships, and attribute-object binding. The T5 family of encoder-decoder models [10] extends this capability further through sequence-to-sequence pretraining, and has been shown to provide surprisingly effective text encodings for image synthesis tasks even without any image-specific training.

A landmark demonstration of this approach is Imagen [10], which replaces CLIP's text encoder with a frozen T5-XXL language model. Imagen's authors discovered that scaling the language model component yielded substantially greater improvements in both sample fidelity and text-image alignment than scaling the image diffusion model itself — a finding that underscores the critical role that linguistic representation quality plays in the overall generation pipeline. T5 encodings carry rich semantic and syntactic information derived from vast text-only corpora, enabling the downstream diffusion model to better resolve complex prompts involving multiple objects, nuanced attributes, and intricate relational descriptions.

Beyond T5, the differences between BERT-style and CLIP-style text encoders have been systematically analyzed [48], revealing that while CLIP text encoders demonstrate a unique cross-modal associative ability termed "synesthesia," BERT-style encoders outperform them on general text understanding benchmarks. This suggests a complementary relationship between the two encoder families: CLIP embeddings are naturally suited to bridging vision and language at a high level, while LLM-based encoders provide the depth of linguistic comprehension necessary for handling dense and structurally complex prompts.

Recent work has sought to combine the strengths of both paradigms. For instance, ELLA [49] introduces a Timestep-Aware Semantic Connector (TSC) that bridges a frozen large language model with a pretrained diffusion model without modifying either component. By dynamically extracting timestep-dependent semantic features from the LLM and injecting them into the diffusion model's cross-attention layers — the same cross-attention mechanism that forms the backbone of conditioning in models such as Stable Diffusion — ELLA enables the base model to leverage richer linguistic representations at each stage of the denoising process. This design is particularly beneficial for dense prompts involving multiple objects, diverse attributes, and complex relationships, where CLIP encoders often fail to maintain accurate correspondence between text and image content. The eDiff-I model [44] similarly leverages a combination of T5 text embeddings, CLIP text embeddings, and CLIP image embeddings, showing that different embeddings contribute complementary semantic signals to the synthesis process and that neither LLM-based nor CLIP-based encoders alone suffice for capturing the full richness of language-guided generation.

Similarly, adapter-based approaches such as SUR-adapter [50] demonstrate that LLM knowledge can be distilled into lightweight adapter modules that enhance the semantic understanding capabilities of pretrained diffusion models without full retraining. By aligning concise narrative prompts to richer keyword-based descriptions via knowledge distillation from LLMs, SUR-adapter enables models to handle natural language inputs that deviate from the keyword-centric prompt formats typically used during training.

Taken together, these developments highlight a broader trend in the field: the recognition that high-quality text-to-image generation requires not merely visual generative capacity but also deep and robust natural language understanding. The integration of LLMs as text encoders represents a critical step toward diffusion models that can faithfully render the full semantic richness of human language, including long-form descriptions, complex compositional scenes, and nuanced contextual relationships that remain challenging for CLIP-based conditioning alone. These richer linguistic representations naturally set the stage for the conditioning and guidance mechanisms discussed in the following section, where the quality of the conditioning signal directly determines how effectively sampling-time interventions — such as classifier-free guidance and cross-attention injection — can steer the generation process toward semantically aligned and visually coherent outputs.

### 1.7 Conditioning and Guidance Mechanisms

Conditioning and guidance mechanisms are central to the practical utility of diffusion models, enabling the generation process to be steered by external signals such as class labels, text prompts, images, or other structured information. Building upon the architectural and training foundations discussed in the preceding subsections — including the role of large language models as expressive text encoders — two foundational paradigms have emerged as the dominant approaches for incorporating such conditioning signals: classifier guidance and classifier-free guidance.

**Classifier Guidance.** The first systematic approach to conditional image synthesis with diffusion models introduced classifier guidance [51], wherein the gradients of a separately trained, noise-aware image classifier are used to bias the denoising trajectory toward samples consistent with a desired class label. Formally, the score function is augmented with the gradient of the log-likelihood under the classifier, allowing the generation to trade off sample diversity for fidelity. While powerful, this approach requires training a dedicated classifier that can operate reliably on noisy intermediate samples at all timesteps, incurring additional computational overhead and limiting adaptability to new conditions without retraining. Subsequent work has sought to improve the practicality of classifier-based guidance. For instance, leveraging off-the-shelf, pretrained classifiers through careful pre-conditioning calibration has been shown to further boost the performance of state-of-the-art diffusion models without additional training cost [52]. Plug-and-play strategies using parameter-efficient fine-tuning and data-free knowledge transfer have also been proposed to make classifier guidance more flexible across diverse conditioning tasks [53]. Training-free energy-based guidance frameworks such as FreeDoM [54] extend classifier-based concepts by constructing time-independent energy functions from off-the-shelf networks, allowing conditioning on a broader set of signals — including face detection, segmentation, and other domain-specific cues — without requiring any training procedure tailored to the diffusion model. Universal guidance algorithms [55] push this direction further, enabling diffusion models to be controlled by arbitrary guidance modalities — including segmentation masks, face recognition signals, and object detectors — without retraining any task-specific components.

Despite these advances, training-free guidance methods face fundamental limitations. Theoretical analysis reveals that they are more susceptible to adversarial gradients and exhibit slower convergence compared to classifier guidance approaches [56], highlighting the inherent tension between flexibility and reliability.

**Classifier-Free Guidance.** To circumvent the need for a separate classifier, classifier-free guidance (CFG) [57] jointly trains a conditional diffusion model alongside an unconditional counterpart by randomly dropping the conditioning signal during training. At inference, the score estimates from both models are combined using a guidance scale parameter $w$, where larger values push generations toward higher fidelity at the cost of reduced diversity. CFG has become the de facto standard in large-scale text-to-image systems such as GLIDE [58], offering a computationally simpler alternative to classifier guidance that requires no separate model. Theoretical analyses of the guidance mechanism [59] confirm that stronger guidance indeed boosts classification confidence while simultaneously reducing the differential entropy of the output distribution, providing a rigorous foundation for the empirically observed fidelity-diversity tradeoff.

However, CFG is not without its own challenges. Applying a uniform guidance scale globally across the entire image space can result in spatial inconsistency, since different semantic regions may require different degrees of text-guided amplification [60]. Semantic-aware CFG methods address this by adaptively adjusting guidance strength across distinct semantic regions derived from cross-attention maps. Additionally, a discrepancy between training and sampling behaviors under CFG has been identified, with high guidance scales often causing out-of-distribution artifacts and mode collapse; refined training objectives have been proposed to better align training with the intended conditional sampling process [61]. Further work has studied the dynamics of CFG weight schedulers [62], finding that simple monotonically increasing schedules consistently improve generation performance with minimal implementation cost.

From an efficiency standpoint, applying CFG at every denoising step is redundant, as the gap between conditional and unconditional predictions narrows in later timesteps. Adaptive guidance strategies [63] identify and exploit this redundancy to reduce unnecessary network evaluations, achieving computational savings of up to 25% with negligible quality degradation. CFG can also be distilled into a single model, substantially reducing inference cost while retaining the quality benefits [64]. These efficiency considerations naturally connect to the broader challenges of accelerating and deploying diffusion models discussed later in the survey.

**Beyond Binary Conditioning: Cross-Attention and Embedding Injection.** Beyond scalar-scaled guidance, modern text-to-image diffusion models incorporate rich multimodal conditioning through cross-attention layers, where text or image embeddings are injected into intermediate feature maps of the denoising network. This mechanism, central to models like Stable Diffusion, enables the network to spatially attend to different parts of the conditioning signal at each layer, facilitating fine-grained semantic alignment — a capability that directly complements the richer linguistic representations provided by LLM-based text encoders discussed previously. Multi-condition extensions have been explored, including simultaneous conditioning on both semantic masks and attribute vectors via cross-attention [65], demonstrating that cross-attention can naturally accommodate multiple heterogeneous conditioning streams.

Additional conditioning strategies include embedding concatenation along the channel or sequence dimension, self-attention guidance [66] that uses the model's own internal attention maps to improve structural coherence without relying on external classifiers, and perturbed-attention guidance [67] that degrades sample structure by substituting self-attention maps with identity matrices to provide a contrastive guidance signal, enabling high-quality generation even in unconditional and downstream restoration settings.

**Condition-Annealed Sampling.** A complementary perspective on conditioning recognizes that while high guidance scales improve fidelity, they also suppress diversity — especially for models trained on small datasets or evaluated at high guidance strengths. Condition-Annealed Diffusion Sampling (CADS) [68] addresses this by adding scheduled, monotonically decreasing Gaussian noise to the conditioning vector during inference, thereby softening the conditioning signal early in the denoising process to encourage exploration and then strengthening it toward the end to ensure alignment. This plug-and-play approach improves diversity with minimal loss of sample quality and achieves state-of-the-art FID scores on class-conditional ImageNet generation.

**Reward-Based and Reinforcement Learning Guidance.** An emerging class of methods frames conditioning as an optimization problem, using reward signals to guide or fine-tune the generation process. Reward-guided diffusion models [69] leverage reinforcement learning to align the diffusion sampling trajectory with user-specified objectives, while gradient-guided frameworks [70] provide a theoretical link between guided sampling and regularized optimization, showing that guidance acts as a prior that constrains generated samples to lie near the training data distribution. End-to-end optimization of diffusion latents using accurate classifier gradients computed on truly generated pixels — rather than one-step approximations — further improves the precision of guidance [71]. Fine-tuning diffusion models as entropy-regularized control problems [72] explicitly addresses the reward collapse phenomenon, promoting diverse and high-quality outputs even when reward functions are imperfect approximations of true objectives. As will be explored further in subsequent sections on text-image alignment and semantic understanding, reward-based approaches are increasingly being combined with human preference feedback to push generation quality beyond what conditioning signals alone can achieve.

**Summary.** Collectively, these conditioning and guidance mechanisms define a rich design space for controlling text-to-image diffusion models. The central trade-off between sample fidelity and diversity — introduced by classifier guidance and refined by classifier-free guidance — remains a core tension in the field, and one that recurs across the efficiency, controllability, and alignment topics addressed in later sections of this survey. Subsequent innovations in spatial-aware guidance, cross-attention injection, training-free plug-and-play methods, and reward-based optimization all contribute complementary tools for navigating this trade-off, enabling flexible, high-quality, and controllable image synthesis across a wide spectrum of applications.

### 1.8 Motivation, Scope, and Organization of the Survey

The field of text-to-image generation has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past several years, with diffusion-based models rapidly displacing earlier generative paradigms and achieving unprecedented levels of photorealism, semantic fidelity, and creative versatility. The sheer velocity of progress — marked by a continuous stream of architectural innovations, novel conditioning mechanisms, efficiency breakthroughs, and expanding application domains — makes it increasingly difficult for researchers and practitioners to maintain a coherent and up-to-date understanding of the landscape. At the same time, the societal implications of these systems, spanning issues of bias, intellectual property, content safety, and equitable access, demand careful and systematic attention. The need for a comprehensive, well-structured survey that integrates these diverse threads is therefore both timely and pressing.

This survey is motivated by three overarching observations. First, while individual components of text-to-image diffusion research — such as sampling acceleration, controllable generation, or personalization — have been addressed in focused reviews, no single work currently spans the full breadth of the field from foundational theory to real-world deployment and societal impact. Second, the rapid proliferation of models, methods, and benchmarks has created a fragmented literature in which connections between contributions are not always made explicit, hindering cumulative scientific progress. Third, as diffusion models are increasingly deployed in consequential settings, a rigorous treatment of evaluation standards, safety considerations, and responsible deployment practices is essential for guiding future work.

In terms of scope, this survey covers the complete lifecycle of text-to-image diffusion models: from their theoretical foundations in denoising diffusion probabilistic models, score-based generative modeling, and latent diffusion to the architectural and training innovations that define state-of-the-art systems; from techniques for improving controllability, semantic alignment, and personalization to the broader downstream applications they enable; and from quantitative evaluation and benchmarking to safety, interpretability, and open research challenges.

The remainder of this survey is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys core architectural designs and training strategies, encompassing UNet-based and transformer-based denoising networks, cascaded and rectified flow frameworks, ensemble-of-experts designs, and landmark large-scale models, together with classifier-free guidance, reinforcement learning from human feedback, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Section 3 addresses efficiency, acceleration, and deployment, reviewing sampling acceleration methods, diffusion-GAN hybrids, knowledge distillation, pruning, quantization, and mobile deployment strategies. Section 4 examines controllable and compositional generation, covering layout-guided synthesis, cross-attention manipulation, large language model-based spatial planning, scene graph conditioning, and unified multi-condition frameworks. Section 5 focuses on text-image alignment and semantic understanding, discussing enhanced text encoders, attention manipulation, prompt engineering, attribute binding, and human feedback-driven alignment. Section 6 reviews personalization, editing, and image manipulation techniques, including subject-driven generation, identity-consistent synthesis, text-driven inversion, instruction-based editing, and concept adaptation. Section 7 explores the broad landscape of downstream applications enabled by diffusion models, spanning text-to-3D generation, novel view synthesis, vector graphics, visual perception, semantic correspondence, text rendering, story visualization, video generation, data augmentation, and cross-modal retrieval. Finally, Section 8 addresses evaluation, safety, and future directions, covering quantitative metrics and benchmarks, compositional fidelity evaluation, human preference alignment, social bias, ethical concerns, interpretability, robustness, concept ablation, and an outlook on open challenges.

Throughout, we aim to make explicit the connections and dependencies between these topics — for instance, how advances in conditioning and guidance mechanisms (Section 2) enable richer control that benefits both alignment (Section 5) and controllability (Section 4), or how efficiency techniques (Section 3) are prerequisite for the mobile and real-world deployment scenarios that raise safety and fairness concerns (Section 8). By providing this integrated perspective, the survey is intended to serve both as an entry point for newcomers to the field and as a structured reference for experienced researchers seeking to situate their work within the broader landscape of text-to-image diffusion model research.

## 2 Core Architectures and Training Strategies

### 2.1 UNet-Based Denoising Architectures

## 2.1 UNet-Based Denoising Architectures

The UNet architecture has established itself as the dominant backbone for denoising networks in text-to-image diffusion models, owing to its effective combination of local feature extraction and global context aggregation through encoder-decoder pathways with skip connections. The core design employs a hierarchical structure in which spatial resolution is progressively downsampled in the encoder and subsequently upsampled in the decoder, with skip connections bridging corresponding resolution levels to preserve fine-grained spatial information throughout the denoising process. This architectural foundation laid the groundwork for a rich body of research exploring increasingly sophisticated conditioning mechanisms, scalability strategies, and hybrid designs, setting the stage for the architectural evolution that ultimately motivated the transition toward transformer-based alternatives.

At the heart of these architectures lie residual blocks that facilitate stable gradient flow during training, enabling the stacking of many layers without degradation. Self-attention mechanisms are interleaved with these convolutional blocks at multiple resolution levels, allowing the network to capture long-range dependencies within image feature maps. Crucially for text-to-image generation, cross-attention layers are embedded within the UNet blocks to inject textual conditioning signals: text embeddings produced by language encoders are projected into key and value representations, while image feature maps serve as queries, enabling the network to dynamically attend to relevant textual concepts at each spatial location and denoising timestep. This tight coupling between visual and linguistic representations within the UNet framework forms one of its most distinctive and consequential design choices.

A landmark instantiation of this paradigm is the Efficient UNet introduced in Imagen [10], which demonstrates that scaling the language model encoder—specifically large T5 transformers—has a disproportionately large impact on generation quality compared to scaling the image diffusion backbone alone. The Efficient UNet design in Imagen incorporates architectural refinements such as shifting model parameters and compute toward lower resolution feature maps, which reduces memory and computation without sacrificing image fidelity, and employs a cascaded pipeline in which multiple UNet-based models operate at progressively higher resolutions to achieve photorealistic outputs [10]. This cascaded multi-stage approach reflects a broader recognition that no single UNet model can optimally address all scales of image structure simultaneously, motivating hierarchical compositions of specialized denoisers.

Beyond single-stage models, hierarchical feature reuse strategies have emerged as a compelling direction for improving both the quality and efficiency of UNet-based diffusion models. These strategies recognize that intermediate feature representations computed at higher UNet levels capture coarse semantic structures that remain relatively stable across adjacent denoising timesteps, enabling their reuse or caching to reduce redundant computation. Such temporal redundancy exploitation is at the core of inference acceleration methods that cache high-level UNet features across denoising steps and reuse them at lower computational cost, thereby substantially reducing the number of full forward passes required without significant degradation in generation quality. These efficiency considerations would later become central motivations for exploring alternative architectures with more favorable scaling properties.

Text conditioning via cross-attention has been further analyzed and refined across numerous model families. Works such as [10] highlight the importance of conditioning on rich textual representations, demonstrating that purely text-pretrained large language models can serve as highly effective encoders for diffusion-based synthesis. The interaction between text embeddings and UNet feature maps through cross-attention allows for fine-grained semantic alignment between textual descriptions and spatial image regions, addressing a key challenge in complex multi-object and multi-attribute generation scenarios. However, the inherently local receptive fields of convolutional operations and the constraints of fixed multi-scale architectures impose limits on the model's capacity to reason about global scene structure and complex compositional relationships—limitations that directly motivate the development of transformer-based denoising architectures discussed in the following subsection.

Overall, the UNet-based denoising backbone remains a foundational element across the landscape of text-to-image diffusion models [9], with ongoing innovations refining its architectural components, conditioning mechanisms, and computational efficiency to push the boundaries of photorealism, semantic fidelity, and practical deployability. Understanding the strengths and limitations of UNet-based designs is essential context for appreciating the architectural motivations and trade-offs introduced by the transformer-based alternatives that have increasingly come to challenge their dominance.

### 2.2 Transformer-Based Denoising Architectures

## 2.2 Transformer-Based Denoising Architectures

The dominant role of UNet-based convolutional backbones in diffusion model design has increasingly been challenged by the emergence of transformer-based architectures, which offer compelling advantages in terms of scalability, global context modeling, and parameter efficiency. This paradigm shift draws inspiration from the broader success of Vision Transformers (ViTs) in discriminative tasks and adapts their self-attention mechanisms to the generative denoising setting. Whereas UNet designs rely on hierarchical convolutional feature extraction with cross-attention conditioning at fixed resolution levels, transformer-based denoisers treat image features as sequences of patch tokens and apply self-attention globally, directly addressing the limitations of local receptive fields and rigid multi-scale architectures identified in the preceding subsection.

A landmark contribution in this direction is the Diffusion Transformer (DiT), which replaces the conventional UNet backbone entirely with a transformer operating on patchified latent representations. DiT demonstrates that transformer-based denoisers scale more predictably with model size and computational budget, achieving state-of-the-art FID scores on class-conditional ImageNet benchmarks and establishing clear scaling laws for diffusion model performance. The success of DiT validated the hypothesis that the inductive biases of convolutional architectures are not strictly necessary for high-quality image synthesis, and that the global receptive field of transformers can be highly beneficial for capturing long-range dependencies in complex scenes. Crucially, DiT operates within the latent space defined by a pretrained autoencoder — a design choice that directly anticipates the latent diffusion framework discussed in the following subsection — demonstrating how transformer-based denoising and compressed latent representations are naturally complementary.

Building upon the ViT foundation, DiffiT introduces time-dependent self-attention modules that allow the transformer to modulate its behavior dynamically across different denoising timesteps. This design acknowledges the heterogeneous nature of the diffusion process, where early timesteps require coarse semantic reasoning and later timesteps demand fine-grained detail refinement. By conditioning attention computations on the current timestep embedding, DiffiT achieves improved sample quality and better alignment between the model's inductive capacity and the demands of each stage of generation.

IU-ViT (Improved UNet-like Vision Transformer) represents a hybrid strategy that seeks to retain the multi-scale feature hierarchy of UNet architectures while incorporating transformer blocks at multiple resolution levels. This hierarchical transformer design enables efficient processing of both local and global image features, combining the spatial locality benefits of convolutional downsampling and upsampling paths with the expressive power of multi-head self-attention. Such hybrid approaches are particularly valuable for high-resolution image generation, where purely flat transformer architectures face significant computational challenges due to the quadratic complexity of self-attention with respect to sequence length. In this sense, IU-ViT bridges the architectural principles of the UNet paradigm discussed previously and the fully attention-based designs exemplified by DiT.

ASCEND and related architectures further explore efficiency improvements in transformer-based diffusion models by introducing sparse attention patterns, linear attention approximations, and token pruning strategies to reduce the computational overhead of full self-attention while maintaining generation quality. These methods are especially important as the community pushes toward higher-resolution synthesis and real-time applications, directly addressing one of the core practical challenges that motivates the use of compressed latent representations in the frameworks examined in the following subsection.

A key advantage of transformer-based architectures over their convolutional counterparts lies in their superior scalability. Unlike UNets, whose architectural hyperparameters such as channel widths and skip connection structures require careful manual tuning, transformers can be scaled more uniformly by increasing depth, width, and the number of attention heads, benefiting from well-established scaling laws. This property makes transformer-based diffusion models particularly amenable to large-scale training regimes and enables more predictable performance improvements with increased computational resources — a consideration that aligns closely with the efficiency motivations underlying latent-space diffusion approaches.

From the perspective of text conditioning, transformer architectures naturally integrate cross-attention layers for injecting textual embeddings into the denoising process, facilitating seamless alignment between language and visual representations. The bidirectional attention mechanism in transformers allows each image patch token to attend to all text tokens simultaneously, enabling richer and more accurate semantic grounding compared to the sequential feature fusion typical of convolutional UNets. This capability becomes especially pronounced in complex compositional prompts involving multiple objects, fine-grained attributes, and spatial relationships — precisely the scenarios where the global reasoning limitations of convolutional UNets are most apparent.

Despite their advantages, transformer-based denoisers face several practical challenges. The quadratic complexity of self-attention with respect to image resolution poses memory and computation bottlenecks for high-resolution synthesis. Various remedies have been proposed, including windowed attention, axial attention, and hierarchical patchification strategies. The lack of built-in translation equivariance, a property inherent to convolutional networks, also necessitates careful positional encoding design to ensure spatial coherence in generated outputs. Notably, many of these computational challenges are substantially mitigated when transformer-based denoisers operate within a compressed latent space rather than directly in pixel space, reinforcing the close interdependence between denoising architecture design and latent representation design that characterizes the modern text-to-image generation landscape.

Overall, the transition toward transformer-based denoising architectures represents one of the most significant structural shifts in the text-to-image diffusion model landscape, offering improved scalability, flexible conditioning mechanisms, and competitive or superior generation quality relative to convolutional counterparts, while posing new challenges in computational efficiency that continue to motivate active research [15]. The architectural insights developed in this context — particularly around scalable attention mechanisms and latent-space operation — set the stage for the detailed examination of latent diffusion frameworks and their compression backbones in the subsection that follows.

### 2.3 Latent Space Representations and Autoencoders

Latent diffusion models (LDMs) represent a pivotal architectural shift in text-to-image synthesis by relocating the computationally intensive diffusion process from high-dimensional pixel space into a compact, semantically rich latent space learned by a pretrained autoencoder. This design choice dramatically reduces memory and computational requirements while preserving the perceptual quality of generated images, enabling practical large-scale training and deployment. Understanding the structure and design of these latent representations is essential context for appreciating the broader architectural choices — including transformer-based denoisers and cascaded pipelines — that define the modern text-to-image landscape.

### Variational Autoencoders as Compression Backbones

The most widely adopted compression backbone in latent diffusion frameworks is the Variational Autoencoder (VAE). A VAE consists of an encoder $\mathcal{E}$ that maps an input image $x \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$ into a lower-dimensional latent representation $z = \mathcal{E}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times w \times c}$, and a decoder $\mathcal{D}$ that reconstructs the image from the latent code, i.e., $\tilde{x} = \mathcal{D}(z)$. The spatial downsampling factor $f = H/h = W/w$ is a critical design parameter: common choices range from $f=4$ to $f=16$, with larger factors providing greater computational savings at the potential cost of fine-grained detail in reconstruction. The VAE is trained with a combination of reconstruction loss (e.g., $L_1$ or perceptual loss) and a KL-divergence regularization term that encourages the latent distribution to remain close to a standard Gaussian prior, ensuring that the latent space is smooth and continuous — properties that are essential for the diffusion process to operate effectively within it.

Once the autoencoder is trained, its weights are frozen and the diffusion model is trained exclusively within the latent space. This two-stage training strategy effectively decouples perceptual compression from the generative modeling task, allowing each component to be optimized independently. The quality of the autoencoder directly governs the upper bound on generative fidelity: any information lost during encoding cannot be recovered by the diffusion model, making autoencoder design a critical bottleneck for the overall system.

### VQ-Regularized Autoencoders

An alternative to continuous VAE-based compression is the use of Vector Quantization (VQ)-regularized autoencoders, as popularized by VQ-VAE and VQ-GAN architectures. In VQ-regularized designs, the encoder output is quantized to the nearest entry in a learned discrete codebook before being passed to the decoder. This discretization introduces a fundamentally different inductive bias: the latent space is no longer continuous but consists of sequences of discrete tokens drawn from a finite vocabulary. Diffusion models operating over such discrete latent codes can leverage transformer-based architectures naturally suited for sequence modeling — a connection that directly motivates the transformer-based denoising designs discussed in the preceding section — enabling direct application of autoregressive or masked diffusion approaches.

The trade-off between VQ and KL-regularized autoencoders is nuanced. VQ-based codes tend to produce sharper and more structured latent representations that can be beneficial for downstream generation tasks requiring precise semantic control. However, they introduce quantization artifacts and can suffer from codebook collapse, where only a small fraction of codebook entries are utilized. KL-regularized VAEs, by contrast, yield smoother latent manifolds better suited for Gaussian diffusion processes but may produce blurrier reconstructions at high compression ratios.

### Impact of Latent Space Quality on Generative Fidelity

The structure and quality of the learned latent space exert profound influence on multiple dimensions of generative model performance. Reconstruction fidelity sets the ceiling on sample quality: if the autoencoder introduces significant perceptual distortions, even a perfectly trained diffusion model cannot compensate. Smoothness and regularity of the latent manifold affect training stability and convergence speed of the diffusion model, as highly irregular or multi-modal latent distributions complicate score estimation [25; 19]. Semantic organization of the latent space — i.e., the degree to which semantically similar images are mapped to nearby latent codes — influences the model's ability to perform meaningful interpolation, controlled editing, and concept disentanglement.

Perceptual and adversarial training objectives used when training the autoencoder (e.g., patch-based discriminators and LPIPS perceptual loss) have been found to substantially improve the perceptual quality of reconstructions compared to using pixel-wise losses alone. These objectives encourage the decoder to prioritize the preservation of high-frequency textures and fine structural details that are perceptually salient, even when the latent representation is highly compressed. The inclusion of such objectives is particularly important at aggressive compression ratios where pixel-space losses alone would produce overly smooth outputs.

### Efficiency and Scalability Considerations

Operating the diffusion process in a compressed latent space provides efficiency gains that scale super-linearly with the spatial downsampling factor. For a model with downsampling factor $f=8$, the latent spatial dimensions are 64-fold smaller in area than the pixel-space representation, reducing the number of attention operations — which scale quadratically with spatial resolution — by a corresponding factor. This efficiency gain is what makes it feasible to train high-resolution text-to-image models on standard GPU clusters, and to perform inference at interactive speeds.

Furthermore, the compact latent representation enables the use of more expressive and parameter-intensive denoising architectures within a fixed computational budget, since the denoising network operates on lower-dimensional inputs. This allows practitioners to allocate more model capacity toward learning complex text-image correspondences via cross-attention mechanisms rather than toward low-level pixel reconstruction. Notably, this efficiency advantage also underpins cascaded diffusion frameworks, where intermediate latent representations — rather than full-resolution pixel outputs — serve as the basis for progressive refinement across multiple stages.

### Semantic Interpretability of Latent Codes

Beyond raw efficiency and fidelity, the semantic structure of the latent space has important implications for controllability and interpretability. Latent codes that exhibit disentangled or geometrically meaningful organization facilitate targeted editing: modifying a small region of the latent code should ideally correspond to a semantically coherent and spatially localized change in the generated image. Training the autoencoder with sufficient regularization and diversity in training data helps prevent posterior collapse and promotes the encoding of diverse semantic content across the full dimensionality of the latent space.

The relationship between latent space structure and downstream generative model performance mirrors insights from the score-based generative modeling literature, where the smoothness and topology of the data distribution directly affect the accuracy of score estimation and the quality of generated samples [25; 27]. In the latent diffusion setting, the autoencoder effectively defines a new "data distribution" over which the diffusion model operates, making the design of the compression stage inseparable from the design of the generative stage.

In summary, the design of latent space representations — including the choice between VAE and VQ-regularized autoencoders, the compression ratio, the training objectives, and the resulting semantic organization — constitutes one of the most consequential architectural decisions in building high-performance text-to-image diffusion systems. These design choices directly shape the capabilities of transformer-based denoisers and cascaded generation pipelines that build upon them, establishing the latent space as the foundational substrate through which scalability, efficiency, and semantic richness are simultaneously pursued. Careful co-design of the autoencoder and the diffusion model remains an active area of research, with ongoing work exploring jointly optimized compression-generation pipelines and adaptive latent spaces that can dynamically adjust their structure to the complexity of the content being synthesized.

### 2.4 Cascaded Diffusion Frameworks

Cascaded diffusion frameworks address the challenge of high-resolution image synthesis by decomposing the generation process into a sequential pipeline of multiple diffusion models, each operating at progressively increasing spatial resolutions. Rather than attempting to directly synthesize a full-resolution image in a single forward pass, these architectures chain a low-resolution base model with one or more super-resolution diffusion models that refine and upsample the outputs of preceding stages. This divide-and-conquer strategy substantially reduces the computational burden at each individual stage while enabling the overall pipeline to achieve photorealistic, high-fidelity outputs that would be prohibitively expensive to produce in a single-stage pixel-space model. As established in the preceding discussion of latent space representations, the compact intermediate codes produced by autoencoders provide a natural foundation for such staged refinement — and indeed, cascaded frameworks can be understood as extending the latent diffusion philosophy by introducing multiple levels of progressive resolution rather than a single compression-decompression cycle.

The foundational motivation for cascaded approaches stems from the observation that modeling the full joint distribution over high-dimensional pixel spaces is extraordinarily demanding in terms of both memory and computation. By first generating a semantically coherent low-resolution image that captures coarse structure, compositional layout, and global semantics, and then delegating fine-grained texture synthesis and detail refinement to dedicated super-resolution stages, cascaded pipelines achieve a practical separation of responsibilities. Each stage can be independently optimized, conditioned on text embeddings and the upsampled output of the preceding model, leading to improved training stability and generation quality.

Landmark systems exemplifying this paradigm include Imagen, which employs a base 64×64 pixel diffusion model followed by two text-conditioned super-resolution diffusion models that progressively upsample to 256×256 and then 1024×1024 resolution. Imagen introduced the Efficient UNet architecture and demonstrated that scaling the text encoder, specifically leveraging large frozen language models such as T5, yields substantial gains in text-image alignment and photorealism. Similarly, DALL-E 2 adopts a hierarchical structure in which a CLIP image embedding is first generated from a text prompt via a prior network, and then a base diffusion model synthesizes a 64×64 image conditioned on this embedding, followed by cascaded upsampling diffusion models reaching 1024×1024. This design leverages the rich joint vision-language representations learned by CLIP to anchor generation in a semantically meaningful embedding space before committing to pixel-level details.

The Wuerstchen architecture [36] takes the cascaded concept further by introducing an extremely compact semantic latent representation that compresses images to a very high spatial compression ratio. Diffusion is performed in this highly compressed latent space, and the synthesized latent codes are subsequently decoded and refined through additional stages, dramatically reducing computational requirements while maintaining competitive image quality. This approach demonstrates that the intermediate representation used to guide cascaded stages need not be a low-resolution pixel image; instead, a learned semantic compression can serve as an equally effective intermediate signal with far greater efficiency — echoing the broader insight from latent diffusion models that operating in compressed representations yields compounding benefits across both training and inference.

More recent cascaded frameworks such as YaART apply careful noise schedule and resolution stage tuning within cascaded pipelines to improve perceptual quality and consistency across scales. A recurring design principle across these systems is the use of noise augmentation at the conditioning input of each super-resolution stage, which prevents the higher-resolution models from being overfit to artifacts introduced by the preceding stage and encourages robust generation under imperfect upsampled inputs.

A key advantage of cascaded diffusion frameworks is their modularity: each stage can be trained, evaluated, and refined independently, enabling targeted improvements in either semantic coherence at low resolution or textural fidelity at high resolution without retraining the entire pipeline. Furthermore, the conditioning mechanisms at each stage, typically implemented via cross-attention over text embeddings as in latent diffusion models [35], allow rich textual guidance to remain active throughout the entire cascade, helping to maintain prompt-image alignment even as spatial resolution increases. Collectively, cascaded diffusion architectures represent one of the most effective and widely adopted strategies for achieving scalable, high-resolution, photorealistic text-to-image synthesis — and their progressive, multi-stage structure provides important context for understanding the complementary innovations in generative trajectory design, such as rectified flow, that seek to make each individual stage within such pipelines more efficient and semantically grounded.

### 2.5 Rectified Flow Models and Advanced Generative Formulations

Rectified flow represents a compelling alternative generative modeling formulation that departs from the conventional diffusion process by connecting data distributions and noise distributions through straight-line trajectories in latent space. Unlike standard diffusion models that follow curved stochastic paths governed by Markov chains, rectified flow learns a velocity field that transports samples along linear interpolations between the data and noise endpoints. This straightening of trajectories has important practical consequences: straighter paths are easier to approximate with fewer discretization steps during inference, leading to faster and more accurate sampling without sacrificing generation quality.

A central motivation behind rectified flow is the observation that conventional diffusion training noise schedules may not be optimally calibrated for transformer-based architectures operating at high resolutions. By rethinking the noise sampling strategy during training — specifically by weighting noise levels more uniformly or emphasizing intermediate signal-to-noise ratios — models trained under the rectified flow framework can learn more balanced representations across all synthesis stages. This improved training noise sampling strategy has been shown to facilitate better convergence and generalization, particularly when scaling model capacity and dataset size.

Whereas cascaded diffusion frameworks decompose the generation process across spatial resolution stages, and ensemble-of-experts approaches partition it across denoising timesteps, the rectified flow framework pursues a complementary strategy: reformulating the underlying generative trajectory itself to be more tractable and efficient. The rectified flow framework has been notably adopted in Stable Diffusion 3, which combines this formulation with a transformer-based denoising backbone that enables bidirectional interaction between image patch tokens and text tokens. In contrast to UNet-based predecessors that process text conditioning through one-directional cross-attention injection, this architecture allows image and text representations to mutually influence each other throughout the denoising process. Such bidirectional token interaction affords richer semantic grounding and more faithful rendering of complex textual descriptions, including accurate attribute binding and compositional scene understanding.

Scaling studies accompanying this line of work demonstrate that the combination of rectified flow objectives with transformer architectures exhibits favorable scaling behavior, achieving superior performance on high-resolution image synthesis benchmarks as model size and training data increase. The joint modeling of image and text tokens within a unified sequence also simplifies the architectural design compared to hybrid UNet-plus-cross-attention pipelines, reducing inductive biases and enabling more flexible conditioning. Collectively, rectified flow and related advanced generative formulations mark a significant architectural shift in the text-to-image diffusion landscape, offering improved training efficiency, sampling speed, and semantic alignment compared to earlier paradigms — and setting the stage for further investigation into how the conditioning signals themselves, including the choice and design of text encoders, influence generation quality across all of these architectural families.

### 2.6 Ensemble-of-Experts and Specialized Denoising Designs

A key insight motivating ensemble-of-experts designs in text-to-image diffusion models is the observation that the generation process is not homogeneous across timesteps. Early denoising steps tend to establish global structure and semantic content aligned with the text prompt, while later steps refine low-level details and textures with comparatively less reliance on the conditioning signal. This non-stationary behavior suggests that a single monolithic model with shared parameters across all timesteps may be suboptimal, as it must simultaneously accommodate qualitatively distinct synthesis behaviors within the same set of weights.

The eDiff-I model [44] directly addresses this challenge by training an ensemble of specialized text-to-image diffusion models, each dedicated to a specific stage of the iterative generation process. Rather than training separate models from scratch, eDiff-I begins by training a single unified model and subsequently splits it into stage-specific expert networks that are fine-tuned for their respective portions of the denoising trajectory. This strategy preserves training efficiency while allowing each expert to specialize in the particular synthesis challenges of its assigned stage.

The benefits of this specialization are particularly pronounced for text alignment. Because early-stage experts handle the semantically critical portion of generation, they can devote their full representational capacity to correctly interpreting and rendering textual conditioning signals, including complex multi-object descriptions and intricate attribute-object relationships. Later-stage experts, freed from this high-level semantic burden, can instead focus on perceptual refinement. Empirically, eDiff-I demonstrates improved text alignment over previous large-scale diffusion baselines while maintaining comparable inference computation, since only one expert is active at any given denoising step.

Furthermore, eDiff-I explores conditioning on multiple embedding types simultaneously, including T5 text embeddings, CLIP text embeddings, and CLIP image embeddings [44]. The availability of CLIP image embeddings enables intuitive style transfer from reference images, while the ensemble framework allows each expert to leverage the most relevant conditioning signals for its synthesis stage. An additional capability demonstrated by eDiff-I is "paint-with-words," wherein users can associate specific words in the prompt with painted regions on a canvas, providing fine-grained spatial control over the generated output without requiring separate spatial conditioning architectures.

More broadly, the ensemble-of-experts paradigm highlights a fundamental design principle: decomposing a complex generative task across specialized components that each solve a more tractable subproblem can yield improvements in quality and alignment without proportional increases in inference cost. This stage-aware decomposition of the denoising process complements the cascaded and hierarchical resolution strategies observed in landmark systems such as Imagen, DALL-E 2, and Wuerstchen, and similarly motivates further architectural innovations — including the reformulation of the generative trajectory itself through approaches such as rectified flow — in pursuit of more efficient and semantically faithful text-to-image synthesis.

### 2.7 Landmark Large-Scale Text-to-Image Models

The rapid maturation of text-to-image diffusion models has been driven in large part by a series of landmark large-scale systems, each introducing distinct architectural innovations, training strategies, and conditioning mechanisms that have collectively pushed the boundaries of image quality, semantic alignment, and computational efficiency.

**DALL-E 2** (OpenAI) adopts a hierarchical two-stage architecture. In the first stage, a CLIP image embedding is generated from the text prompt via a diffusion-based prior, and in the second stage, a diffusion decoder conditioned on this CLIP embedding synthesizes the final image. This design leverages the rich joint vision-language representations of CLIP to achieve strong semantic grounding, while the cascaded structure allows the prior to capture abstract semantic content and the decoder to render fine visual details. The use of classifier-free guidance [57] is central to controlling the fidelity-diversity tradeoff during inference.

**Imagen** (Google Brain) takes a different approach by coupling an extremely powerful large language model text encoder (T5-XXL) with a cascade of pixel-space diffusion models. The base model generates images at 64×64 resolution, and two successive super-resolution diffusion models upscale outputs to 256×256 and 1024×1024. A key finding of Imagen is that the quality of the text encoder plays a more important role than the scale of the diffusion model itself, demonstrating that richer linguistic representations substantially improve semantic fidelity and compositional accuracy. Imagen also introduces an Efficient UNet architecture with improved memory and compute efficiency for high-resolution synthesis stages. Classifier-free guidance [57] is employed throughout, and dynamic thresholding is introduced to mitigate over-saturation at high guidance scales, a common pathology in pixel-space diffusion models.

**Stable Diffusion** (Stability AI / CompVis / RunwayML) represents a pivotal shift from pixel-space to latent-space diffusion, a paradigm that dramatically reduces computational costs. By performing the denoising process in the compressed latent space of a pretrained variational autoencoder, Stable Diffusion achieves high-quality 512×512 image synthesis at a fraction of the memory and compute required by pixel-space counterparts. Text conditioning is implemented via cross-attention layers within a UNet backbone, where CLIP text embeddings are projected and injected at multiple resolution levels. The open release of Stable Diffusion weights and code catalyzed an enormous ecosystem of downstream research and applications, making it the de facto standard for community experimentation. Its latent diffusion design establishes the foundation for flexible multi-modal conditioning strategies applicable to tasks beyond text-to-image synthesis.

**SDXL** (Stable Diffusion XL) extends the latent diffusion paradigm with a substantially larger UNet backbone, incorporating more attention blocks and a broader range of transformer widths. SDXL introduces a two-stage pipeline wherein a base model generates a latent representation that is subsequently refined by a specialized high-resolution refinement model operating in the same latent space. Additionally, SDXL adopts a dual text encoder strategy, combining the original CLIP ViT-L encoder with the larger OpenCLIP ViT-G encoder, concatenating their outputs to provide richer and more expressive conditioning signals. Conditioning on image size and cropping parameters during training further improves output sharpness and reduces artifacts introduced by arbitrary aspect ratios. Together, these innovations yield substantially improved image quality and prompt adherence compared to earlier Stable Diffusion versions.

**Wuerstchen** introduces a three-stage hierarchical latent diffusion framework designed for extreme computational efficiency. Its most distinctive feature is the use of a highly compact semantic latent space that compresses images to a spatial resolution approximately 42 times smaller than the input, far beyond the compression ratios used in standard latent diffusion models. A lightweight decoder subsequently upscales the compressed latent to a higher-resolution latent, from which a standard VAE decoder reconstructs the final image. This extreme compression drastically reduces the cost of training and inference while preserving perceptually high-quality outputs. By decoupling semantic content generation from fine-detail reconstruction across stages, Wuerstchen achieves a favorable balance between generation quality and resource efficiency, making large-scale text-to-image generation more accessible.

Taken together, these landmark systems illustrate a clear trajectory in the field: from pixel-space cascaded generation (Imagen) and CLIP-prior guided synthesis (DALL-E 2) to increasingly efficient latent-space formulations (Stable Diffusion, SDXL, Wuerstchen). Key shared innovations include the near-universal adoption of classifier-free guidance [57] for quality-diversity control, the integration of powerful pre-trained text encoders for semantic conditioning, cascaded or hierarchical generation for high-resolution output, and the progressive migration toward transformer-augmented UNet architectures. Each system has contributed unique insights—whether in text encoder design, latent space compression, multi-encoder conditioning, or architectural scaling—that continue to inform subsequent research across the field. Notably, the stage-aware specialization principles observed across these cascaded and hierarchical architectures set the stage for more explicit ensemble-of-experts approaches, wherein distinct model components are dedicated to qualitatively different phases of the denoising process, as explored in the following section.

### 2.8 Classifier-Free Guidance and Conditioning Strategies

Classifier-free guidance (CFG) has emerged as one of the most influential training and inference strategies in modern text-to-image diffusion models, fundamentally shaping how conditional generation is approached across virtually all major model families discussed in the preceding section. Unlike classifier guidance, which requires a separately trained discriminative classifier to provide gradients that steer the denoising process toward a desired class or text condition, classifier-free guidance elegantly integrates conditional and unconditional generation within a single unified model. The core idea is to jointly train a diffusion model on both conditioned and unconditioned objectives by randomly dropping the conditioning signal—typically the text prompt—during training with a fixed probability, replacing it with a null token or zero embedding. At inference time, the model's score estimates for the conditional and unconditional cases are combined through a linear extrapolation, where the guidance scale parameter controls how strongly the model is pushed toward the conditional distribution relative to the unconditional one.

Formally, the guided score estimate is computed as:

$$\hat{\epsilon}_\theta(z_t, c) = \epsilon_\theta(z_t, \varnothing) + w \cdot (\epsilon_\theta(z_t, c) - \epsilon_\theta(z_t, \varnothing))$$

where $w$ denotes the guidance scale, $c$ is the text conditioning signal, and $\varnothing$ represents the null conditioning. When $w = 1$, the model reduces to standard conditional generation; increasing $w$ beyond 1 amplifies the conditional signal, steering samples toward higher fidelity to the prompt at the cost of reduced diversity. This elegant formulation allows practitioners to continuously navigate the fidelity-diversity tradeoff without any modification to the model architecture or the need for an auxiliary classifier, making it highly practical for large-scale deployment. As noted in the preceding discussion of landmark systems such as DALL-E 2, Imagen, and Stable Diffusion, CFG is near-universally adopted across these architectures precisely because of this combination of simplicity and effectiveness.

The classifier-free guidance framework has proven to be remarkably versatile, and its adoption spans virtually every major text-to-image diffusion system. Its simplicity—requiring only a minor modification to the training procedure—combined with its powerful effect on output quality and prompt adherence has made it a de facto standard. The guidance scale becomes a critical hyperparameter that users can adjust at inference time to balance photorealism and semantic alignment against sample diversity and creativity. Low guidance scales tend to produce more varied, imaginative outputs that may loosely follow the prompt, while high guidance scales yield images that are more tightly aligned with the textual description but can suffer from oversaturation, loss of fine detail, and reduced diversity. This pathology of over-saturation at high guidance scales, identified in the context of Imagen's dynamic thresholding, is a concrete illustration of why careful tuning and extensions of CFG remain important areas of investigation.

Beyond the original formulation, numerous extensions and refinements of guidance strategies have been proposed to address limitations and extend applicability. One important direction involves dynamic or adaptive guidance schedules, where the guidance scale is varied across denoising timesteps rather than held constant. Early denoising steps that establish coarse structure may benefit from stronger guidance to ensure semantic correctness, while later steps focused on fine-grained detail synthesis may use weaker guidance to preserve naturalness. This insight has motivated timestep-aware guidance strategies that improve overall generation quality without the artifacts associated with uniformly high guidance scales.

Another significant refinement involves the use of multiple conditioning signals simultaneously, such as combining text prompts with spatial layouts, reference images, or style conditions. In such multi-condition settings, guidance weights can be assigned independently to each conditioning modality, allowing fine-grained control over how different signals influence the final output. This generalization of classifier-free guidance to multi-modal conditioning has been particularly impactful in controllable generation frameworks, where text conditions must be balanced against geometric or appearance constraints.

The interaction between classifier-free guidance and the underlying text encoder also plays a critical role in generation quality. Models conditioned on richer text representations, such as those derived from large language models like T5-XXL as employed in Imagen, can leverage guidance more effectively because the conditional and unconditional score estimates are better differentiated. The quality of the null embedding used to represent the unconditional case matters as well; carefully designed null tokens that represent a semantically neutral state tend to produce cleaner guidance gradients and sharper conditional outputs.

Guidance distillation has emerged as an important technique for reducing inference costs while preserving the benefits of classifier-free guidance. Since CFG effectively doubles the number of neural function evaluations per denoising step—requiring both conditional and unconditional forward passes—distillation approaches seek to encode the guidance effect directly into a single-pass model. Progressive distillation methods and consistency-based approaches have demonstrated that it is possible to train compact student models that implicitly incorporate the guidance behavior of teacher models with large guidance scales, thereby achieving fast, high-quality generation in fewer steps without the computational overhead of dual forward passes.

The relationship between classifier-free guidance and other conditioning mechanisms, such as cross-attention injection, embedding concatenation, and ControlNet-style adapter modules, is also important to understand. CFG operates at the level of the overall conditioning signal, governing the global trade-off between conditional and unconditional generation, while mechanisms like cross-attention directly mediate how local textual tokens influence spatial regions of the generated image. These two levels of conditioning are complementary: cross-attention determines how the conditioning information is spatially distributed and semantically integrated, while CFG determines the overall strength with which the model adheres to the conditioning signal as a whole. In practice, the combination of well-designed cross-attention mechanisms with appropriately tuned classifier-free guidance scales produces the best results across benchmarks measuring both image quality and text-image alignment.

In summary, classifier-free guidance represents a foundational innovation in the training and inference of text-to-image diffusion models, providing a simple yet powerful mechanism for controlling the fidelity-diversity tradeoff. Its widespread adoption across all landmark architectures, combined with ongoing refinements addressing adaptive schedules, multi-condition settings, and distillation-based acceleration, underscores its central role in the continued advancement of text-to-image generation capabilities. Importantly, while CFG governs the inference-time balance between conditional and unconditional generation, it operates independently of the training-level alignment strategies discussed in the following section—where reinforcement learning from human feedback introduces explicit reward signals that shape model behavior toward human aesthetic preferences and prompt-following accuracy, offering a complementary axis of control that CFG alone cannot provide. Understanding and extending CFG remains an active area of research with direct implications for image quality, prompt adherence, computational efficiency, and the overall user experience of generative image systems.

### 2.9 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback for Diffusion Models

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for aligning text-to-image diffusion models with human aesthetic preferences, prompt-following accuracy, and fairness criteria. While the previous discussion of classifier-free guidance addressed how models balance fidelity and diversity during inference, RLHF operates at the training level, defining explicit reward signals that capture dimensions of generation quality difficult to express through pixel-level losses alone and enabling iterative refinement of model behavior toward outputs that better match human intent.

A central challenge in applying reinforcement learning to diffusion models is the non-differentiable nature of the multi-step sampling process, which prevents direct gradient propagation through the denoising chain. Several methods address this by differentiating through the final denoising steps or by treating reward maximization as a fine-tuning objective applied to selected model components. TextCraftor [73] demonstrates that fine-tuning the CLIP text encoder of Stable Diffusion—rather than only the UNet denoiser—through reward-guided optimization yields substantial improvements in both quantitative benchmarks and human assessments of image quality and text alignment. Importantly, TextCraftor shows that its text encoder fine-tuning is orthogonal to UNet-level fine-tuning, meaning the two approaches can be combined to further boost generative quality [73]. The method also enables controllable image generation by interpolating between text encoders trained with different reward signals [73], offering an additional axis of user control that complements inference-time mechanisms such as guidance scale adjustment.

Beyond text encoder adaptation, reward signals can target multiple quality dimensions simultaneously. Aesthetic reward models trained on human preference data can guide models to produce visually pleasing compositions, while text-image alignment rewards derived from vision-language scoring functions such as CLIP encourage faithful rendering of all concepts mentioned in a prompt. Fairness-oriented reward signals have also been proposed to reduce demographic and cultural biases in generated outputs, though balancing such objectives against perceptual quality remains an active area of investigation.

A recurring practical concern in RLHF-based fine-tuning is reward hacking, where models over-optimize for the proxy reward metric at the expense of image diversity, realism, or coherence. Regularization strategies, such as constraining updates via low-rank adaptation or early stopping schedules, are commonly employed to mitigate this effect. This connection to parameter-efficient adaptation is significant: the LoRA-based and adapter-based fine-tuning strategies discussed in the following section naturally complement RLHF pipelines, as their parameter-efficient nature reduces the computational overhead associated with reward-driven optimization and makes iterative experimentation more accessible. Overall, RLHF represents a complementary and increasingly important training strategy that operates alongside architectural innovations, classifier-free guidance, and standard diffusion objectives to close the gap between model outputs and genuine human preferences.

### 2.10 Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning and Adaptation Strategies

As large-scale text-to-image diffusion models continue to grow in parameter count and training complexity, adapting them to new tasks, domains, or quality objectives through full fine-tuning becomes prohibitively expensive. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) strategies address this challenge by updating only a small subset of model parameters or introducing lightweight auxiliary modules, thereby preserving the rich generative knowledge encoded in pretrained weights while substantially reducing computational and memory overhead. As introduced in the preceding discussion of RLHF-based fine-tuning, the practical appeal of such approaches is closely tied to their compatibility with reward-driven optimization pipelines, where iterative experimentation demands that each update cycle remain computationally tractable.

Among the most widely adopted PEFT approaches is Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which decomposes weight updates into low-rank matrix products and inserts them into key linear layers of both the UNet denoising backbone and the text encoder. By constraining the parameter space of the update to a low-dimensional subspace, LoRA achieves competitive fine-tuning performance with a fraction of the trainable parameters required by full fine-tuning, making it especially practical for downstream customization tasks such as concept learning, style transfer, and domain adaptation. Empirical results across multiple model families demonstrate that LoRA-based adaptation can preserve the original model's generalization capabilities while effectively steering generation toward target distributions.

Adapter modules represent another prominent family of PEFT techniques, in which small bottleneck layers are inserted between the existing transformer or convolutional blocks of a pretrained diffusion model. These adapters encapsulate task-specific knowledge in a compact set of parameters while leaving the original network weights frozen, enabling rapid switching between different adaptation targets without interference. When applied to cross-attention layers responsible for text conditioning, adapter modules can selectively modulate how textual features interact with visual representations, offering fine-grained control over semantic alignment without the risk of catastrophic forgetting.

Beyond LoRA and adapters, lightweight fine-tuning strategies targeting the text encoder components of diffusion pipelines have also been explored. Since the quality of text-image alignment depends critically on how linguistic concepts are encoded and passed to the denoising network, updating only the text encoder or a small subset of its layers can significantly improve prompt following for complex or domain-specific descriptions. This observation resonates with findings from reward-based text encoder adaptation, where targeted updates to the text encoder alone yielded substantial improvements in both quantitative benchmarks and human assessments. Techniques such as soft prompt tuning, embedding optimization, and partial layer unfreezing offer complementary mechanisms for improving semantic fidelity with minimal parameter overhead.

The interplay between PEFT methods and the underlying diffusion architecture is an important consideration. For UNet-based backbones, residual block weights and cross-attention projection matrices are common targets for LoRA injection, as these components are most directly responsible for both structural image generation and text-conditioned semantic control. For transformer-based denoising architectures such as DiT [74], the attention projection layers and feed-forward network weights serve analogous roles, and PEFT strategies designed for language transformers transfer naturally to this setting given the architectural similarity.

Recent work has also investigated combining multiple PEFT strategies to achieve complementary benefits. For instance, coupling LoRA-based weight updates with adapter insertion enables simultaneous adaptation of both the generative capacity of the denoising backbone and the conditioning mechanism of the text encoder. Such hybrid approaches have demonstrated strong performance on personalization benchmarks while remaining orders of magnitude more efficient than full model fine-tuning in terms of both compute and storage requirements. The modularity of these combined strategies also makes them well-suited for integration with RLHF objectives, as individual components can be selectively updated in response to different reward signals without destabilizing the broader model.

Overall, parameter-efficient fine-tuning and adaptation strategies have become essential tools in the practical deployment and customization of text-to-image diffusion models. They democratize access to powerful pretrained systems by lowering the computational barriers to downstream adaptation, enabling researchers and practitioners to tailor generation behavior across a wide spectrum of tasks—from artistic style personalization to domain-specific image synthesis—without sacrificing the high visual quality and semantic coherence achieved through large-scale pretraining. Together with architectural innovations, classifier-free guidance, and RLHF-based alignment, PEFT methods form a critical component of the broader ecosystem that has made modern text-to-image generation both powerful and practically accessible.

## 3 Efficiency, Acceleration, and Deployment

### 3.1 Sampling Acceleration Techniques

Reducing the number of denoising steps required at inference time is one of the most actively pursued research directions in making text-to-image diffusion models practical for real-world deployment. Standard diffusion models typically require hundreds to thousands of sequential neural function evaluations (NFEs) to produce a single high-quality image, creating a significant computational bottleneck that limits throughput and user experience. The approaches surveyed in this subsection address this challenge through a diverse set of strategies—ranging from fundamental reformulations of the generative process to practical system-level optimizations—and lay the groundwork for the hybrid diffusion-GAN frameworks discussed in the following subsection, which pursue an complementary route to fast synthesis by incorporating adversarial objectives directly into the diffusion training procedure.

**Progressive Distillation.** One of the most principled approaches to accelerating sampling is progressive distillation, where a student model is trained to match the output of two teacher steps in a single step. By iteratively halving the required number of sampling steps, this approach can reduce a model requiring 1000 steps down to as few as 4 steps while maintaining competitive image quality. This paradigm has proven highly influential and spawned a broad family of distillation-based acceleration methods that are discussed further in Section 3.3.

**Consistency Models.** Consistency-based approaches enforce that all points along a denoising trajectory map to the same clean data point, enabling single-step or few-step generation without the iterative refinement process. By training models to be self-consistent across the entire trajectory, these methods decouple image quality from the number of inference steps and represent a compelling alternative to step-reduction distillation.

**Sub-Path Linear Approximation.** Another strategy approximates the curved probability flow ODE trajectories with piecewise linear segments, enabling larger integration step sizes without accumulating significant discretization error. By identifying sub-paths along which the score function changes slowly, these methods selectively apply coarser numerical integration to reduce NFEs while preserving sample fidelity in regions requiring higher precision.

**Deterministic and Accelerated Samplers.** Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM) reformulate the reverse process as a non-Markovian deterministic mapping, decoupling the number of generation steps from the length of the forward diffusion chain. This allows high-quality image generation with as few as 10–50 steps and supports meaningful interpolation in latent space. Extensions such as higher-order ODE solvers further reduce discretization error per step, enabling even more aggressive step reduction.

**Early Stopping.** Rather than completing the full denoising trajectory, early stopping strategies terminate the reverse diffusion process before reaching the clean data distribution. By recognizing that later denoising steps contribute diminishing perceptual improvements for certain applications, early stopping can substantially reduce inference cost with minimal impact on perceived quality, particularly when combined with upsampling or refinement stages.

**De-Randomized Sampling for Discrete Diffusion.** For discrete diffusion models operating over tokenized image representations, de-randomized sampling strategies replace stochastic token selection with deterministic decoding procedures. This eliminates variance introduced by random sampling at each step and allows higher confidence tokens to be committed earlier in the generation process, reducing the effective number of decoding steps required to produce coherent outputs. Non-autoregressive models such as those explored in [8] also demonstrate that generating multiple image tokens in parallel can dramatically compress inference latency.

**Approximate Caching of Intermediate States.** An orthogonal line of acceleration research focuses on reusing computation across generation calls rather than reducing steps within a single call. When a deployed text-to-image system receives many similar prompts, the intermediate denoising states computed for one prompt may be recycled as warm initializations for closely related prompts, avoiding redundant computation from scratch. This approximate caching paradigm can yield significant throughput improvements in production environments with high query volumes, though it introduces trade-offs between reuse efficiency and output diversity.

**Anytime Generation.** Frameworks such as nested diffusion processes [75] offer an anytime generation capability by recomposing the standard diffusion schedule into two nested diffusion processes. This allows the model to produce increasingly refined images as more computation is allocated, so that viable intermediate results are available at any point during generation rather than only upon completion. Such approaches are particularly valuable in interactive applications where users may wish to inspect and steer generation before it finishes.

Together, these techniques form a complementary toolkit for reducing the inference cost of text-to-image diffusion models. While the methods surveyed here primarily target step reduction and computation reuse within the conventional diffusion framework, they share the broader goal of making high-quality generative synthesis faster and more accessible. The following subsection examines how hybrid diffusion-GAN approaches pursue this same goal through a different mechanism—replacing the iterative Gaussian reverse process with expressive adversarially trained distributions—offering an alternative path toward real-time and resource-constrained deployment.

### 3.2 Diffusion-GAN Hybrid Approaches

Hybrid frameworks that integrate diffusion models with generative adversarial network (GAN) objectives have emerged as a compelling strategy for dramatically reducing the number of denoising steps required at inference time, bridging the gap between the high sample quality of diffusion models and the fast synthesis speed characteristic of GANs. As introduced in the previous subsection, step-reduction techniques such as progressive distillation, consistency models, and accelerated samplers form one major axis of acceleration research; the hybrid diffusion-GAN approaches surveyed here pursue a complementary route by replacing or augmenting the iterative Gaussian reverse process with expressive adversarially trained distributions, enabling generation in as few as one to four steps.

A foundational observation motivating this line of work is that standard denoising diffusion probabilistic models require hundreds or even thousands of sequential neural network evaluations to produce a single high-quality sample [19; 20]. This computational burden has driven researchers to explore whether adversarial training objectives, which enable single-forward-pass generation in GANs, can be incorporated into the diffusion framework without sacrificing the diversity and fidelity advantages that diffusion models are known for.

**Denoising Diffusion GANs.** The denoising diffusion GAN paradigm represents one of the earliest and most influential hybrid approaches. The core insight is to model each reverse diffusion step not with a simple Gaussian but with a multimodal, expressive distribution parameterized by a conditional GAN. By doing so, the model can take larger denoising steps—covering a greater portion of the diffusion trajectory in each evaluation—without incurring the approximation errors that plague coarser Gaussian reverse kernels. This allows generation in as few as two to four steps while maintaining competitive sample quality, achieving significant wall-clock speedups compared to standard diffusion samplers.

**Robust Diffusion GANs via Optimal Transport.** Subsequent work has sought to improve the stability and robustness of adversarial diffusion training. Approaches grounded in semi-unbalanced optimal transport theory reformulate the adversarial objective as an optimal transport problem between the diffused and generated distributions. By introducing asymmetric marginal constraints—enforcing the generated distribution to match the target while relaxing the constraint on the noise distribution—these methods improve training stability and reduce mode dropping, which is a common failure mode of standard GAN training when combined with diffusion objectives. The semi-unbalanced formulation provides theoretical guarantees on the convergence of the hybrid training procedure and has been shown to yield improved Fréchet Inception Distance scores relative to symmetric optimal transport baselines.

**UFOGen and One-Step Text-to-Image Synthesis.** Scaling diffusion-GAN hybrids to the text-to-image setting introduces additional challenges, as the model must simultaneously satisfy adversarial realism objectives and maintain semantic alignment with complex textual prompts. UFOGen addresses this by combining a diffusion model backbone with an adversarial loss specifically designed for one-step generation from text descriptions. By training the generator to produce images in a single forward pass that are indistinguishable from real images under a discriminator conditioned on the text prompt, UFOGen achieves text-to-image synthesis at inference costs comparable to a single GAN evaluation. This represents a qualitative leap in deployment efficiency for text-to-image systems, which are otherwise bottlenecked by iterative sampling.

**Adversarial Diffusion Distillation.** Adversarial diffusion distillation (ADD) combines two complementary training signals: a score distillation loss that transfers knowledge from a large pretrained diffusion teacher into a student generator, and an adversarial discriminator loss that encourages the student's single-step outputs to be photorealistic. The distillation component ensures semantic coherence and prompt alignment inherited from the teacher, while the adversarial component enforces fine-grained perceptual quality that score distillation alone may fail to capture. The result is a model capable of producing high-resolution, photorealistic images in a single network evaluation, enabling real-time interactive image synthesis on commodity hardware. This approach has demonstrated particularly strong performance when the teacher is a large-scale latent diffusion model such as Stable Diffusion, with the adversarially distilled student matching or exceeding multi-step teacher samples on perceptual quality metrics. Notably, ADD serves as a direct conceptual precursor to the variational score distillation and LoRA-enhanced distillation methods discussed in the following subsection, which extend the distillation paradigm further by removing adversarial objectives entirely in favor of score-matching alignment.

**Wavelet-Based Diffusion-GAN Schemes.** A complementary axis of innovation involves incorporating frequency-domain decompositions into hybrid diffusion-GAN frameworks. Wavelet-based approaches decompose images into low- and high-frequency subbands and apply diffusion or adversarial objectives selectively across frequency components. The low-frequency subband, which captures global structure and semantics, is modeled by the diffusion component to ensure diversity and semantic coherence, while the high-frequency subbands, which encode fine-grained textures and edges, are handled by adversarial objectives that are particularly well-suited to capturing sharp, high-frequency details. This frequency-aware decomposition allows the hybrid model to allocate model capacity and computation more efficiently, reducing the number of diffusion steps needed to achieve high perceptual quality while preserving the texture richness associated with GAN-based synthesis.

**Summary and Outlook.** Together, these diffusion-GAN hybrid approaches represent a rich and rapidly evolving design space for accelerating generative image synthesis. By strategically incorporating adversarial objectives—whether through multimodal reverse kernels, optimal transport formulations, distillation-based training, or frequency decomposition—they achieve inference speeds orders of magnitude faster than standard iterative diffusion samplers [16; 20] while retaining or improving sample quality. Crucially, the adversarial distillation techniques surveyed here occupy an important middle ground between the step-reduction methods of the previous subsection and the purely distillation-based approaches of the following subsection: they share the distillation goal of compressing multi-step teachers into lean students, but do so with the additional inductive bias of adversarial realism, yielding a distinctive trade-off between training complexity and inference-time efficiency. As large-scale text-to-image models continue to grow in parameter count and deployment scale, hybrid approaches of this kind are likely to play an increasingly central role in making high-quality diffusion-based generation practical for real-time and resource-constrained applications.

### 3.3 Knowledge Distillation for Diffusion Models

Knowledge distillation has emerged as a powerful paradigm for compressing and accelerating diffusion models, enabling faster inference by transferring knowledge from a large, slow teacher model into a compact, efficient student model. While the previous section explored how adversarial objectives can be incorporated into diffusion frameworks to reduce sampling steps, distillation-based approaches offer a complementary strategy: rather than modifying the generative objective itself, they preserve the learned knowledge of a pretrained teacher and encode it into a leaner student architecture. Unlike standard neural network distillation, diffusion models pose unique challenges due to their iterative multi-step generation process, requiring specialized techniques that preserve generative fidelity while dramatically reducing the number of function evaluations at inference time.

**Progressive Distillation.** One of the most influential early approaches is progressive distillation, which iteratively halves the number of required sampling steps by training a student model to match the output of two consecutive teacher denoising steps in a single student step. This procedure is applied repeatedly, reducing a model requiring hundreds of steps down to one requiring only a handful, while maintaining competitive image quality. Progressive distillation provides a principled, stage-by-stage compression strategy that has become a foundation for subsequent distillation-based acceleration methods.

**Variational Score Distillation.** Variational score distillation, as exemplified by approaches such as SwiftBrush, offers an alternative formulation wherein the student generator is optimized to match the score function of the teacher diffusion model rather than directly matching individual denoising outputs. By framing the distillation objective in terms of score alignment, these methods can produce high-quality single-step generators. The variational perspective connects closely to theoretical frameworks for diffusion models [24; 32], which show that score matching objectives provide tractable lower bounds on data likelihood, lending theoretical grounding to the distillation objective. Such approaches are particularly appealing because they decouple generation quality from the number of denoising steps, enabling one-step synthesis without severe degradation in sample fidelity.

**LoRA-Enhanced Distillation.** Memory constraints during distillation represent a significant practical barrier, especially when the teacher model is a large-scale text-to-image system with billions of parameters. LoRA-enhanced distillation addresses this challenge by introducing low-rank adaptation modules into the student model, allowing efficient fine-tuning of a pretrained diffusion backbone without storing full-rank gradient updates. This combination of low-rank parameter-efficient adaptation with distillation objectives substantially reduces GPU memory requirements during training, making high-quality diffusion distillation accessible without specialized high-memory hardware infrastructure. The resulting compressed models retain strong generative quality while being significantly cheaper to adapt and deploy.

**Classifier-Free Guidance Distillation via Plug-and-Play Hyper-Networks.** Classifier-free guidance is widely used to improve text-image alignment and sample quality in text-to-image diffusion models, but it requires running the denoising network twice per step — once conditioned and once unconditionally — effectively doubling the inference cost. Plug-and-play hyper-network approaches such as SpeedUpNet tackle this by distilling the combined conditional and unconditional denoising behavior into a single forward pass via a lightweight auxiliary hyper-network, recovering the quality benefits of CFG at the cost of a single network evaluation. This strategy is training-efficient and can be applied on top of frozen pretrained diffusion models, making it broadly compatible with existing deployment pipelines.

**Conditional Diffusion Distillation for Few-Step Generation.** Conditional diffusion distillation methods focus on preserving high fidelity under specific conditioning signals, such as text prompts or class labels, during the compression process. These methods typically align the student's conditional output distribution with that of the teacher across a diverse set of conditioning inputs, often augmenting the distillation objective with perceptual losses or adversarial terms to sharpen fine-grained visual details. By targeting conditional generation quality directly, these approaches ensure that few-step student models do not sacrifice text-image alignment or fine-grained semantic accuracy in exchange for speed, addressing a key practical concern for deployed text-to-image systems.

**Image-Free Distillation.** A practical limitation of many distillation methods is their reliance on large curated image datasets during the distillation phase, which introduces data licensing concerns and computational overhead. Image-free distillation approaches circumvent this dependency entirely by leveraging the teacher diffusion model itself as a synthetic data source: samples generated on-the-fly by the teacher are used as distillation targets, eliminating the need for any real image dataset. This paradigm is especially valuable in contexts where proprietary or sensitive training data cannot be redistributed, and it enables distillation to be performed using only the pretrained teacher weights and a text prompt corpus. The theoretical connections between score matching and density estimation [76; 25] provide the basis for understanding why teacher-generated samples carry sufficient information to train a high-quality student generator.

**Summary.** Together, these distillation-based techniques form a rich and complementary toolkit for making text-to-image diffusion models practical for real-world deployment. Progressive distillation reduces step count incrementally; variational score distillation targets one-step generation through score alignment; LoRA-enhanced methods reduce memory overhead; CFG-distillation hyper-networks halve per-step inference cost; conditional distillation preserves alignment quality; and image-free schemes remove dataset dependencies. Crucially, these distillation strategies are not mutually exclusive with the structural compression methods discussed in the following section — pruning and architectural redesign can be applied on top of distilled models, or distillation can be used to recover quality lost through pruning, making the two families of techniques naturally synergistic. As diffusion models continue to grow in scale, knowledge distillation will remain a critical component in bridging the gap between research-scale model quality and production-scale efficiency requirements.

### 3.4 Architectural Pruning and Compression

Architectural pruning and structural compression represent a critical family of techniques for reducing the parameter count, memory footprint, and inference latency of text-to-image diffusion models without requiring fundamental changes to the training paradigm. While the previous section examined how knowledge distillation can transfer the generative capabilities of large teacher models into compact student networks, structural compression methods take a more direct approach: they physically eliminate redundant layers, blocks, or channels from the model architecture, yielding leaner networks that can be deployed more efficiently. Unlike quantization, which operates by reducing numerical precision, or distillation, which preserves architectural form while compressing learned behavior, structural compression reshapes the model itself — and as such, it can be naturally combined with both distillation and quantization to achieve compounding efficiency gains.

A prominent example of this approach is task-agnostic structured pruning applied to latent diffusion models. LD-Pruner introduces a novel performance-preserving structured pruning framework specifically tailored to the unique characteristics of latent diffusion models (LDMs) [40]. Traditional pruning methods designed for standard deep neural networks do not account for the high computational cost of retraining LDMs or the absence of a fast, task-independent metric for evaluating model performance after pruning. LD-Pruner addresses these challenges by leveraging the latent space itself during the pruning process to quantify the impact of removing specific components on the model's output, thereby enabling task-agnostic evaluation that does not depend on downstream task metrics. By selectively pruning components with minimal impact on the latent representations, LD-Pruner achieves faster convergence during subsequent fine-tuning, since the surviving model has less information to relearn. Notably, experiments on text-to-image generation with Stable Diffusion demonstrate a 34.9% reduction in inference time while simultaneously improving FID by 5.2% on the MS-COCO benchmark, highlighting that judicious pruning can not only compress models but sometimes improve generalization by removing redundant or noisy parameters [40].

Beyond task-agnostic pruning, block-level and layer-level compression strategies have also been explored extensively. Block pruning approaches such as BK-SDM combine architectural pruning of UNet building blocks with feature-level distillation, training the compressed student model to replicate intermediate feature maps of the original teacher network. This combination ensures that pruning-induced capacity loss is compensated by transferring rich representational knowledge from the full-scale model to the lighter variant — illustrating the natural synergy between structural pruning and the distillation-based techniques discussed in the previous section. Similarly, layer pruning with normalized distillation, as employed in methods such as LAPTOP-Diff, applies structured removal of entire transformer or attention layers from the denoising backbone while using carefully normalized distillation objectives to preserve semantic fidelity in the generated outputs.

Sparsity-based compression provides a complementary perspective, where instead of removing discrete architectural blocks, individual weights or attention heads are driven toward zero through regularization, and sparse subnetworks are subsequently extracted. Methods such as SparseDM apply sparsity-inducing objectives during fine-tuning to identify and suppress less informative parameters in the diffusion denoising network, enabling structured sparsity patterns that are amenable to hardware acceleration.

Another innovative direction involves embedding fixed-point solving layers directly into the denoising network architecture. By formulating certain computational stages as implicit fixed-point equations whose solutions are found iteratively, these approaches replace explicit layer stacks with compact implicit representations, substantially reducing the total parameter count while maintaining the expressive capacity needed for high-quality image synthesis.

The Wuerstchen architecture exemplifies how architectural redesign motivated by compression principles can yield dramatic efficiency gains at a systemic level [36]. By learning a highly compact semantic image representation to guide the diffusion process, Wuerstchen reduces the dimensionality of the space in which the denoising model operates, enabling inference more than twice as fast as comparable models while requiring significantly fewer GPU hours for training [36]. This demonstrates that compression-aware architectural thinking, when applied holistically, can deliver efficiency improvements that go far beyond what post-hoc pruning alone can achieve.

Together, these structural compression methods form an important and complementary toolkit for deploying text-to-image diffusion models in resource-constrained settings. By directly reducing the architectural complexity of the models themselves, pruning and structural redesign work in concert with the distillation-based techniques discussed previously and the quantization strategies examined in the following section, collectively constituting a comprehensive suite of efficiency-oriented approaches for making high-quality text-to-image synthesis broadly accessible.

### 3.5 Quantization of Diffusion Models

Quantization represents a critical compression technique for reducing the memory footprint and computational demands of text-to-image diffusion models, enabling their deployment in resource-constrained environments without sacrificing generation quality. While structural compression methods such as pruning and architectural redesign reduce model complexity by eliminating redundant components, quantization operates orthogonally by reducing the numerical precision of weights and activations, offering complementary efficiency gains that can be combined with other approaches.

A fundamental challenge in quantizing diffusion models arises from their unique multi-timestep inference structure: unlike standard neural networks that perform a single forward pass, diffusion models execute hundreds of sequential denoising steps, each with potentially different activation distributions. This temporal heterogeneity means that naive application of standard quantization methods, originally designed for discriminative models, often leads to significant degradation in generation quality. Consequently, researchers have developed quantization strategies specifically tailored to the diffusion process.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) approaches for diffusion models must carefully account for the variance in weight and activation distributions across different timesteps. Since activations at early denoising steps (corresponding to high noise levels) can differ substantially from those at later steps (low noise levels), fixed quantization parameters calibrated on a single timestep may be poorly suited to others. Timestep-aware quantization techniques address this by learning or selecting different quantization scales at different stages of the denoising trajectory, effectively treating the multi-step process as a sequence of related but distinct quantization problems.

Quantization-aware training and fine-tuning frameworks offer an alternative to pure PTQ by incorporating quantization error into the optimization objective. EfficientDM exemplifies this approach by combining low-rank adapter modules with quantization-aware fine-tuning, enabling compact model adaptation that jointly optimizes for both task performance and quantization friendliness. By introducing trainable low-rank perturbations that compensate for quantization-induced errors, such methods avoid the prohibitive cost of full model retraining while recovering generation quality that PTQ alone cannot achieve.

Temporal learned step-size quantization methods further refine this framework by parameterizing the quantization step sizes as learnable variables conditioned on the current timestep. This enables the model to dynamically adjust its numerical precision budget according to the sensitivity of each denoising stage, allocating finer-grained quantization to the most critical steps while aggressively compressing less sensitive regions of the denoising trajectory.

Efforts to enable 8-bit and even 4-bit deployment of large-scale diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion represent practical milestones in this space. At 8-bit precision, models can achieve substantial memory savings — typically reducing memory consumption by roughly half compared to 32-bit or 16-bit baselines — while maintaining generation fidelity that is largely indistinguishable from full-precision counterparts. Achieving comparable quality at 4-bit precision is significantly more challenging due to the coarser numerical representation, and requires more sophisticated techniques such as mixed-precision schemes that retain higher precision for particularly sensitive layers (e.g., cross-attention layers responsible for text conditioning), block-wise quantization with fine-grained scaling factors, and outlier-aware quantization that handles the heavy-tailed activation distributions commonly observed in transformer-based architectures.

Calibration data selection also plays an important role: the set of prompts and timesteps used to calibrate quantization parameters strongly influences the resulting model quality, motivating the use of diverse and representative calibration sets that span the full denoising trajectory and a broad range of textual conditions.

Collectively, these quantization strategies form an essential toolkit for deploying text-to-image diffusion models at scale, and naturally connect to the broader landscape of efficiency-oriented design choices explored throughout this section. Alongside pruning, distillation, compact latent space architectures, and training-side optimizations, quantization completes a comprehensive suite of complementary techniques that together make high-quality text-to-image synthesis increasingly accessible on commodity hardware.

### 3.6 Efficient Architectures and Compact Latent Space Design

A central challenge in deploying text-to-image diffusion models at scale is the inherent computational expense of operating in high-dimensional pixel or latent spaces across many denoising timesteps. While the preceding discussion of quantization addresses this challenge through numerical precision reduction, an orthogonal and equally important direction involves rethinking the fundamental architecture and training methodology of diffusion models themselves to reduce their intrinsic computational cost without sacrificing output quality.

One of the most striking examples of compact latent space design is the Wuerstchen framework, which introduces an extreme compression pipeline operating on a highly compact semantic latent space. Rather than diffusing directly in a moderately compressed latent space as in standard latent diffusion models, Wuerstchen employs a two-stage compression strategy that first encodes images into a dramatically smaller semantic representation and then learns to generate within this ultra-compact space, followed by a separate decoder that reconstructs high-fidelity images. This hierarchical compression dramatically reduces the number of tokens the diffusion model must process per step, yielding substantial reductions in both training and inference compute while maintaining competitive visual quality and text alignment.

Cascaded relay diffusion frameworks represent another architectural strategy for improving efficiency. CogView3 exemplifies this approach by adopting a relay-style cascaded design in which a low-resolution diffusion model generates a coarse semantic layout, which is subsequently refined by super-resolution stages. This relay formulation allows each stage to specialize in its particular resolution range, avoiding the need for any single model to operate across the full resolution hierarchy simultaneously, thereby reducing peak memory and computation.

One-step latent diffusion models such as SDXS push efficiency to its logical extreme by collapsing the multi-step denoising process into a single forward pass. Achieved through aggressive distillation and architectural simplification, these models dramatically reduce inference latency while leveraging the rich representations of pretrained latent diffusion models. This architectural trend toward minimal denoising steps forms a natural conceptual bridge to the mobile and edge deployment frameworks discussed in the following section, where step reduction is equally central to achieving real-time performance on resource-constrained hardware.

Training efficiency also constitutes a critical dimension of compact architecture design. Strategies such as time-decoupled training, as explored in PanGu-Draw, decouple the optimization of different temporal components of the diffusion process, allowing more targeted and computationally frugal training schedules. Similarly, Min-SNR weighting addresses the well-known imbalance in the signal-to-noise ratio across diffusion timesteps by assigning adaptive loss weights to each timestep, thereby accelerating convergence and reducing the total number of training steps required to reach a given quality level. Together, these training-side innovations complement architectural compactness by ensuring that the reduced-capacity models are trained as efficiently as possible.

Collectively, these approaches demonstrate that significant computational savings can be realized not only through post-hoc compression techniques such as pruning and quantization explored in the preceding subsections, but also through principled architectural choices at the level of latent space design, model topology, and training methodology. This rich design space for resource-efficient text-to-image generation sets the stage for the following discussion of mobile and edge deployment, where these compact architectural foundations are further specialized to meet the stringent memory, compute, and latency requirements of consumer devices.

### 3.7 Mobile and Edge-Device Deployment

Deploying text-to-image diffusion models on mobile and edge devices presents unique challenges, as these platforms impose strict constraints on memory, compute budgets, and power consumption. Unlike server-side deployments that can leverage high-end GPUs, mobile hardware demands highly optimized model architectures, reduced inference step counts, and careful co-design between model compression and hardware-specific execution pipelines. While the architectural innovations discussed in the previous section address computational efficiency through principled design choices at the level of latent spaces and training strategies, mobile and edge deployment introduces an additional layer of constraints that necessitates further specialization.

Several dedicated frameworks have emerged to address these challenges. MobileDiffusion targets sub-second text-to-image inference on mobile hardware by radically redesigning the UNet backbone to minimize latency, replacing expensive attention operations in spatial layers with more efficient alternatives and reducing parameter counts without sacrificing perceptual quality. SnapFusion similarly focuses on on-device generation by combining an efficient UNet architecture with step distillation techniques that dramatically reduce the number of required denoising steps, enabling real-time performance on consumer smartphones. EdgeFusion further pushes the boundary by integrating compact model variants with latent consistency distillation, allowing generation in very few steps while keeping memory footprints compatible with edge device constraints. Taken together, these frameworks illustrate that effective mobile deployment requires co-optimizing both model architecture and inference efficiency simultaneously, rather than treating them as independent concerns.

Beyond individual model designs, compiler-aware pruning and neural architecture search frameworks represent an important complementary direction. These approaches jointly optimize model structure and inference graphs with respect to hardware-specific performance profiles, enabling fine-grained control over the trade-off between generation quality and execution speed. By bridging model compression techniques such as structured pruning and quantization with hardware-aware optimizations, such frameworks ensure that the resulting models not only have reduced parameter counts but also map efficiently onto the instruction sets and memory hierarchies of mobile accelerators. This hardware-software co-design perspective stands in contrast to the caching and inference optimization techniques discussed in the following section, which operate at the system level without modifying model weights, highlighting the complementary nature of these two broad families of efficiency methods.

Collectively, these efforts reflect a broader trend toward democratizing text-to-image generation, making powerful synthesis capabilities accessible beyond data centers and into everyday personal devices. The combination of efficient architecture design, distillation-based step reduction, and hardware-co-optimized compression constitutes the core toolkit for practical edge deployment of diffusion models, forming an essential bridge between the compact architectural designs explored earlier and the system-level inference optimizations examined next.

### 3.8 Caching and Inference Optimization

Caching and inference optimization represent a class of training-free and system-level techniques that accelerate deployed diffusion models by reducing redundant computation during the iterative denoising process. Unlike distillation or architectural compression methods that require additional training, these approaches exploit structural properties of the diffusion inference pipeline itself to improve throughput without modifying model weights. As discussed in the preceding section, mobile and edge deployment demands co-optimization of both model architecture and inference efficiency; the techniques explored here complement that hardware-software co-design perspective by operating entirely at the system level, leaving model weights unchanged while still yielding meaningful acceleration.

A central observation motivating caching-based acceleration is that the denoising process in diffusion models exhibits significant temporal redundancy across successive timesteps. Because consecutive denoising steps produce highly similar intermediate feature representations, particularly at higher semantic levels of the UNet hierarchy, it is possible to cache and reuse these features across multiple steps rather than recomputing them at every iteration. DeepCache exemplifies this principle by identifying that the high-level features extracted by the UNet's encoder and bottleneck layers change slowly across timesteps, while lower-level spatial details vary more rapidly. By caching high-level features computed at one timestep and reusing them in subsequent steps while only updating the lower-level decoder features, DeepCache achieves substantial speedups with minimal degradation in generation quality. This strategy effectively decouples the expensive high-level semantic processing from the cheaper low-level spatial refinement, enabling a dynamic allocation of computation across the denoising trajectory.

Beyond step-level caching within a single generation, approximate caching systems extend this concept to the level of individual user requests in production serving environments. Systems such as Nirvana target scenarios where multiple users submit semantically similar or identical prompts, as is common in large-scale deployment settings. By storing intermediate denoising states associated with previously processed prompts and retrieving them as starting points for new but similar requests, such systems can dramatically reduce the total number of denoising steps needed, effectively amortizing the computational cost of generation across requests. This prompt-similarity-aware caching paradigm introduces new challenges around cache key design, similarity thresholding, and consistency guarantees, but offers significant throughput improvements in high-volume production environments.

Complementary to feature caching, computation reallocation strategies recognize that not all timesteps in the diffusion process contribute equally to final image quality. Early timesteps, which establish coarse semantic structure, and late timesteps, which refine fine-grained details, may warrant different computational budgets. By profiling the sensitivity of output quality to computation at each timestep, such strategies can selectively reduce the number of network function evaluations or the depth of network execution at less critical steps, reallocating saved computation toward timesteps where it has the greatest impact. This timestep-aware scheduling of computation complements caching by providing a principled framework for balancing quality and efficiency across the entire denoising trajectory.

Together, these caching and inference optimization techniques form an important layer of the efficiency stack for deployed text-to-image diffusion models, operating orthogonally to the model compression, distillation, and hardware-aware design methods discussed in preceding sections. By offering practical, plug-in acceleration that requires no retraining and no modification of model weights, they serve as a natural and complementary final tier in the broader hierarchy of efficiency methods that spans architectural design, training-time optimization, edge deployment, and system-level inference acceleration.

## 4 Controllable and Compositional Generation

### 4.1 Layout-Guided Generation with Bounding Boxes and Segmentation Maps

Layout-guided generation represents a critical frontier in controllable image synthesis, enabling precise control over where objects appear in a generated scene. By providing spatial conditioning signals such as bounding boxes and segmentation maps, these methods bridge the gap between free-form text prompts and spatially structured image generation, addressing a fundamental limitation of purely text-conditioned diffusion models that often struggle to correctly position and compose multiple objects within a scene.

**Training-Based Approaches**

Among training-based methods, GLIGEN [77] stands as a landmark contribution. Rather than modifying the weights of a pretrained diffusion model, GLIGEN freezes the original parameters and injects grounding information — specifically caption and bounding box conditions — through new trainable gated self-attention layers. This design choice preserves the rich generative knowledge encoded in the pretrained model while extending its functionality to support open-world grounded text-to-image generation. GLIGEN demonstrates strong zero-shot performance on COCO and LVIS benchmarks, substantially outperforming prior supervised layout-to-image baselines. Its gating mechanism ensures that grounding signals are incorporated smoothly into the denoising process, allowing the model to generalize spatial configurations and object concepts beyond those seen during training.

Scene graph-based conditioning provides an alternative structured representation for guiding spatial layout. SceneGenie [45] proposes a guidance approach that leverages bounding box and segmentation map information at inference time by structuring the text prompt as a scene graph. Each node in the graph is enriched with CLIP embeddings, and a novel loss in the sampling process enforces geometric constraints while incorporating semantic features. This approach enables high-resolution image generation that more accurately represents complex scene descriptions involving multiple instances of objects and precise spatial arrangements, achieving state-of-the-art performance on public benchmarks for scene-graph-conditioned image generation.

**Training-Free Approaches**

Training-free layout-guided methods offer complementary advantages, avoiding the need for additional fine-tuning while still exploiting the spatial information encoded in pretrained diffusion models. These approaches typically manipulate the cross-attention maps or the latent features of the denoising network at inference time to align generated content with specified spatial constraints, connecting naturally to the broader family of cross-attention manipulation techniques explored in the following subsection.

BoxDiff exemplifies this paradigm by introducing spatial constraints directly into the inference process of a pretrained diffusion model using only bounding box inputs, guiding attention activations to concentrate within designated regions without modifying any model parameters. Similarly, training-free attention manipulation techniques leverage the observation that cross-attention maps in diffusion models carry implicit spatial information about where different concepts are rendered, enabling targeted interventions that steer object placement according to provided bounding box specifications.

MultiDiffusion extends spatial controllability to panoramic and region-specific generation by combining multiple diffusion paths that each operate on overlapping image regions, fusing their denoising trajectories to ensure global coherence while respecting local spatial constraints. This approach is particularly effective for generating high-resolution images with user-specified region assignments without requiring any additional training. ZestGuide and LoCo similarly propose inference-time guidance strategies that exploit segmentation map signals or cross-attention alignment losses to achieve fine-grained spatial control, further demonstrating the viability of training-free spatial conditioning.

**Discussion**

The dichotomy between training-based and training-free approaches reflects a fundamental trade-off in layout-guided generation. Training-based methods such as GLIGEN [77] and SceneGenie [45] achieve more robust and accurate spatial grounding, particularly for complex multi-object scenes with precise positional requirements, at the cost of additional training data and compute. Training-free approaches, by contrast, offer greater flexibility and accessibility, enabling users to leverage existing pretrained models with minimal overhead. The cross-attention maps that both paradigms rely upon serve as the fundamental interface between spatial layout specifications and the internal representations of the denoising network, a connection that motivates the dedicated treatment of cross-attention manipulation strategies in the following subsection. Together, these complementary paradigms have substantially advanced the ability of text-to-image diffusion models to generate spatially structured and compositionally accurate images, laying the groundwork for more sophisticated controllable generation frameworks discussed in subsequent sections.

### 4.2 Cross-Attention Manipulation for Spatial and Semantic Control

Cross-attention maps in text-to-image diffusion models serve as a critical interface between textual conditions and spatial image features, making them a natural target for manipulation to improve spatial accuracy and semantic fidelity. As established in the preceding discussion of layout-guided generation, the cross-attention layers of a diffusion model determine which spatial regions of the image are influenced by which textual tokens, effectively encoding a soft spatial layout of semantic concepts. While layout-guided approaches such as GLIGEN and BoxDiff already exploit this mechanism to some extent, a dedicated family of methods focuses specifically on manipulating these attention maps as a primary control signal—offering a powerful and often training-free mechanism for steering object placement, attribute binding, and compositional coherence without requiring explicit bounding box inputs or architectural modifications.

A foundational observation motivating this line of work is that cross-attention maps naturally localize semantic concepts to spatial regions during the denoising process, but they frequently exhibit misalignment, overlap, or leakage between concepts—particularly in prompts describing multiple objects with distinct attributes. Direct manipulation of these maps during inference can correct such failures without requiring architectural changes or additional training data.

Several distinct intervention strategies have emerged. **Attention refocusing** approaches penalize or constrain attention maps so that each textual token activates within a desired spatial region. By formulating this as an optimization objective applied at inference time, these methods push the denoising trajectory toward configurations where objects appear in intended locations. **Masked-attention guidance** restricts the influence of particular text tokens to user-specified or automatically derived spatial masks, preventing semantic concepts from bleeding into irrelevant image regions. This is particularly effective for compositional prompts where objects must occupy non-overlapping regions.

**Forward and backward guidance** strategies treat cross-attention manipulation as a form of energy-based optimization. In forward guidance, attention maps are compared against target spatial distributions and a gradient signal is derived to nudge the latent state toward better alignment. Backward guidance operates by backpropagating through the denoising network with respect to an attention-based loss, directly modifying intermediate latent representations to improve spatial correspondence. These approaches draw conceptual parallels with classifier guidance [19] but replace an external classifier with an internally computed attention alignment objective.

**Attention regulation** methods introduce explicit constraints on the magnitude and distribution of cross-attention activations, preventing any single token from dominating the attention field in a way that suppresses other concepts. Such regulation is particularly important for attribute binding—the correct association of adjectives with their intended nouns—since without it, color or texture tokens may activate globally rather than being localized to the specific object they modify.

**Structured diffusion guidance** leverages linguistic structure extracted from the text prompt, such as noun phrases and syntactic parse trees, to partition the cross-attention space into semantically coherent regions. By aligning attention patterns with the hierarchical compositional structure of language, these methods improve the model's ability to render complex multi-entity scenes faithfully. Related approaches decompose the prompt into subexpressions and apply region-specific attention constraints for each sub-concept independently, then fuse the resulting attention patterns into a globally consistent generation.

**Directed diffusion** extends spatial control by allowing users to specify not only where objects should appear but also how strongly each spatial region should be guided, enabling fine-grained control over generation without retraining. This connects naturally to spatial-temporal attention control methods, which track how cross-attention patterns evolve across denoising timesteps and selectively apply interventions at specific timesteps where spatial structure is being determined. Empirical analysis [78] has shown that early denoising steps predominantly establish coarse spatial composition while later steps refine texture and detail, suggesting that attention interventions are most impactful when applied during the compositional phase.

Collectively, these cross-attention manipulation techniques represent a versatile and computationally accessible family of methods for improving controllability in text-to-image diffusion models. Their training-free nature makes them broadly applicable across different model families, and their direct correspondence to human-interpretable spatial and semantic concepts makes them particularly amenable to integration with complementary control strategies. Importantly, while these methods excel at redistributing attention activations to improve spatial accuracy, they still rely on the diffusion model itself to infer plausible spatial layouts from language—a limitation that motivates the integration of large language models as explicit spatial planners, as explored in the following subsection. By decoupling high-level scene planning from low-level pixel synthesis, LLM-assisted pipelines provide structured bounding box layouts that can be further refined through the attention manipulation techniques discussed here, suggesting a natural synergy between these two complementary families of controllable generation methods.

### 4.3 Large Language Models for Layout Planning and Spatial Reasoning

A fundamental limitation of standard text-to-image diffusion models is their inability to faithfully translate complex, multi-object textual descriptions into spatially coherent image compositions. Even powerful diffusion backbones frequently misplace objects, conflate attributes, or omit entities entirely when prompts grow semantically dense. While cross-attention manipulation techniques discussed in the previous subsection offer training-free mechanisms for improving spatial accuracy, they still rely on the diffusion model itself to infer spatial layout from language—a task for which these models are not explicitly optimized. A promising complementary solution has emerged through the integration of large language models (LLMs) and multimodal LLMs as intermediate spatial planners, which decompose a free-form text prompt into structured, spatially grounded layout representations—typically bounding box configurations—that can then guide the downstream diffusion generation process with much greater precision.

The core intuition behind this line of work is that LLMs, having been trained on vast corpora of textual and structured data, possess strong priors about real-world object sizes, spatial relationships, and scene plausibility. By prompting LLMs with chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning strategies, these methods encourage the model to explicitly think through scene structure before committing to a layout. The resulting bounding box predictions serve as spatial blueprints that constrain the diffusion denoising process, effectively bridging the gap between linguistic intent and visual realization—and providing a richer form of structured conditioning than what cross-attention interventions alone can achieve.

LLM-grounded Diffusion exemplifies this paradigm: it queries an LLM to first generate a coarse bounding box layout conditioned on the text prompt, then feeds this layout as a spatial condition into a grounded diffusion model, substantially improving the placement accuracy of objects relative to end-to-end baselines. Similarly, LayoutLLM-T2I frames layout generation as a structured prediction task solved by an LLM, translating prompt semantics into explicit spatial coordinates before invoking image synthesis, achieving improved compositional fidelity across multi-object scenes.

RPG (Recurrent Prompt Generation) extends these ideas by adopting a coarse-to-fine planning paradigm, where an LLM decomposes the global scene into a set of complementary regional sub-prompts, each governing a distinct spatial region of the image. These regional prompts are then executed in parallel or sequentially through complementary regional diffusion, allowing fine-grained control over local content while maintaining global scene coherence. This divide-and-conquer strategy is especially powerful for scenes involving multiple interacting entities that would otherwise be conflated by a single holistic diffusion pass.

LLM Blueprint takes a related but distinct approach by using the LLM to produce not only bounding box layouts but also rich per-region textual descriptions, constructing a full scene blueprint that guides both the spatial and semantic dimensions of image generation. DivCon further explores diversity-preserving composition, employing LLM-based spatial reasoning to generate diverse yet spatially consistent layouts, thereby reducing mode collapse in multi-concept generation scenarios.

The "Reason out Your Layout" framework explicitly harnesses step-by-step chain-of-thought reasoning within LLMs to infer plausible spatial arrangements from natural language, emphasizing that spatial reasoning—reasoning about sizes, distances, relative positions, and occlusions—requires deliberate multi-step inference that standard prompt-response LLM queries alone may fail to elicit. By externalizing this reasoning process through intermediate scratchpad steps, such methods arrive at layouts that are more physically and semantically plausible.

Collectively, these approaches highlight a broader trend toward modular, LLM-assisted generation pipelines in which language understanding and visual synthesis are decoupled into specialized components. The LLM acts as a high-level scene planner, converting linguistic complexity into structured intermediate representations, while the diffusion model specializes in high-fidelity pixel synthesis conditioned on those representations. This decomposition not only improves spatial accuracy but also enhances interpretability and editability, as users can inspect and modify the intermediate layout before triggering image generation. Notably, the bounding box layouts produced by these LLM-based planners serve as natural inputs to scene graph-based conditioning methods discussed in the following subsection, where relational structure between objects can be overlaid atop spatial coordinates to provide an even more comprehensive compositional blueprint. Challenges remain, however, in grounding LLM spatial predictions to realistic image proportions, handling ambiguous spatial language, and ensuring that the layout-conditioned diffusion stage faithfully respects the bounding box constraints without sacrificing visual naturalness.

### 4.4 Scene Graph Conditioning for Structured Image Synthesis

Scene graphs offer a powerful structured representation for image generation, encoding complex relational information between objects, attributes, and their interactions as graph nodes and edges. Unlike unstructured text prompts, which often fail to precisely capture multi-object relationships and attribute bindings, scene graphs provide an explicit compositional blueprint that can guide generative models toward semantically accurate and structurally coherent outputs. As noted in the preceding discussion of LLM-based spatial planners, bounding box layouts produced by language models capture spatial positioning effectively, yet they remain agnostic to the semantic relationships that hold between objects—a gap that scene graph conditioning is uniquely positioned to fill.

Methods leveraging scene graph conditioning for image synthesis typically employ graph neural networks or graph attention mechanisms to encode the relational structure into conditioning embeddings that are subsequently injected into the generation process. By representing entities as nodes and their pairwise relationships (e.g., "dog to the left of cat," "person wearing hat") as typed edges, these approaches enable fine-grained control over spatial arrangements and semantic associations that would be difficult or ambiguous to express through natural language alone. This explicit encoding of relational predicates directly addresses the attribute binding failures and entity leakage phenomena that motivate the compositional generation methods discussed in the following subsection.

In the context of latent diffusion models [35], scene graph conditioning can be naturally integrated through cross-attention mechanisms, where graph-encoded representations of objects and relations serve as conditioning inputs alongside or instead of text embeddings. This allows the denoising network to attend to specific relational constraints during generation, improving multi-object compositional fidelity. The compact latent space of such models further enables efficient conditioning on rich structured inputs without prohibitive computational overhead.

Compared to bounding box or segmentation map conditioning, scene graph conditioning offers complementary benefits: while geometric approaches specify where objects should appear, scene graphs additionally specify what semantic relationships should hold between them, enabling more holistic scene understanding. Importantly, this positions scene graph-based methods as a natural bridge between the spatially-grounded layout representations generated by LLM-based planners discussed previously and the fine-grained attention manipulation and attribute-binding correction strategies explored in the following subsection. This makes scene graph-based methods particularly valuable for generating complex, multi-entity scenes where both spatial layout and semantic coherence are critical requirements. Future directions include combining scene graph conditioning with layout-based spatial controls and large language model-derived structured representations to further close the gap between textual intent and visual output.

### 4.5 Compositional Generation with Multi-Object and Multi-Attribute Handling

Generating images that faithfully depict complex compositional prompts involving multiple objects, their attributes, and inter-object relationships remains one of the most persistent challenges in text-to-image diffusion models. Standard diffusion models frequently suffer from attribute binding failures—where visual properties such as color or texture are incorrectly assigned to the wrong object—as well as entity leakage, object mixing, and condition misalignment, all of which degrade the semantic accuracy of generated scenes. These limitations motivate a dedicated family of methods that address compositional fidelity through structured, linguistically-informed, and spatially-aware conditioning mechanisms, complementing the scene graph and geometric conditioning approaches discussed in surrounding sections.

A foundational line of work addresses these issues through attention manipulation. Focused cross-attention (FCA) leverages syntactic constraints derived from the input sentence to guide visual attention maps, ensuring that attributes are associated with their correct referent objects rather than bleeding across regions [43]. Complementing this, disentangled CLIP (DisCLIP) embeddings are proposed to separate distinct conceptual components within the joint embedding space, reducing the conflation of attributes across entities [43]. Both approaches share the insight that the cross-attention mechanism—which mediates the interaction between textual conditioning and spatial feature maps—is a critical locus of compositional failure and, correspondingly, a powerful handle for corrective intervention.

Training-free structured diffusion guidance extends these ideas by injecting structured linguistic representations—derived from the syntactic parse tree of a prompt—into the cross-attention mechanism of a frozen diffusion backbone. By processing sub-expressions corresponding to individual objects and their attributes independently, the method reduces cross-concept contamination and improves compositional fidelity without requiring any additional training data or fine-tuning. This training-free paradigm is particularly attractive as it can be readily applied on top of existing pretrained models, including large-scale latent diffusion frameworks.

Bounded Attention complements this direction by imposing spatial constraints directly on the self- and cross-attention layers, assigning specific attention budget regions to each subject-attribute pair mentioned in a prompt. This hard partitioning of attention resources prevents the information of one entity from dominating the feature maps of another, mitigating the object-mixing phenomenon that plagues models with unconstrained attention. In this way, Bounded Attention bridges the gap between purely linguistic attention manipulation and the spatially-grounded conditioning approaches found in layout- and mask-based methods.

Detector Guidance incorporates external object detection signals as feedback during the diffusion sampling process. By querying a pretrained detector at intermediate denoising steps, the method identifies when objects are incorrectly placed or missing, and applies corrective gradients to steer generation toward configurations more consistent with the prompt's compositional structure. This approach is particularly effective for prompts specifying precise counts or specific co-occurrences of multiple objects, and illustrates a broader strategy of coupling generative models with external perception modules to enforce semantic constraints.

GLoD (Global-Local Diffusion) decomposes the generation process into complementary global and local streams. The global stream maintains holistic scene coherence, while the local stream focuses on individual objects and their associated attributes using region-level conditioning. By iteratively reconciling these two streams, GLoD achieves consistent multi-object scenes without sacrificing the appearance fidelity of individual entities, effectively addressing the tension between local attribute precision and global scene coherence that underlies many compositional failures.

DenseDiffusion addresses condition misalignment by redistributing attention density across spatial regions according to the layout implied by the input prompt. Rather than applying a uniform conditioning signal over the entire image, it modulates the influence of each textual token based on its associated spatial region, ensuring that descriptive phrases activate the correct portions of the image canvas. This spatially adaptive conditioning shares conceptual ground with the mask-based and region-boundary guidance methods discussed in the context of geometric conditioning.

The Spatial Semantic Matching Guidance (SSMG) framework extends compositional control to relational semantics by modeling not only individual object attributes but also the spatial and semantic relationships between entities. Through relation-sensitive attention mechanisms, SSMG encourages the model to respect predicate constraints such as positional relationships, enabling more semantically coherent multi-object scenes. This relational focus connects SSMG conceptually to scene graph-based conditioning approaches, which similarly encode inter-object relationships as explicit structured constraints.

The Decompose and Realign framework takes a complementary approach to partially aligned or conflicting conditions. When a complex prompt contains multiple objects with overlapping or ambiguous attribute assignments, the method decomposes the prompt into atomic subject-attribute sub-prompts and independently generates regional proposals before realigning them into a coherent composite image. This divide-and-conquer strategy explicitly handles the challenge of partial condition misalignment that arises in highly complex prompts, and points toward compositional generation pipelines that treat image synthesis as a structured assembly problem rather than a single holistic decoding step.

Collectively, these approaches highlight that addressing multi-object compositional generation requires moving beyond holistic prompt encoding toward structured, region-aware, and linguistically-informed conditioning mechanisms that respect the internal compositional structure of natural language descriptions. The field continues to draw on tools from syntactic parsing, attention manipulation, spatial layout conditioning, and external perception models to close the gap between textual intent and visual realization in complex multi-object scenes. As explored in the following section, further progress in spatial precision can be achieved through richer geometric conditioning signals that operate beyond simple bounding boxes, providing complementary mechanisms for enforcing both semantic and structural coherence in generated imagery.

### 4.6 Geometric and Spatial Conditioning Beyond Bounding Boxes

While bounding boxes offer a convenient spatial conditioning interface, they fundamentally limit the expressiveness of spatial control by providing only coarse rectangular regions without encoding shape, boundary, or depth information. Richer geometric signals — including depth maps, edge maps, normal maps, sketches, segmentation masks, and camera viewpoints — offer far more precise control over the spatial layout, geometry, and appearance of synthesized content, motivating a class of methods that move well beyond simple box-based conditioning. Just as compositional approaches address semantic fidelity through structured linguistic conditioning, geometric conditioning methods address structural fidelity through richer spatial representations, forming a complementary axis of control over the generation process.

A central contribution in this direction is ControlNet, which augments pretrained diffusion model backbones with trainable convolutional branches that accept diverse geometric conditioning inputs such as Canny edges, depth maps, HED boundary maps, human pose skeletons, and semantic segmentation maps. By locking the weights of the original model and training only the auxiliary control branch, ControlNet preserves the generative capacity of the base model while enabling precise spatial guidance. Extensions that integrate cross-attention control mechanisms further improve the alignment between spatial conditions and generated semantic content, allowing users to simultaneously specify geometric structure and high-level semantics through the combination of layout conditioning and natural language prompts.

Mask-based conditioning approaches provide a complementary axis of geometric control by associating distinct textual descriptions with non-overlapping or partially overlapping spatial regions defined by segmentation masks or arbitrary polygons. Unlike bounding boxes, masks can capture irregular object boundaries and enable pixel-level disentanglement of different semantic regions, which is essential for scenes with complex object arrangements, occlusions, or non-rectangular shapes. Masked-attention diffusion guidance methods leverage these masks to restrict cross-attention activations to their designated regions, preventing semantic leakage across region boundaries and improving compositional fidelity — a concern that directly parallels the attribute binding and entity leakage problems addressed in the previous section through linguistically-informed attention manipulation.

Spatial-aware latent initialization strategies address geometric control at the very beginning of the denoising process, encoding spatial layout information directly into the initial noise tensor. By constructing geometry-aware noise priors that embed depth gradients, edge structure, or region boundaries, these approaches bias the denoising trajectory toward image configurations consistent with the desired geometric layout from the earliest sampling steps, complementing guidance applied during subsequent denoising stages.

Region-boundary aware guidance methods, such as R&B, explicitly model region interiors and boundaries as distinct conditioning signals, recognizing that interior texture and boundary sharpness often require different forms of guidance. By providing separate conditioning pathways for region-level semantic content and their bounding contours, such approaches produce sharper object boundaries and more geometrically coherent layouts compared to methods that apply uniform spatial guidance across the entire image canvas.

Camera viewpoint conditioning represents a further extension of geometric control, enabling users to specify the three-dimensional vantage point from which a scene should be synthesized. By incorporating explicit camera parameters — such as elevation, azimuth, and field of view — as conditioning signals, these methods bridge text-to-image synthesis and novel view synthesis, enabling geometrically consistent generation of scenes from user-specified perspectives. This capability is particularly valuable for downstream applications in 3D content creation and augmented reality, where precise control over the viewing geometry is essential.

Depth-conditioned generation approaches use monocular or stereo depth maps to communicate the three-dimensional structure of a scene to the diffusion model, ensuring that foreground objects appear larger and more detailed while background elements recede consistently. Sketch-based conditioning, on the other hand, provides a highly intuitive interface for creative users, allowing freehand strokes to specify the rough contours and structure of desired objects without requiring photorealistic references. Together, these diverse geometric conditioning modalities — depth, edges, sketches, masks, normals, and camera views — establish a rich vocabulary of spatial control signals that significantly expand the precision and expressiveness of text-to-image diffusion models beyond what is achievable with text prompts or bounding boxes alone.

The integration of these richer geometric signals also raises important architectural questions about how to fuse heterogeneous conditioning inputs of different spatial resolutions and semantic abstraction levels with the denoising backbone. Hierarchical injection strategies that introduce geometric conditions at multiple resolution scales of the UNet encoder-decoder or transformer backbone have been shown to improve the fidelity of fine-grained spatial details relative to single-scale injection. Cross-attention mechanisms that allow the denoising network to attend selectively to relevant portions of the geometric conditioning signal further improve the flexibility and compositionality of geometric control, enabling models like [79] to leverage structural cues encoded in cross-attention representations for improved spatial and semantic coherence.

Overall, geometric and spatial conditioning beyond bounding boxes represents a critical frontier in controllable text-to-image generation, enabling professional users, designers, and artists to exercise fine-grained creative control over the spatial structure of synthesized imagery while retaining the expressive power of natural language prompts. Having addressed both the semantic compositional structure of scenes and their geometric spatial organization, the discussion naturally extends to the stylistic and appearance dimensions of generation — where the question shifts from what is depicted and where it is placed, to how it visually looks and feels, motivating the style and appearance conditioning methods explored in the following section.

### 4.7 Style Transfer and Appearance Control in Diffusion Models

Controlling the stylistic and appearance properties of generated images represents a critical dimension of controllable generation, enabling users to direct not just the semantic content but also the visual aesthetic, artistic style, and surface appearance of synthesized outputs. Just as geometric conditioning extends spatial control beyond simple bounding boxes, style and appearance conditioning extends semantic control beyond content specification, forming a complementary axis along which diffusion models can be precisely directed. Diffusion models offer several complementary pathways toward such appearance control, ranging from text-driven style specification to reference-image-guided transfer and hybrid frameworks that decouple content from style.

One prominent direction leverages the rich joint vision-language embedding space provided by CLIP to guide the diffusion process toward target styles specified either through textual descriptions or reference images. Semantic diffusion guidance approaches exploit CLIP-based similarity metrics to steer the denoising trajectory such that the generated output aligns with both a content reference and a separate style reference, enabling flexible disentanglement of what is depicted from how it appears. This CLIP-based guidance paradigm [71] demonstrates that precise guidance using pre-trained vision-language models can meaningfully redirect the generation toward user-specified aesthetic targets while preserving semantic coherence.

Plug-and-play approaches to style and appearance control exploit the intermediate feature representations within pretrained diffusion model backbones. By injecting or swapping spatial features and attention activations from a style reference image into the denoising network of a target generation, these methods achieve image-to-image translation with strong appearance fidelity without requiring additional fine-tuning. The self-attention layers of UNet-based diffusion models have been shown to encode structural and stylistic information separately, making them amenable to targeted manipulation for appearance transfer [66]. Swapping self-attention keys and values from a reference image during denoising allows the generated image to inherit the texture and style of the reference while following the spatial layout dictated by the content conditioning.

Design booster frameworks take a more structured approach by explicitly decoupling content and style conditions within the diffusion pipeline. Rather than relying on implicit feature injection, these frameworks introduce separate conditioning pathways for layout-defining content signals and appearance-defining style signals, enabling the model to satisfy both constraints simultaneously. Such architectures preserve spatial structure dictated by semantic or geometric conditions while allowing the stylistic surface properties to be freely substituted or interpolated. The classifier-free guidance framework [57] serves as a natural foundation for these multi-conditional designs, as it already supports flexible combination of conditional and unconditional score estimates, which can be extended to accommodate multiple independent conditioning streams corresponding to content and style respectively.

Text-driven style transfer approaches, such as ControlStyle, push this paradigm further by enabling purely language-based specification of artistic styles. By encoding style descriptions through powerful text encoders and conditioning the diffusion process accordingly, these methods allow users to request outputs in the manner of specific artistic movements, media types, or visual aesthetics without needing reference images. The integration of large language model-based text encoders into such pipelines enhances the specificity and nuance with which stylistic concepts can be communicated and rendered.

Conditioning mechanisms that incorporate both semantic masks and attribute specifications [65] further extend appearance control to region-specific stylization, allowing different areas of the generated image to exhibit distinct stylistic properties while maintaining global coherence. Cross-attention exploitation in these multi-conditioning frameworks enables fine-grained assignment of style conditions to specific spatial regions, providing a level of granularity that global style transfer approaches cannot achieve. This region-aware appearance control naturally complements the geometric and spatial conditioning methods discussed previously, as both spatial structure and local stylistic properties can now be jointly specified with high precision.

Beyond style transfer alone, the richer relational and compositional understanding required for complex scene generation — including interactions between entities and structured relationships among objects — further motivates the development of conditioning frameworks that go beyond appearance and geometry to encode higher-level semantic structure. Overall, style transfer and appearance control in diffusion models has evolved from coarse guidance-based approaches toward sophisticated multi-pathway frameworks that disentangle content, layout, and appearance. The combination of CLIP-based semantic guidance, plug-and-play feature manipulation, and structured multi-condition architectures provides a rich toolkit for flexible and precise stylistic control over text-to-image synthesis outputs, setting the stage for even more semantically grounded generation paradigms that address entity interactions and relational scene understanding.

### 4.8 Human-Object Interaction and Relation-Aware Generation

Generating semantically coherent scenes that faithfully capture interactions and relationships between entities represents a particularly challenging frontier in controllable image synthesis. While the style and appearance conditioning methods discussed previously address how a scene looks, and geometric conditioning addresses where objects are placed, a complementary and equally important dimension concerns the relational and semantic structure between entities — specifically, how they interact with one another. Standard text-to-image diffusion models often struggle to accurately render the nuanced spatial and semantic relationships that exist between humans and objects or among multiple objects in a scene, as flat text embeddings tend to lose relational structure present in complex prompts.

A key contribution in this space is InteractDiffusion, which addresses the problem of conditioning generation on human-object interaction (HOI) triplets composed of a human, an action verb, and an object, each paired with corresponding bounding box coordinates. By encoding these structured interaction triplets as explicit conditioning signals, InteractDiffusion enables the model to generate images where humans and objects are not merely co-present but are depicted in semantically correct interactive poses and spatial configurations. This structured conditioning goes substantially beyond what is achievable through unstructured natural language alone, as it allows fine-grained specification of who is interacting with what and how, at precise spatial locations within the image canvas.

Complementing interaction-centric approaches, relation-sensitive generation frameworks seek to model the broader landscape of multi-object relationships within a scene. Methods inspired by scene-graph and spatial-relational conditioning employ relation-aware attention mechanisms that explicitly encode pairwise relationships between object entities. These mechanisms allow the model's attention layers to differentiate between distinct relational contexts, such as distinguishing a person holding an umbrella from a person standing near an umbrella, thereby reducing relational ambiguity that plagues purely text-conditioned baselines.

The integration of structured relational information, whether through HOI triplets, scene graphs, or relation-sensitive attention modules, represents a general paradigm shift toward more compositionally grounded generation. Such approaches acknowledge that real-world image synthesis often requires reasoning over structured knowledge about how entities relate, interact, and co-occur, rather than simply mapping token-level text features to visual patterns. This relational grounding naturally motivates the development of unified frameworks capable of simultaneously incorporating multiple heterogeneous conditioning signals — including relational constraints alongside geometric, stylistic, and semantic cues — which is precisely the challenge taken up by the multi-condition alignment frameworks discussed in the following subsection. Future directions in this area include scaling relational conditioning to handle longer interaction chains, incorporating temporal interaction dynamics for video synthesis, and developing richer relational ontologies that capture a wider variety of human-object and object-object relationships beyond the spatial and action-level cues currently addressed.

### 4.9 Multi-Condition Alignment and Unified Controllable Frameworks

Achieving precise controllability in text-to-image diffusion models often requires the simultaneous integration of multiple heterogeneous conditioning signals, including textual descriptions, spatial layouts, visual references, and semantic masks. Unified controllable frameworks address this challenge by designing architectures and alignment strategies capable of harmonizing diverse control modalities within a single coherent generation pipeline.

A central challenge in multi-condition generation is handling partially aligned or conflicting conditions, where different control signals may impose contradictory constraints on the generated image. Several works have proposed decomposition-based alignment strategies that disentangle the contributions of individual conditions and reconcile their interactions before fusing them into the denoising process. These approaches typically operate by decomposing complex conditioning inputs into region-specific or semantically consistent sub-conditions, applying each within its relevant spatial or semantic scope, and then composing the results through intermediate fusion mechanisms. Such compositional strategies help prevent condition leakage and attribute misalignment that frequently arise when multiple signals are naively concatenated or injected simultaneously.

Unified adapter-based frameworks represent another prominent direction, where a small set of condition-specific adapters are attached to a frozen pretrained diffusion backbone and trained to accept diverse inputs without interfering with one another. For instance, [80] introduces a unified framework that simultaneously accommodates local controls such as edge maps, depth maps, and segmentation masks alongside global controls such as CLIP image embeddings within a single model. By requiring only two additional lightweight adapters irrespective of the number of conditions, Uni-ControlNet achieves compositional flexibility while minimizing fine-tuning cost and preserving the generative prior of the base model. This design philosophy — freezing the core generative model and attaching modular condition pathways — has emerged as a broadly adopted paradigm for scalable multi-condition control.

Spatial conditioning frameworks such as [81] further demonstrate the effectiveness of hierarchical feature injection, where condition-specific encoders supply intermediate features to the denoising UNet via zero-initialized convolution layers. ControlNet's architecture naturally extends to multi-condition scenarios by allowing multiple condition encoders to inject complementary spatial information at different resolution levels of the UNet decoder, with their contributions summed or selectively merged. Extensions of this approach have explored cross-attention control as an additional mechanism for associating localized textual descriptions with spatially constrained regions, enabling more precise semantic grounding when layout-based conditions are combined with open-vocabulary text prompts [82].

Beyond spatial conditioning, multi-condition alignment frameworks must also address the integration of visual style references, identity cues, and semantic region annotations alongside text prompts. Complementary regional diffusion strategies decompose the generation canvas into non-overlapping or softly overlapping regions, each governed by a distinct combination of conditions, and compose the final image through region-aware denoising. This allows the model to independently optimize text-image alignment, spatial layout adherence, and stylistic consistency within each region before blending them into a globally coherent output.

Intermediate fusion architectures provide a complementary perspective by learning shared representations that bridge textual and visual modalities at multiple scales of the denoising network. By inserting cross-modal fusion modules at intermediate layers of the UNet or transformer backbone, these architectures enable bidirectional information exchange between heterogeneous conditions, allowing each signal to inform and refine the others during the denoising trajectory. The PanGu-Draw framework [83] exemplifies this direction by introducing a cooperative diffusion algorithm that enables multiple pretrained diffusion models — each specialized for a different control modality and potentially operating in distinct latent spaces — to collaborate within a unified denoising process without retraining, thereby supporting multi-control image synthesis at arbitrary resolutions.

Temporal alignment across denoising timesteps constitutes another important dimension of multi-condition frameworks. Different conditioning signals may exert their strongest influence at different stages of the diffusion process: semantic layout conditions are most influential during the early high-noise timesteps that determine coarse structure, while fine-grained texture and style conditions become more relevant during later low-noise timesteps that refine visual details. Time-decoupled training strategies exploit this property by assigning distinct model components to structure and texture generation phases, each trained with the conditioning signals most relevant to their respective timestep range [83]. Such temporal specialization reduces conflicting gradient signals during training and enables more effective multi-condition alignment.

Overall, unified multi-condition frameworks represent a critical frontier in controllable text-to-image generation, with ongoing research targeting greater modularity, scalability, and robustness to partially aligned or conflicting conditions. The convergence of adapter-based injection, cross-modal fusion, regional decomposition, and temporal specialization offers a rich design space for future systems capable of faithfully rendering complex scenes governed by diverse and heterogeneous user-specified constraints.

## 5 Text-Image Alignment and Semantic Understanding

### 5.1 Enhanced Text Encoders and LLM-Based Prompt Adapters

A central challenge in text-to-image generation is bridging the semantic gap between complex textual descriptions and visually faithful outputs. Early diffusion models relied predominantly on CLIP-based text encoders, which, despite their strong vision-language alignment, exhibit notable limitations when handling dense, compositionally complex prompts involving multiple objects, fine-grained attributes, and intricate relational structures. CLIP encoders are trained with a contrastive objective that emphasizes global image-text alignment rather than deep linguistic parsing, making them less effective at capturing subtle syntactic dependencies and long-range semantic relationships present in elaborate user prompts.

To address these limitations, a growing body of research has explored the integration of large language models (LLMs) as more powerful text encoding components within text-to-image diffusion pipelines. Models such as Imagen leverage large transformer-based language models, specifically T5, as text encoders rather than relying exclusively on CLIP embeddings [10]. The key finding from Imagen is that scaling the language model encoder yields disproportionately larger gains in both image fidelity and text-image alignment compared to scaling the image diffusion component itself, underscoring the critical importance of rich linguistic representations for high-quality generation [10].

Beyond simple encoder substitution, researchers have proposed more sophisticated adapter-based architectures that dynamically couple LLM representations with the internal conditioning mechanisms of pretrained diffusion models. One prominent paradigm involves training lightweight connector modules that translate LLM-derived text embeddings into conditioning signals compatible with cross-attention layers inside the denoising network. These adapters can be designed to be timestep-aware, recognizing that the informational requirements of the denoising process vary across diffusion timesteps — coarse semantic structure is primarily resolved at high noise levels, while fine-grained details such as attribute assignment and object placement are refined at lower noise levels. A timestep-dependent semantic connector extracts dynamically varying conditioning features from a frozen LLM according to the current denoising timestep, enabling the model to leverage different aspects of the language understanding at different stages of generation. This approach has demonstrated notable improvements in handling dense prompts with multiple co-occurring entities, complex attribute-object bindings, and relational descriptions that routinely challenge standard CLIP-conditioned models.

The broader trend of using LLMs as prompt adapters also intersects with prompt enrichment strategies. Systems such as DiffusionGPT employ LLMs to parse and interpret input prompts, routing them to domain-appropriate generative models and augmenting the prompt representation with structured semantic information [84]. Similarly, interactive systems inspired by DALL-E 3's integration of conversational LLMs explore how natural language dialogue can be used to iteratively refine and expand prompts before passing them to the image generation backend [85]. These approaches collectively highlight a shift toward treating the text encoding stage not as a static lookup but as an active semantic reasoning process.

Furthermore, the integration of LLM-based encoders aligns with findings that emphasize the importance of deep language understanding for compositional generation. Imagen's experiments confirm that a generic large language model pretrained purely on text corpora can serve as a highly effective image-conditioning encoder, even without any image-text paired pretraining [10]. This suggests that the richness of linguistic structure encoded in LLMs transfers beneficially to the visual domain, enabling more accurate rendering of complex scene descriptions, multi-attribute objects, and spatially grounded relationships. As text-to-image systems continue to scale, enhanced text encoders and LLM-based prompt adapters represent a foundational direction for narrowing the semantic alignment gap between user intent and generated visual content. This improved semantic grounding, in turn, sets the stage for more targeted interventions within the diffusion model itself — most notably through direct manipulation of cross-attention maps, which serve as the primary mechanism by which textual conditioning is spatially distributed across the generated image.

### 5.2 Attention Map Manipulation for Semantic Alignment

Cross-attention maps in text-to-image diffusion models encode the spatial correspondence between textual tokens and image regions, making them a natural target for direct manipulation to improve semantic alignment. As established in the previous section, enhanced text encoders and LLM-based adapters provide richer linguistic representations to the diffusion pipeline; however, even with improved textual conditioning, the internal mechanism by which these representations are spatially distributed across the generated image remains a critical bottleneck. When attention maps are poorly calibrated—for instance, when an attribute intended for one object bleeds into another region—the resulting image fails to faithfully reflect the text prompt regardless of the quality of the upstream text encoding. Researchers have therefore developed a variety of techniques that intervene directly in the attention mechanism at inference time or during fine-tuning to steer generation toward more accurate text-image correspondence.

One prominent line of work frames attention manipulation through an energy-based perspective, where desired attention configurations are encoded as energy functions whose gradients guide the denoising process. Such frameworks enable Bayesian-style context updating, progressively refining the model's internal representation of each textual concept as generation proceeds. By penalizing attention activations that deviate from target spatial assignments, these methods encourage individual tokens to attend to their semantically appropriate image regions rather than diffusing their influence across irrelevant areas.

Object-conditioned energy-based alignment has proven particularly valuable for addressing the attribute binding problem, where attributes such as color, shape, or texture are incorrectly associated with unintended objects. By constructing per-object energy terms that enforce localized attention peaks corresponding to each entity and its attributes, these approaches reduce the cross-contamination between distinct semantic concepts, yielding images where "a red cube next to a blue sphere" renders with correct color-object pairings rather than blended or swapped attributes. This class of failure modes is precisely what the richer compositional understanding afforded by LLM-based encoders begins to address at the representation level, but which attention-level interventions tackle more directly within the spatial generation process itself.

Syntactic structure provides another powerful signal for focusing cross-attention. By parsing the input prompt to extract grammatical dependencies—such as adjective-noun pairs or prepositional phrase attachments—methods guided by syntactic constraints can define expected attention topologies, penalizing deviations from linguistically motivated spatial groupings. This allows the model to leverage the compositional structure of language more explicitly, improving multi-object scene understanding and relational accuracy beyond what is achievable through unstructured prompt conditioning alone. In this sense, syntactic guidance at the attention level complements the semantic richness provided by upstream LLM encoders, forming a coherent two-stage approach to semantic alignment.

A complementary challenge arises in image editing scenarios, where the goal is to modify specific aspects of an existing image while preserving unrelated content. In this context, cross-attention leakage—where editing tokens inadvertently affect regions that should remain unchanged—can degrade editing quality and introduce unwanted artifacts. Dynamic prompt learning strategies have been proposed to address this issue by learning lightweight token adjustments that repair leaking attention patterns during the editing process, ensuring that modification signals are spatially contained and do not propagate to unrelated image regions. Together, these attention manipulation techniques represent a coherent and effective toolkit for improving the fidelity of text-image correspondence across both generation and editing tasks in diffusion-based systems. While these methods operate at the level of internal model activations, a complementary and practically significant direction involves intervening even earlier in the pipeline—at the level of the textual input itself—through prompt engineering strategies, which are explored in the following section.

### 5.3 Prompt Engineering and Automatic Prompt Optimization

Prompt engineering has emerged as a practical and impactful strategy for improving the semantic alignment between user intent and the outputs of text-to-image diffusion models. While the previous approaches discussed in this survey—such as cross-attention manipulation and energy-based guidance—intervene directly in model internals, prompt engineering operates at the input level, focusing on crafting, refining, and structuring textual inputs themselves to elicit more accurate and coherent visual outputs from pretrained generative systems without modifying model weights or architectural components.

A fundamental observation motivating this line of work is that text-to-image models are highly sensitive to the precise wording, structure, and content of input prompts. Small variations in phrasing can lead to dramatically different generated images, suggesting that the space of effective prompts is complex and non-trivial to navigate. This sensitivity, closely related to the attention-level misalignments discussed in the preceding sections, has motivated a variety of strategies ranging from manual best-practice guidelines to fully automated optimization frameworks.

Interactive prompt exploration systems represent one category of approaches, where large language models are leveraged as intelligent assistants to help users iteratively refine and expand their prompts. By engaging in a dialogue-style interaction, users can progressively disambiguate their intent, add relevant descriptive details, and receive suggestions for more expressive or specific language. Such systems democratize access to effective prompt crafting by reducing the expertise required from end users, enabling individuals without specialized knowledge of model behavior to achieve high-quality semantic alignment.

Automatic prompt optimization frameworks constitute a more systematic approach, wherein an optimization loop iteratively revises prompts to maximize quantitative alignment scores between generated images and intended semantics. These frameworks typically employ metrics such as CLIP-based consistency scores or vision-language model evaluations as proxy objectives, and use language models to propose candidate prompt modifications. The iterative revision process can correct common failure modes such as missing objects, incorrect attribute bindings, or ambiguous spatial descriptions—precisely the categories of errors that attention manipulation and discriminative guidance methods aim to address at the model level—thereby improving the overall fidelity of generated images to complex textual specifications.

Analysis of large-scale prompt logs from real-world deployment of text-to-image systems has provided valuable empirical insights into how users naturally formulate prompts. Studies of these logs reveal systematic patterns in user behavior, including common shorthand conventions, frequently used style descriptors, recurring compositional structures, and characteristic failure modes that arise when users attempt to describe intricate scenes. Such analyses inform the design of better user interfaces, prompt suggestion systems, and training data curation strategies, ultimately contributing to more robust and user-aligned generative models.

Camera-description augmentation methods represent a specialized but practically significant variant of prompt engineering, wherein prompts are enriched with explicit photographic parameters such as camera angle, focal length, lighting conditions, and depth of field. By grounding the generation process in well-defined photographic conventions, these augmentation strategies improve consistency and safety of outputs, reducing the likelihood of generating unintended or ambiguous visual interpretations of a given prompt. Such camera-aware prompts also enable finer-grained control over the aesthetic and compositional properties of generated images, bridging the gap between the linguistic and visual domains.

Collectively, prompt engineering and automatic prompt optimization strategies occupy an important and complementary niche alongside the model-level alignment methods discussed throughout this survey. Unlike approaches that require model retraining, architectural modifications, or inference-time gradient computation—such as the classifier-free guidance refinements and reward-based fine-tuning explored in the following section—these techniques operate purely at the input level, making them computationally lightweight, broadly applicable across different model families, and easy to integrate into practical deployment pipelines. As text-to-image diffusion models continue to scale and diversify, prompt engineering will likely remain a critical tool for maximizing semantic alignment between user intent and generated visual content, working in concert with the deeper model-level interventions described elsewhere in this survey.

### 5.4 Classifier-Free Guidance Refinements and Discriminative Alignment

Classifier-free guidance (CFG) remains one of the most influential mechanisms for controlling the fidelity-diversity trade-off in text-to-image diffusion models. By jointly training a single model with and without conditioning signals and then linearly extrapolating between the two at inference time, CFG effectively amplifies the influence of the text prompt on the generated output. However, standard CFG does not inherently guarantee fine-grained semantic correctness, and several refinements have been proposed to address its limitations from both discriminative and reward-based perspectives. These refinements are particularly relevant given that prompt engineering strategies, discussed in the preceding section, can only partially compensate for alignment failures that originate in how the model processes and responds to conditioning signals.

One prominent direction involves discriminative probing of pretrained text-to-image models to better understand and exploit their internal alignment capabilities. Research has shown that cross-attention layers within diffusion models encode rich semantic correspondences between textual tokens and spatial image regions, and that these representations can be leveraged to identify and correct alignment failures. Techniques such as manipulating cross-attention maps with adaptive masks, as explored in works like [86], demonstrate that augmenting the guidance process with discriminatively derived signals can substantially reduce token-level misalignment without requiring full model retraining. This cross-attention perspective also establishes a natural bridge to the attribute binding challenges discussed in the following section, where similar attention-level interventions are employed to address finer-grained object-attribute correspondence failures.

Self-correction mechanisms represent another refinement paradigm, wherein discriminative gradients derived from auxiliary classifiers or vision-language scoring models are used at inference time to iteratively steer the denoising trajectory toward outputs that more faithfully reflect the prompt. These approaches effectively act as test-time classifiers that provide corrective gradients, complementing or replacing the standard unconditional score used in CFG. By conditioning corrections on visual features extracted from intermediate denoising states, such methods can address semantic omissions and attribute binding errors that persist under standard guidance.

Reward-based fine-tuning approaches further extend CFG refinements by incorporating human preference signals directly into the training objective. Rather than relying solely on the statistical patterns encoded during large-scale pretraining, these methods train reward models to predict human judgments of prompt adherence, aesthetic quality, and semantic correctness, and then use these reward signals to fine-tune model components — including the UNet denoiser and text encoder — to maximize expected reward. The result is a model whose conditional generation is more tightly coupled to human-specified intents, effectively closing the gap that standard CFG alone leaves between nominal prompt conditioning and perceptually satisfying alignment. Together, these discriminative and reward-informed refinements represent a maturing toolkit for pushing text-image alignment beyond what the original CFG formulation alone can achieve, and they lay essential groundwork for understanding the more specialized attribute binding techniques explored in the section that follows.

### 5.5 Attribute Binding Strategies

Attribute binding—the correct association of visual attributes (such as color, shape, or texture) with their corresponding objects in a generated image—remains one of the most persistent failure modes in text-to-image diffusion models, closely related to the broader compositional accuracy challenges discussed above. When a prompt such as "a red cube and a blue sphere" is provided, models frequently misassign colors to objects or blend attributes across entities, producing semantically inconsistent outputs. While the preceding discussion addressed compositional accuracy at the level of scene layout and multi-object arrangement, attribute binding focuses on the finer-grained problem of ensuring that individual object-attribute pairings are faithfully rendered. A growing body of work has developed targeted strategies to address this challenge.

One prominent line of work focuses on syntactic-constraint-guided cross-attention. By parsing the syntactic structure of input prompts—identifying noun phrases, adjective-noun dependencies, and relational clauses—these methods enforce that cross-attention maps within the diffusion model selectively activate for the correct objects and their associated attributes. This connects naturally to the cross-attention manipulation strategies introduced in the context of CFG refinements, but applies them specifically to the problem of attribute localization. [43] proposes focused cross-attention (FCA), which directly controls visual attention maps using syntactic constraints derived from the input sentence. This approach ensures that attention regions corresponding to specific adjectives are spatially confined to the relevant noun regions, significantly reducing attribute leakage across objects.

Complementing attention-level interventions, another strategy involves disentangling the CLIP embeddings used for conditioning. Standard CLIP text embeddings aggregate information across all tokens, making it difficult for the model to distinguish which attribute belongs to which object in a compositional scene. This limitation is analogous to the encoding bottleneck that motivates the use of richer language model encoders for compositional understanding, but here the solution targets the representational structure of the conditioning signal itself. [43] additionally introduces DisCLIP embeddings, which leverage the syntactic structure of prompts to disentangle multimodal CLIP representations along object-attribute axes. By separating embeddings of distinct concepts, the conditioning signal more precisely conveys which attribute should be associated with which object, improving compositional fidelity without requiring retraining of the base diffusion model.

Energy-based formulations have also been explored to derive object-centric attribute binding losses. These approaches frame attribute binding as an energy minimization problem, where the energy function penalizes misalignments between attribute activations and object-specific spatial regions in the attention maps. Such losses can be incorporated as auxiliary objectives during fine-tuning or as guidance terms during inference, steering the generation process toward configurations where each attribute is correctly localized to its target object. This inference-time guidance perspective echoes the self-correction mechanisms discussed under CFG refinements, extending the principle of corrective gradients to the specific challenge of attribute-object correspondence.

Contrastive objectives offer yet another avenue for improving attribute-object correspondence. By constructing training signals that push apart embeddings of different attribute-object combinations while pulling together embeddings of correctly paired concepts, contrastive learning encourages the model to form more discriminative and disentangled representations of compositional concepts. These objectives are particularly effective when applied to the text encoder, as they directly shape how attribute-object relationships are encoded before being injected into the denoising network.

Evaluation of attribute binding progress has also advanced alongside these methods. [43] introduces EPViT (Edge Prediction Vision Transformer), a novel image-graph alignment model that quantitatively assesses how well generated images reflect the attribute-object associations specified in text prompts, providing a more fine-grained evaluation signal than aggregate metrics like FID or CLIP score. Together, these complementary strategies—syntactic attention control, embedding disentanglement, energy-based binding losses, and contrastive objectives—represent the current frontier of efforts to achieve reliable attribute binding in text-to-image generation, and collectively form a critical component of the broader landscape of semantic alignment techniques surveyed in this section.

### 5.6 Compositional Accuracy and Multi-Object Scene Understanding

Compositional accuracy in text-to-image generation remains one of the most challenging aspects of semantic alignment, particularly when prompts describe complex scenes involving multiple objects, diverse attributes, and intricate spatial relationships. Standard diffusion models trained with CLIP-based text encoders frequently struggle with such compositional prompts, leading to attribute misassignment, object omission, and incorrect spatial arrangements. Addressing these challenges forms a natural starting point for the broader discussion of semantic alignment techniques in this section, as compositional fidelity underpins many of the finer-grained problems—such as attribute binding and VQA-based evaluation—explored in subsequent subsections.

A prominent line of work addresses these challenges by leveraging large language models as structured layout generators. By employing Chain-of-Thought prompting, LLMs can decompose complex scene descriptions into explicit spatial plans—typically bounding box layouts—that serve as intermediate representations guiding the downstream diffusion generation process. This coarse-to-fine paradigm bridges the gap between high-level textual semantics and pixel-level spatial fidelity, ensuring that each object in a complex prompt is placed in a compositionally coherent manner.

Divide-and-conquer agent frameworks extend this idea by treating complex prompts as compositional tasks that can be recursively decomposed into simpler sub-problems. Each sub-problem corresponds to a local generation objective, and the results are subsequently integrated into a globally coherent image. This modular strategy reduces the cognitive load placed on a single diffusion model, enabling more accurate rendering of individual object-attribute bindings as well as their inter-object relationships.

Progressive text-to-image synthesis approaches tackle compositional fidelity through sequential generation strategies, where soft latent directions guide the denoising trajectory to progressively introduce each semantic concept into the image. Rather than attempting to simultaneously satisfy all compositional constraints, such methods build up scene complexity incrementally, reducing interference between different objects and attributes.

Layout-bridging methods provide yet another complementary perspective by explicitly modeling spatial relationships between objects before generation begins. By constructing intermediate spatial graphs or layout representations that capture relational predicates such as "to the left of," "above," or "next to," these approaches enable the diffusion model to respect compositional constraints more faithfully.

The incorporation of structural linguistic representations into the diffusion guidance process further supports compositional accuracy. Work on training-free structured diffusion guidance [79] demonstrates that manipulating cross-attention keys and values according to the linguistic structure of prompts—such as noun phrases and their associated attributes—leads to measurable improvements in attribute binding and object composition without requiring additional training data. This approach reveals that the semantic content encoded in cross-attention layers is strongly tied to object layouts and content, making linguistically-informed attention manipulation a powerful tool for compositional control. As will be elaborated in the following subsection, this cross-attention perspective directly motivates more targeted attribute binding strategies that operate at the finer granularity of individual object-attribute pairings.

Richer text encoders also play a critical role in enabling compositional understanding. Models equipped with large language model encoders such as T5 [10] demonstrate significantly improved ability to parse and faithfully render complex multi-object prompts compared to their CLIP-based counterparts, as the richer contextual representations capture finer-grained semantic dependencies. Similarly, adapter-based approaches such as ELLA [49] introduce timestep-aware mechanisms that dynamically extract LLM-derived semantic features at different stages of the denoising process, explicitly targeting compositional challenges involving multiple objects, diverse attributes, and complex relationships.

Together, these approaches reflect the broader recognition that compositional accuracy requires not only powerful generative architectures but also explicit mechanisms for decomposing, planning, and spatially grounding the semantic content of complex textual descriptions. The limitations that persist despite these advances—particularly the misassignment of fine-grained attributes to specific objects—motivate the dedicated attribute binding strategies discussed in the following subsection, which target precisely the object-level correspondence problems that scene-level compositional methods leave unresolved.

### 5.7 VQA and Vision-Language Model Feedback for Alignment Evaluation and Refinement

Visual question answering (VQA) models and large vision-language models (LVLMs) have emerged as powerful tools for both evaluating and improving the semantic alignment between generated images and their conditioning text prompts. Unlike aggregate metrics such as FID or CLIP score, which measure holistic distributional similarity or coarse embedding alignment, VQA-based evaluation frameworks offer fine-grained, interpretable assessments by decomposing complex prompts into specific assertions or questions and verifying whether the generated image satisfies each one independently. This granular perspective complements the compositional alignment strategies discussed previously, providing diagnostic tools that can precisely identify where models succeed or fail in rendering complex multi-object scenes.

One influential paradigm is decompositional alignment scoring, wherein a complex text prompt is first parsed into a set of atomic propositions or factual assertions—covering object presence, attribute binding, spatial relationships, and counting—and a VQA model is then queried on each assertion against the generated image. This decomposition strategy reveals failure modes that aggregate metrics obscure, such as an image that captures the overall scene described in a prompt but incorrectly assigns colors to objects or misses a specific spatial relationship. By providing assertion-level alignment scores, such methods enable detailed diagnostic analysis of where a model systematically fails compositionally.

Beyond evaluation, LVLM-guided feedback has been integrated into training pipelines to iteratively refine text-to-image diffusion models. In multi-dimensional assessment frameworks, large vision-language models serve as automated annotators that score generated images across multiple quality axes—including prompt faithfulness, object-level correctness, aesthetic quality, and spatial coherence—and these scores are subsequently used to fine-tune diffusion model components. This approach reduces reliance on expensive and slow human annotation while enabling more nuanced reward signals than single scalar metrics permit. The rich linguistic reasoning capabilities of modern LVLMs make them particularly suited for evaluating compositional aspects of generation that simpler models cannot reliably assess.

Multimodal large language models have also been employed as annotators for constructing human-aligned preference datasets. In this paradigm, an LVLM compares pairs of generated images for the same prompt and produces preference judgments along dimensions such as prompt-following accuracy, visual realism, and aesthetic appeal. These judgments are used to train reward models or directly to fine-tune diffusion models via preference optimization objectives. Because LVLMs can articulate reasons for their preferences in natural language, they offer interpretable supervision signals that better capture human evaluation criteria compared to implicit pairwise feedback alone.

The integration of VQA-based feedback with reward-based fine-tuning approaches further strengthens the alignment pipeline. In this combined paradigm, alignment scores derived from structured visual questioning serve as reward signals that guide the denoising process toward better prompt adherence. Such reward-based fine-tuning approaches that maximize alignment scores—derived from VQA or LVLM assessments—share conceptual foundations with human feedback optimization pipelines, enabling models to improve on specific failure modes identified through structured visual questioning [72]. This connection directly bridges the VQA-based diagnostic perspective with the broader reward optimization framework discussed in the following section.

Collectively, these VQA and LVLM-based approaches represent a significant advance over traditional evaluation methodologies, providing both richer diagnostic tools for understanding model limitations and actionable feedback signals for targeted model improvement. They are particularly valuable for evaluating compositional and semantic fidelity aspects of text-to-image generation that remain challenging to capture through standard aggregate metrics, and they serve as a natural foundation for the human feedback and reward optimization methods that further refine generative model alignment.

### 5.8 Human Feedback and Reward Optimization for Alignment

Human feedback and reward optimization have emerged as powerful paradigms for improving the alignment between generated images and their corresponding text prompts, addressing limitations that purely supervised training objectives cannot resolve. Building directly on the VQA-based and LVLM-based evaluation frameworks discussed in the previous section, which provide fine-grained diagnostic signals for identifying where models fail compositionally, reward optimization methods close the loop by incorporating such signals back into the training process. While standard diffusion model training optimizes likelihood-based objectives over large datasets, these objectives do not directly capture subtle dimensions of human preference such as aesthetic quality, prompt faithfulness, compositional accuracy, or cultural appropriateness. Incorporating human preference signals into the training loop offers a principled mechanism for closing this gap between what models generate and what users actually desire.

The core methodology in this line of work involves three interconnected stages: collecting human preference data, training a reward model, and fine-tuning the generative model to maximize the learned reward. In the data collection phase, human annotators compare pairs or rank sets of generated images across multiple quality dimensions, including overall visual appeal, adherence to the textual prompt, absence of artifacts, and compositional coherence. These annotations are subsequently used to train scalar reward functions that predict human preference scores for novel image-text pairs, effectively distilling the collective judgment of human raters into a differentiable signal.

Reward model design constitutes a critical component of alignment pipelines. Effective reward models must capture diverse and sometimes conflicting aspects of quality simultaneously. Some approaches train separate specialized reward heads for distinct dimensions such as aesthetics and text-image alignment, allowing the optimization process to balance multiple objectives. Others leverage pretrained vision-language models as the backbone for reward prediction, exploiting their rich joint representations of images and text to generalize effectively across diverse prompt distributions. The quality of the reward model directly limits the ceiling of alignment improvement, and reward hacking—where the generative model exploits spurious correlations in the reward function—remains a significant practical challenge.

Fine-tuning strategies for reward optimization span a spectrum from lightweight adapter-based approaches to full model fine-tuning. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), originally developed in the context of large language models, has been adapted to the diffusion model setting. In this formulation, the denoising network is treated as a policy, and reward signals are backpropagated through the sampling trajectory to update model parameters. Gradient-based reward optimization approaches such as AlignProp and related methods directly differentiate through the diffusion sampling process to compute policy gradients with respect to reward objectives targeting text-image alignment and visual quality. TexForce and TextCraftor further demonstrate that targeting specific alignment dimensions such as text rendering accuracy through reward-weighted fine-tuning yields measurable improvements on compositional and attribute-binding benchmarks without catastrophic forgetting of general generation capabilities.

Beyond direct policy gradient methods, reward-weighted supervised fine-tuning offers a computationally lighter alternative in which high-reward generations are used as pseudo-labels for continued training. This approach avoids the instabilities associated with online reinforcement learning while still pushing the model distribution toward higher-reward regions. Combining reward optimization with parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategies such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA) further reduces the computational burden, enabling alignment improvements with minimal modification to the pretrained model weights.

A complementary direction involves using large vision-language models and visual question answering systems as automated proxies for human judgment during both evaluation and iterative refinement. As highlighted in the preceding section, these automated reward signals enable scalable alignment without continuous human annotation, though they introduce their own biases and failure modes. Preference datasets constructed using multimodal large language model annotators provide an alternative data source for training reward models, particularly for dimensions such as compositional fidelity that are difficult to assess through scalar metrics alone. This tight integration between VQA-based diagnostic evaluation and reward-based optimization thus represents a natural and powerful synergy within the broader alignment pipeline.

Collectively, human feedback and reward optimization methods represent a critical bridge between the statistical objectives of diffusion model pretraining and the nuanced, multidimensional preferences of real users. Ongoing research challenges include reducing the cost of human annotation, improving reward model generalization, mitigating reward hacking, and developing alignment techniques that remain robust across diverse cultural and linguistic contexts.

## 6 Personalization, Editing, and Image Manipulation

### 6.1 Subject-Driven Generation and Personalization

Subject-driven generation and personalization represent one of the most active research frontiers in text-to-image diffusion models, aiming to enable pretrained generative models to faithfully reproduce specific user-provided subjects in novel contexts, poses, and compositions while preserving their unique visual identity. While the following section addresses the specialized challenge of preserving human facial identity in portrait-centric generation, this section focuses on the broader and foundational problem of subject-driven personalization, which encompasses arbitrary objects, animals, and visual concepts alongside human subjects.

**Fine-Tuning-Based Approaches.** The most straightforward paradigm for subject personalization involves fine-tuning all or a subset of model parameters on a small collection of reference images depicting the target subject. DreamBooth pioneered this direction by associating a rare token identifier with the subject and fine-tuning the entire diffusion model, achieving remarkably faithful subject reconstruction across diverse textual contexts. Building upon this foundation, SVDiff introduced singular value decomposition-based fine-tuning that updates only a compact set of spectral parameters, substantially reducing the storage footprint of personalized models while maintaining generation quality. These fine-tuning approaches are powerful but inherently slow, requiring separate optimization runs for each new subject, which limits their scalability in practical deployment scenarios.

**Prompt-Tuning Strategies.** An alternative line of work frames personalization as a prompt or embedding optimization problem rather than full model adaptation. DreamArtist adopts a one-shot learning perspective, introducing both positive and negative prompt embeddings that are jointly optimized to capture subject appearance while encouraging compositional diversity. ProSpect extends this direction by decomposing the prompt embedding into multiple attribute-specific components that are progressively optimized across diffusion timesteps, allowing finer-grained disentanglement of subject identity, style, and contextual attributes. These prompt-tuning methods offer a more parameter-efficient alternative to full fine-tuning, though they may still require subject-specific optimization at inference time.

**Encoder-Based and Tuning-Free Methods.** To overcome the per-subject optimization bottleneck, a growing body of work has shifted toward encoder-based architectures that amortize the learning of subject representations across many training examples, enabling zero-shot or few-shot personalization at inference time without any gradient updates. FastComposer introduces a lightweight encoder that extracts subject-specific embeddings from reference images and injects them into the cross-attention layers of a frozen diffusion model, supporting efficient multi-subject generation with accurate identity preservation. Subject-Diffusion further advances this paradigm by constructing large-scale training datasets with dense subject-level annotations and training an encoder that produces semantically rich subject tokens compatible with compositional text prompts. DreamTuner adopts a self-subject-driven approach, leveraging subject attention modules that decouple subject features from background context during both training and inference, enabling precise identity preservation for single and multiple subjects simultaneously without per-subject fine-tuning.

**Multi-Subject Personalization Challenges.** Extending personalization to scenes containing multiple distinct subjects introduces additional challenges, including subject blending, attribute leakage, and identity confusion when multiple reference embeddings interact within the same cross-attention space. Methods such as FastComposer and DreamTuner address these issues through region-aware attention masking and decoupled subject feature injection, ensuring that each subject's identity is rendered faithfully without interfering with other entities in the scene. The progression from single-subject fine-tuning paradigms toward scalable encoder-based inference-time personalization reflects the field's broader trajectory toward practical, user-friendly generation systems capable of handling real-world personalization demands efficiently. This general personalization machinery naturally motivates the more specialized and perceptually demanding task of human portrait generation, where the heightened sensitivity of human observers to facial identity imposes uniquely stringent requirements on fidelity and consistency, as explored in the following section.

### 6.2 Identity-Consistent and Portrait-Centric Generation

Identity-consistent and portrait-centric generation represents a specialized and challenging frontier within personalized diffusion-based image synthesis, where the primary objective is to faithfully preserve the unique facial identity, appearance, and attributes of a specific individual across diverse scenes, poses, and stylistic contexts. While the preceding section established the broader machinery of subject-driven personalization applicable to arbitrary objects and visual concepts, portrait-centric generation occupies a uniquely demanding niche: human observers are extraordinarily sensitive to facial features, making even subtle identity distortions immediately perceptible in ways that analogous errors in non-human subjects are not.

A central challenge in this domain is achieving robust identity preservation while simultaneously enabling flexible control over expression, pose, background, and artistic style. Early approaches to identity-consistent generation relied on fine-tuning pretrained diffusion models on small sets of reference portrait images, allowing the model to associate a learned token or embedding with a specific individual's facial characteristics. However, such per-subject optimization pipelines are computationally expensive and often suffer from overfitting to the limited reference views, resulting in reduced diversity in the generated outputs — a limitation that mirrors the scalability concerns motivating encoder-based personalization methods discussed previously, here rendered especially acute by the perceptual demands of facial fidelity.

To address the tension between identity fidelity and generation diversity, face recognition-guided methods have been proposed that incorporate pretrained face recognition networks as supervisory signals during training or inference. By computing identity-preserving losses in the embedding space of facial recognition models, such approaches ensure that generated portraits remain semantically close to the reference identity even when substantial variations in expression, lighting, or scene context are introduced. Methods such as PortraitBooth exemplify this direction by integrating face recognition objectives directly into the diffusion training process, enabling fine-grained identity binding to unique textual identifiers while supporting diverse expression and attribute editing without sacrificing facial fidelity.

Collaborative and decoupled generation pipelines represent another important paradigm for high-fidelity person-centric synthesis. Frameworks such as Face-diffuser address the inherent difficulty of simultaneously modeling scene composition and individual identity by decomposing the generation process into distinct specialized branches. In such architectures, one branch focuses on synthesizing contextually coherent scene elements — including backgrounds, lighting conditions, and non-identity-specific attributes — while a separate branch handles the generation of the person with preserved facial identity. These two branches are subsequently integrated through carefully designed fusion mechanisms, such as attention-based blending or feature-space composition, to produce harmonious final images in which both scene plausibility and identity consistency are maintained. This decoupling strategy effectively reduces the interference between scene-level and identity-level generation objectives, leading to improved overall output quality.

Multi-subject generation with consistent facial attributes introduces additional complexity, as the model must simultaneously maintain distinct identities for multiple individuals within a single generated scene while avoiding cross-identity contamination — a phenomenon in which facial features from one subject bleed into the representation of another. Approaches targeting this challenge often employ region-specific conditioning strategies, assigning dedicated attention regions or embedding slots to each individual, thereby ensuring that identity-specific features remain localized and do not interfere with one another during the denoising process. This challenge connects directly to the multi-subject personalization difficulties discussed in the preceding section, though the stakes are heightened when the subjects in question are human faces whose distinctiveness must be perceptually convincing.

Expression diversity remains a notable limitation of many identity-preserving generation systems, which tend to produce outputs that are overly anchored to the expressions present in the reference images. Addressing this limitation requires models to disentangle identity-invariant facial structure from expression-dependent deformations, enabling independent control over who is depicted and how they appear emotionally. Some methods incorporate explicit expression conditioning through landmark-based or action-unit-based representations, allowing users to specify target expressions while the underlying identity is preserved through separate conditioning pathways.

Beyond technical challenges, portrait-centric generation raises important ethical considerations related to consent, privacy, and potential misuse for generating non-consensual depictions of real individuals. These concerns motivate the development of responsible deployment frameworks alongside the technical advances, including mechanisms for verifying the provenance of reference images and implementing safeguards against the generation of harmful or misleading portrait content. The intersection of identity-preserving generation capabilities with safety and ethical requirements represents an increasingly important research direction as these technologies mature and find broader real-world deployment. Having examined both general subject-driven personalization and the specialized demands of portrait-centric identity preservation, the following section turns to style personalization and artistic customization, where the goal shifts from preserving the identity of a subject to faithfully capturing and reproducing the distinctive visual language of an artistic style across freely varying content and subject matter.

### 6.3 Style Personalization and Artistic Customization

Style personalization and artistic customization represent a vibrant research direction within text-to-image diffusion models, enabling users to transfer, adapt, and bind specific artistic styles to generative pipelines with high fidelity and compositional flexibility. Just as identity-consistent generation seeks to preserve the unique attributes of a specific individual across diverse contexts, style personalization aims to faithfully capture and reproduce the distinctive visual language of an artistic style while allowing free variation in content and subject matter.

One prominent line of work explores **arbitrary style guidance** using reference images, wherein the style of a provided exemplar is extracted and injected into the diffusion generation process without requiring per-style fine-tuning. These approaches typically leverage the rich feature representations encoded in pretrained diffusion models to disentangle content from style, allowing the model to synthesize novel content rendered in the visual language of the reference artwork. Such methods are attractive for their generality, as they can handle a wide variety of artistic styles — from impressionist paintings to abstract digital art — at inference time, without the computational overhead of optimization-based personalization.

Another important paradigm involves **self-attention swapping** for style prompting. Since the self-attention layers in diffusion model UNets encode structural and stylistic information about the generated image, swapping or blending attention features from a style reference into the generation process enables flexible style transfer without modifying model weights. This attention-level manipulation allows style characteristics such as texture, color palette, and brushwork patterns to be transferred while preserving the semantic content specified by the text prompt — a principle that connects naturally to the attention-based mechanisms explored in subsequent text-driven editing approaches.

Beyond training-free approaches, **fine-tuning-based strategies** have demonstrated strong performance in binding unique textual identifiers to target artistic styles. Methods such as Single-StyleForge and Direct Consistency Optimization train the model on a small set of style exemplars to associate a learned token or embedding with the target style, enabling repeated and controllable style generation through simple prompt modification. A key challenge addressed by these approaches is the preservation of **compositional flexibility**: the fine-tuned model must generate diverse subjects and scenes in the learned style without suffering from overfitting to the specific content present in the training exemplars. Direct Consistency Optimization in particular introduces a consistency-driven training objective that regularizes the style binding process to ensure that stylistic attributes remain disentangled from content, enabling the user to freely compose new scenes in the learned artistic style.

Collectively, these methods reflect a progression from purely inference-time style control toward increasingly expressive and fine-grained personalization of artistic appearance, with ongoing work balancing the trade-off between style fidelity, content controllability, and generalization across unseen subjects and compositions. This focus on disentangling style from content provides a conceptual bridge to text-driven image editing, where a complementary challenge arises: given a real image, how can diffusion models be inverted and redirected by new textual instructions while faithfully preserving the structural and semantic content of the original?

### 6.4 Text-Driven Image Editing and Inversion

Text-driven image editing via diffusion model inversion has emerged as a powerful paradigm that enables precise manipulation of real images guided by natural language prompts, forming a natural complement to the style personalization and artistic customization methods discussed previously. While style personalization focuses on binding new visual languages to the generative pipeline, inversion-based editing addresses the complementary challenge: given a real image, how can diffusion models be inverted and redirected by new textual instructions while faithfully preserving the structural and semantic content of the original? The core challenge in this domain lies in faithfully inverting a real image into the diffusion model's noise space such that the denoising process can be redirected according to a new target text prompt while maintaining the integrity of unedited content.

A foundational approach to image inversion in diffusion models is based on DDIM inversion, which reverses the deterministic DDIM sampling trajectory to encode a real image into a sequence of latent noise codes. This deterministic inversion provides a starting point for text-driven editing, as the inverted latent can be denoised under a new target prompt to produce an edited image. However, naive DDIM inversion often suffers from reconstruction errors that accumulate across timesteps, leading to degraded fidelity when the source prompt and target prompt diverge significantly. To address this, prompt tuning inversion methods optimize a soft text embedding such that the diffusion model's denoising trajectory precisely reconstructs the original image, thereby improving source-target disentanglement and enabling more faithful edits.

Optimization-based inversion methods take a complementary approach by directly minimizing a reconstruction objective in the latent or pixel space, often incorporating regularization terms to maintain semantic consistency. These methods trade computational efficiency for higher reconstruction accuracy, making them suitable for scenarios requiring precise preservation of identity or fine-grained texture details.

An influential line of work explores improved source-target disentanglement through Direct Inversion strategies, which aim to separate the content of the source image from the editing direction implied by the target prompt. By disentangling these two components, such methods reduce unwanted content leakage from the source image into edited regions and vice versa, producing cleaner and more semantically coherent edits — a goal that echoes the content-style disentanglement central to the personalization methods explored in the preceding subsection.

A particularly elegant approach to text-driven editing is exemplified by mask-based methods such as DiffEdit, which automatically identifies the regions of an image that should be modified by contrasting the diffusion model's denoising predictions under the source and target text conditions. By computing the difference between noise estimates conditioned on the original and target prompts, these methods generate soft editing masks that localize the relevant semantic regions without requiring manual annotation. The masked regions are then edited through targeted diffusion while the unmasked regions are preserved, enabling semantically aware and spatially precise edits.

Collectively, these inversion and editing techniques form a rich toolkit for text-guided image manipulation, with ongoing work focusing on improving reconstruction fidelity, reducing optimization costs, and achieving finer-grained control over which aspects of an image are modified in response to a given textual instruction. The interplay between accurate inversion, effective conditioning, and semantic disentanglement remains a central challenge driving innovation in this area, with methods such as [87] contributing advances in optimizing value network inputs within cross-attention layers alongside attention regularization to achieve accurate style editing while preserving structural integrity. This foundation in inversion and prompt-guided control naturally sets the stage for instruction-based editing approaches, where rather than carefully engineering target prompts, users express desired modifications through intuitive natural language commands — a paradigm explored in the following subsection.

### 6.5 Instruction-Based and Automated Image Editing

Instruction-based image editing represents a highly intuitive paradigm in which users specify desired modifications through natural language commands rather than carefully engineered prompts or manual mask annotations. This approach substantially lowers the barrier to image manipulation, enabling non-expert users to perform complex edits through conversational-style directives, and sits naturally alongside inversion-based and localized editing methods as part of the broader landscape of text-driven image manipulation.

A landmark contribution in this direction is InstructPix2Pix, which frames image editing as a conditional generation problem guided by human-written instructions. Rather than relying on paired real-world before-and-after image datasets—which are prohibitively expensive to collect at scale—the method constructs large-scale synthetic training data by combining the capabilities of a large language model to generate instruction-paired caption rewrites with a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model to synthesize the corresponding edited images. The resulting model is trained to map an input image and an editing instruction jointly to an output image, enabling a wide variety of edits such as changing object appearance, transferring artistic styles, and altering scene attributes in a single forward pass without requiring test-time optimization or inversion. This stands in contrast to the inversion-based editing methods discussed previously, which rely on careful reconstruction of the source image's latent trajectory before applying target-prompt guidance.

Beyond direct instruction following, automated correction pipelines extend this paradigm by integrating higher-level reasoning and iterative self-assessment into the editing process. Self-correcting LLM-controlled diffusion frameworks decompose the editing task into perception, reasoning, and generation stages, where a large language model continuously evaluates the generated or edited content against user intent and issues corrective instructions to the diffusion model until satisfactory alignment is achieved. This iterative loop enables the system to recover from generation errors, handle ambiguous instructions, and produce edits that more faithfully reflect complex user requirements.

Further generalizations of instruction-guided editing, such as InstructGIE, aim to improve the transferability and robustness of editing models across diverse domains and instruction styles. These approaches typically employ techniques such as domain-adaptive fine-tuning, contrastive learning objectives, or auxiliary consistency losses to ensure that the model generalizes beyond the specific distribution of synthetic training instructions, handling a broader range of real-world editing scenarios without degradation in edit quality or semantic preservation of unedited regions. In this respect, instruction-based methods share a common goal with the localized inpainting and region-specific approaches explored in the following subsection, namely achieving precise, semantically coherent modifications while protecting the integrity of unedited content.

Collectively, instruction-based and automated editing methods represent a compelling convergence of natural language understanding, generative modeling, and iterative reasoning, dramatically expanding the accessibility and expressiveness of image manipulation pipelines built on top of pretrained diffusion models. Together with inversion-based techniques and spatially localized editing frameworks, they constitute a comprehensive and complementary suite of tools for fine-grained, user-directed image editing.

### 6.6 Local Editing, Inpainting, and Region-Specific Manipulation

Local editing, inpainting, and region-specific manipulation represent a critical frontier in diffusion-based image editing, enabling users to modify specific spatial regions of an image while preserving the integrity of surrounding content. While the instruction-based and inversion-guided methods discussed in the previous section operate primarily at a global semantic level—applying edits that affect the overall image in response to textual directives—localized methods must additionally address the spatial dimension of editing, carefully balancing coherence at region boundaries, semantic consistency between edited and unedited areas, and faithfulness to the conditioning text or reference signal.

Text-guided inpainting frameworks have emerged as a natural extension of text-to-image diffusion models. Systems such as GLIDE and Imagen Editor demonstrate that diffusion models pretrained on large-scale text-image corpora can be adapted to fill masked image regions with content that is both semantically aligned with the provided text prompt and visually coherent with the preserved surroundings. These frameworks typically operate by conditioning the denoising network on a binary mask alongside the noisy image and text prompt, allowing the model to attend to unmasked context regions during generation. In this respect, they share the goal of semantic preservation articulated by instruction-based editing methods, but pursue it through explicit spatial conditioning rather than learned instruction following.

Progressive feature blending approaches such as PFB-Diff address the challenge of seamlessly integrating locally generated content with the original image by blending intermediate feature representations at multiple stages of the denoising process rather than only at the pixel level. This multi-scale blending strategy reduces visible artifacts at mask boundaries and improves perceptual coherence, particularly when the edited region must interact with complex textures or structures in the surrounding image.

Differential diffusion introduces a more fine-grained paradigm for region-specific control by assigning pixel-level or region-level edit strength values, allowing different parts of an image to undergo varying degrees of modification within a single forward pass. This approach moves beyond hard binary masks, enabling smooth spatial gradients of editing intensity that are particularly useful for tasks such as relighting, texture blending, and soft object insertion—complementing the iterative, multi-granular editing workflows explored in the following section.

Attention-based masking strategies leverage the cross-attention maps naturally produced within diffusion UNet architectures to identify semantically relevant spatial regions without requiring explicit user-provided masks [79]. By analyzing how textual tokens attend to spatial locations during the denoising process, these methods can automatically localize objects or attributes mentioned in the editing prompt, enabling mask-free localized edits driven purely by text. This connection between attention mechanisms and spatial semantics also underpins several of the spatio-temporal adaptive attention techniques that will be discussed in the context of multi-step iterative editing in the following section.

Layered editing frameworks such as LayerDiffusion extend the paradigm further by decomposing an image into multiple semantically distinct layers, each of which can be independently edited through separate diffusion passes while maintaining global compositional consistency. This layered representation allows simultaneous handling of multiple editing operations on distinct image regions, supporting complex workflows such as foreground object replacement combined with background style transfer within a unified generation framework. Such layered decompositions provide a natural bridge toward the multi-granular and iterative editing frameworks examined next, where managing accumulated edits across successive modification rounds demands similarly structured representations and careful attention to inter-region coherence. Together, these localized editing approaches substantially expand the practical utility of diffusion models for fine-grained, spatially controlled image manipulation tasks, forming an essential complement to both the global instruction-following methods that precede them and the iterative refinement paradigms that follow.

### 6.7 Multi-Granular and Iterative Image Editing

Multi-granular and iterative image editing represents an important frontier in diffusion-based image manipulation, addressing the challenge of performing sequential, layered modifications that span from fine-grained local adjustments to holistic global transformations. Building upon the localized and region-specific editing capabilities discussed in the previous section, iterative frameworks extend the editing paradigm further by enabling multi-step workflows where successive modifications must remain mutually coherent—requiring specialized architectural and algorithmic designs to manage accumulated errors and preserve semantic consistency across editing rounds.

A central challenge in iterative editing is ensuring that repeated modifications do not degrade image quality or introduce unintended artifacts. Methods in this space leverage the inherent structure of the diffusion denoising process to support multi-step editing workflows. Latent iteration strategies, for instance, enable models to revisit and refine intermediate representations across multiple edit cycles, allowing users to progressively adjust content at varying levels of granularity—from localized texture changes to broader compositional rearrangements—without catastrophic interference between edits. This progressive refinement complements the single-pass inpainting and region blending techniques described earlier, offering a pathway toward truly interactive editing experiences.

Spatio-temporal adaptive attention mechanisms have also been explored to support continuity-sensitive editing scenarios, where the model must preserve the structural and stylistic coherence of unedited regions while applying targeted modifications. By adaptively modulating soft-attention weights across both spatial and temporal dimensions of the denoising process, such approaches allow edits to propagate smoothly without abrupt discontinuities at region boundaries—addressing limitations that arise when attention-based masking strategies are applied repeatedly across multiple editing passes.

Another line of work addresses non-rigid image editing—transformations that alter object shapes, poses, or spatial arrangements rather than merely swapping textures or colors. Balanced attention modules that combine target-text inversion with progressive interpolation offer a promising direction here, enabling fine-grained semantic control over non-rigid deformations without requiring model fine-tuning or retraining. By progressively blending source and target attention distributions, these methods achieve smooth transitions between semantic states while respecting the underlying image structure. This capability naturally complements the cross-domain compositing and object-level manipulation approaches discussed in the following section, where repositioning and reshaping objects within complex scenes demands similarly flexible control over spatial semantics.

Collectively, these multi-granular and iterative editing frameworks highlight the importance of designing diffusion-based pipelines that are not only capable of high-quality single-shot generation but also robust to the demands of interactive, multi-turn editing workflows that reflect how users naturally engage with image creation and refinement tools. Together with the localized, layered, and compositional editing methods surveyed across this and neighboring sections, they paint a comprehensive picture of the rich design space emerging around controllable, user-driven diffusion-based image editing.

### 6.8 Cross-Domain Compositing and Object-Level Manipulation

Cross-domain image compositing and object-level manipulation represent a compelling set of capabilities enabled by pretrained text-to-image diffusion models, extending the multi-granular and iterative editing paradigms discussed in the previous section toward a broader range of scene construction and compositional control tasks. While iterative frameworks address the challenge of sequential, layered modifications within a single image, compositing and object-level manipulation introduce the additional complexity of reconciling visual elements drawn from disparate domains—demanding that diffusion models simultaneously manage perceptual coherence, geometric consistency, and semantic plausibility across region boundaries.

A central challenge in cross-domain compositing lies in achieving perceptual coherence between pasted or blended regions and their surrounding context. Localized iterative refinement approaches address this by progressively refining boundary regions and texture statistics within the denoising process, leveraging the strong generative prior of diffusion models to harmonize lighting, color distribution, and stylistic characteristics between composited elements. By constraining the diffusion process to localized regions while conditioning on the broader scene context, such methods enable plausible texture replacement and seamless blending without requiring domain-specific retraining.

For object-level manipulation tasks such as resizing, repositioning, and removal, editable image element representations have been proposed that disentangle individual objects from their backgrounds within the latent space of diffusion models. These representations allow targeted geometric transformations to be applied to specific objects, with the surrounding scene automatically inpainted to maintain global coherence. Such approaches leverage the inpainting capabilities of diffusion models to fill disoccluded regions that arise from object removal or repositioning, producing photorealistic results that preserve scene illumination and perspective consistency.

Subject-driven image editing pipelines offer a more structured paradigm for object-level manipulation, combining segmentation-guided pasting with diffusion-based inpainting and harmonization. Representative of this direction, the PhD pipeline integrates automatic segmentation to precisely extract target subjects, pastes them into destination scenes, and subsequently applies diffusion-based harmonization to reconcile appearance discrepancies arising from differences in lighting conditions, image style, and background context. This multi-stage design ensures that composited subjects appear as natural constituents of their target environments rather than artificially superimposed foreground elements.

Collectively, these cross-domain compositing and object-level manipulation approaches highlight the versatility of pretrained diffusion models as powerful engines for creative image editing, extending well beyond simple text-conditioned synthesis toward fine-grained scene construction and compositional control. The flexible manipulation of visual concepts at the object and scene level naturally sets the stage for the following section, which examines how the knowledge and representations encoded within pretrained diffusion models can be further adapted, customized, or selectively suppressed to meet the demands of new domains, emerging concepts, and responsible deployment constraints.

### 6.9 Concept Adaptation, Domain Transfer, and Concept Erasure

Adapting pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to new concepts, domains, or styles while minimizing computational overhead represents a critical research direction, encompassing a spectrum of techniques from soft prompt learning and domain transfer to concept erasure and content protection. While the previous section explored cross-domain compositing and object-level manipulation as means of compositional control, the techniques discussed here operate at a deeper level, directly modifying or protecting the knowledge encoded within model weights and representations.

**Concept Adaptation and Domain Transfer.** A central challenge in concept adaptation is enabling models to generalize to new visual concepts or stylistic domains without incurring the cost of full retraining. Parameter-efficient strategies such as low-rank adaptation and lightweight tuning modules have proven effective in this regard. For instance, [88] proposes editing skip connections in the UNet decoder through lightweight SC-Tuner modules, enabling efficient generative adaptation with significantly reduced training parameters and memory overhead. Similarly, [89] addresses the problem of sequentially adapting models to multiple fine-grained concepts by introducing a continually self-regularized low-rank adaptation applied to cross-attention layers of Stable Diffusion. This approach mitigates catastrophic forgetting—a key failure mode when new concepts arrive sequentially—without storing historical user data for replay, demonstrating strong performance in both continual text-to-image customization and rehearsal-free continual learning for image classification.

Beyond sequential adaptation, domain transfer approaches seek to bind new stylistic or conceptual identities to unique text identifiers, allowing the generation of novel images that conform to user-specified domains while retaining the compositional flexibility of the base model. Such methods often rely on carefully designed prompt initialization strategies and regularization objectives to preserve general generation capability while anchoring new knowledge.

**Concept Erasure.** As diffusion models are increasingly deployed in open-ended creative and commercial settings, the ability to selectively remove undesired, copyrighted, or sensitive visual concepts from model weights has emerged as an important safety and compliance requirement. Concept erasure methods aim to permanently suppress specific concepts—such as particular artistic styles, explicit content, or identifiable individuals—without degrading the model's overall generative quality. These approaches typically formulate erasure as a targeted weight modification problem, fine-tuning model parameters to minimize the model's ability to generate the target concept while preserving its behavior on unrelated prompts. A key challenge lies in ensuring specificity: erasure should be concept-specific and should not inadvertently degrade the generation of semantically adjacent or unrelated content.

**Content Suppression and Protective Perturbations.** Complementing erasure-based approaches, a parallel line of research focuses on preventing unauthorized manipulation of user-provided images through imperceptible protective perturbations. These methods craft adversarial noise patterns that are visually imperceptible to human observers but significantly degrade the quality of edits produced by diffusion-based pipelines when applied to the protected image. By embedding such perturbations into source images, content creators can protect their visual assets against unauthorized inpainting, style transfer, or identity manipulation, providing a proactive defense mechanism against misuse. The effectiveness of these protection strategies depends on their transferability across different diffusion model variants and editing pipelines, as well as their robustness against common image processing operations such as compression or resizing.

Together, these three directions—concept adaptation, concept erasure, and content suppression—define a comprehensive toolkit for managing the knowledge and capabilities encoded in large-scale pretrained diffusion models, balancing creative flexibility with responsible deployment constraints. These considerations become especially pertinent as diffusion models extend beyond static image synthesis into the temporal domain, where the challenges of efficient adaptation and consistent content generation take on additional complexity, as examined in the following section on video editing.

### 6.10 Video Editing via Image Diffusion Models

Extending image diffusion models to video editing represents a natural yet challenging frontier, requiring temporal consistency across frames while preserving the rich generative capabilities developed for static image synthesis. Building upon the adaptation and knowledge management techniques discussed in the previous section, video editing introduces an additional temporal dimension that demands new strategies for ensuring coherent, high-quality results across sequences of frames. Several paradigms have emerged to bridge this gap, ranging from entirely training-free approaches to fine-tuning-based frameworks.

Training-free methods leverage the structural properties of pretrained image diffusion models to propagate edits across video frames without any additional optimization. These approaches typically exploit self-attention feature injection, where edited features from a keyframe are injected into the processing of subsequent frames, encouraging temporal coherence without requiring the model to be retrained on video data. By reusing intermediate attention representations computed during the diffusion denoising process, such methods can achieve surprisingly consistent edits across frames while benefiting from the full generative capacity of the underlying image model.

Zero-shot multi-concept video editing frameworks extend this paradigm by enabling the simultaneous application of multiple distinct editing concepts across different regions or temporal segments of a video. Attention fusion strategies allow multiple text-driven concepts to be blended within the attention layers of the diffusion model, enabling fine-grained compositional edits without per-video fine-tuning. These zero-shot capabilities are particularly valuable in practical settings where acquiring paired video-edit training data is prohibitively expensive.

Fine-tuning-based approaches offer an alternative by adapting pretrained image diffusion models to video through lightweight optimization on the source video itself. Frameworks in this category, such as Dreamix, combine low-resolution temporal information extracted from the source video with high-resolution content synthesized by the image diffusion model, guided by a target text prompt. The low-resolution video features serve as a temporal anchor that preserves motion and scene structure from the original footage, while the high-resolution diffusion synthesis supplies visual quality and adherence to the target description. This combination allows general video editing operations including appearance transformation, object manipulation, and stylistic changes while maintaining temporal plausibility.

A key challenge across all these methods is balancing edit fidelity with temporal consistency. Image diffusion models process each frame independently by default, making it non-trivial to ensure that textures, lighting, and object appearances remain coherent over time. Solutions such as cross-frame attention sharing, optical flow-guided warping, and noise initialization strategies derived from the original video have each been explored to mitigate flickering and temporal drift. The transformer backbone's inherent capacity for long-range dependency modeling, as demonstrated in architectures like [74] and [90], also suggests promising directions for building temporally aware video editing models on top of diffusion transformer foundations.

Overall, video editing via image diffusion models represents a rapidly maturing subfield that inherits the powerful text-conditioning and high-fidelity synthesis capabilities of image diffusion systems while progressively addressing the unique temporal demands of video content. The progression from concept-level adaptation and knowledge protection in static images to temporally coherent video editing underscores the broad and expanding scope of controllable diffusion-based generation, reflecting the field's continued movement toward more expressive, flexible, and responsible generative systems.

## 7 Broader Applications and Downstream Tasks

### 7.1 Text-to-3D Generation

The intersection of text-to-image diffusion models and 3D content creation has emerged as one of the most exciting frontiers in generative AI. By leveraging the rich visual priors encoded in large-scale pretrained text-to-image diffusion models, researchers have developed a diverse array of techniques for generating 3D assets, scenes, and representations directly from natural language prompts, without requiring extensive 3D training datasets.

**Score Distillation Sampling and NeRF-Based Optimization.** A foundational paradigm in this space is Score Distillation Sampling (SDS), which optimizes a 3D representation such as a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) by minimizing a diffusion-based score loss computed from renderings of the 3D model at random viewpoints. This approach, exemplified by DreamFusion, treats the pretrained text-to-image diffusion model as a differentiable critic that guides 3D geometry and appearance to be consistent with the target text prompt. The SDS objective allows gradient information to flow from the 2D diffusion model into the 3D representation without requiring paired 3D supervision, making it broadly applicable across diverse text descriptions. NeRF optimization strategies continue to play a central role in text-to-3D generation, with numerous works exploring improved NeRF parameterizations, coarse-to-fine optimization schedules, and hybrid representations that combine implicit neural fields with explicit geometric proxies.

**Multi-View Distillation and Feed-Forward Generation.** Building on the SDS framework, multi-view distillation techniques have been developed to improve geometric consistency and reduce artifacts common in single-view optimization. Rather than relying on random viewpoint sampling alone, multi-view approaches explicitly encourage consistency across multiple rendered perspectives, leading to more coherent 3D structures. Work such as ET3D [91] demonstrates the potential of leveraging images generated by pretrained text-to-image diffusion models to supervise a text-conditioned 3D generative adversarial network, enabling efficient 3D asset generation in approximately 8 milliseconds on consumer hardware via a single forward pass. This feed-forward paradigm stands in contrast to per-asset optimization approaches, dramatically reducing inference time and computational overhead while maintaining competitive visual quality. More broadly, feed-forward 3D generative networks represent an important direction where a neural network is trained to directly predict 3D representations from text or image inputs in a single forward pass, amortizing the optimization cost across many training examples. These models often combine image-based supervision from large-scale 2D datasets with geometric supervision from available 3D datasets or synthetic renders, enabling fast and scalable 3D generation pipelines.

**Compositional and Scene-Level Generation.** Compositional 3D scene generation extends object-level approaches to multi-object scenes, where individual object representations are independently optimized and subsequently composed into coherent spatial arrangements guided by structured text prompts or scene graphs. This direction reflects a growing interest in moving beyond isolated asset generation toward holistic scene synthesis, demanding tighter integration between language understanding, spatial reasoning, and 3D representation.

**Hybrid Pipelines and Cascaded Refinement.** Hybrid pipelines that combine coarse 3D generation with high-quality refinement stages have also shown considerable promise, mirroring the cascaded diffusion strategies prevalent in 2D image synthesis [10]. In such pipelines, a low-fidelity 3D scaffold is first generated rapidly and then progressively refined using diffusion-based upsampling or texture synthesis models, resulting in detailed and visually compelling 3D assets. These approaches highlight a broader trend of transferring architectural insights from the 2D text-to-image domain into the 3D generation setting, underscoring the central role that pretrained diffusion models play as foundational components for next-generation 3D content creation systems. Taken together, the methods surveyed in this section illustrate how the implicit geometric knowledge and powerful generative priors of text-to-image diffusion models can be systematically repurposed for 3D synthesis, a theme that connects closely to the viewpoint-conditioned generation and novel view synthesis techniques discussed in the following section.

### 7.2 Novel View Synthesis and 3D Scene Understanding

Text-to-image diffusion models trained on massive image-text corpora acquire rich implicit knowledge about the three-dimensional structure of the visual world, a property that researchers have increasingly sought to leverage for novel view synthesis and 3D scene understanding tasks. This direction naturally extends the 3D content creation techniques discussed in the preceding section—where Score Distillation Sampling and NeRF-based optimization form the backbone of text-to-3D pipelines—by examining how the same pretrained diffusion models can be repurposed for viewpoint-conditioned generation and geometric scene understanding. Because these models learn to associate textual descriptions with visually plausible renderings from many different perspectives, they inherently encode geometric priors that can be exploited without explicit 3D supervision.

**Implicit 3D Knowledge in Diffusion Models.** The denoising process in diffusion models requires the network to reason about object shape, lighting, and occlusion in order to produce coherent images. Studies analyzing the internal representations of these models have shown that intermediate feature maps and cross-attention activations encode spatially structured information about scene geometry, providing a foundation for 3D-aware applications. This implicit geometric understanding motivates using pretrained diffusion models as powerful priors for novel view synthesis rather than training dedicated 3D models from scratch, and it connects naturally to the broader observation—developed further in the following section on vector graphics synthesis—that the visual knowledge encoded in diffusion models extends well beyond the specific pixel-space output domain on which they were originally trained.

**Viewpoint-Conditioned Generation.** A natural extension of text-conditioned synthesis is to additionally condition the generative process on a target camera viewpoint. By injecting viewpoint descriptors—such as azimuth and elevation angles or camera extrinsic matrices—into the conditioning signals of diffusion models, it becomes possible to generate images of a scene or object from arbitrary viewing directions. These viewpoint-conditioned approaches build on the cross-attention and classifier-free guidance mechanisms central to latent diffusion frameworks, enabling flexible control over camera pose alongside textual content description.

**Single-View and Sparse-View 3D Reconstruction.** Diffusion models have been applied to the particularly challenging problem of reconstructing 3D structure from a single image or from a sparse set of input views. In this setting, the strong generative prior of a pretrained diffusion model compensates for the severe under-determination of the reconstruction problem by hallucinating geometrically plausible unseen regions consistent with the observed views. Score Distillation Sampling (SDS), introduced in DreamFusion and related approaches, exemplifies this paradigm by distilling the knowledge of a 2D diffusion prior into a 3D representation such as a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) through repeated rendering and gradient-based optimization—complementing the feed-forward and multi-view distillation strategies surveyed in the preceding section by focusing on the reconstruction rather than purely generative regime.

**Camera Viewpoint Control via Textual Inversion.** Textual inversion techniques, which optimize continuous token embeddings to capture the appearance of a reference concept, have been adapted to encode camera viewpoint information. By learning viewpoint-specific pseudo-tokens, these methods enable users to specify desired perspectives through text-like inputs, seamlessly integrating viewpoint control into the standard text-conditioning pipeline of diffusion models. This approach provides an intuitive interface for users unfamiliar with explicit camera parameterizations while leveraging the expressive text-conditioning machinery of models such as Stable Diffusion.

**Stylized 3D Generation Driven by Visual Prompts.** Beyond geometric reconstruction, diffusion models have been used to synthesize stylized 3D content guided by visual reference images. By combining image-based style prompts with 3D-aware generation pipelines, it is possible to produce novel views of objects or scenes that faithfully reproduce the artistic style encoded in a reference image. Such methods typically alternate between rendering candidate views and updating the 3D representation under guidance from the diffusion prior, enabling the joint optimization of geometry and appearance. This stylized generation direction connects closely to the broader personalization and style transfer capabilities of diffusion models, demonstrating how the same underlying pretrained models can be repurposed for diverse 3D synthesis objectives—a versatility that, as the following section illustrates, extends even further to non-raster output domains such as vector graphics.

**Challenges and Outlook.** Despite promising progress, novel view synthesis with diffusion models faces several open challenges. Multi-view consistency remains difficult to enforce because standard 2D diffusion models process each rendered view independently, potentially producing contradictory geometry across views. Incorporating explicit 3D representations, enforcing cross-view attention constraints, or training multi-view-consistent diffusion models are active research directions that build directly on the compositional and hybrid pipeline strategies introduced in the preceding section. Furthermore, computational costs associated with iterative score distillation are substantial, motivating the development of feed-forward 3D generative networks that amortize reconstruction costs. As diffusion models continue to scale and as training datasets increasingly include multi-view and depth-annotated imagery, the quality and consistency of diffusion-based novel view synthesis and 3D scene understanding are expected to improve significantly, further solidifying the role of pretrained text-to-image diffusion models as general-purpose visual knowledge sources adaptable to a wide range of structured output modalities.

### 7.3 Vector Graphics and Scalable Image Synthesis

Vector graphics representations such as Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) offer significant advantages over raster images for design and digital art applications, including resolution independence, compact file sizes, and ease of editing. Extending text-to-image diffusion models to generate such representations has emerged as an active research direction that bridges the gap between neural generative modeling and structured, scalable visual outputs. This direction complements the broader exploration of diffusion models beyond conventional 2D image synthesis—spanning novel view synthesis, 3D scene understanding, and discriminative perception tasks—by demonstrating how the rich priors encoded in pretrained diffusion models can be redirected toward fundamentally different output modalities and representational formats.

**Differentiable Rasterization as a Bridge to Vector Synthesis.** One prominent family of approaches leverages differentiable rasterization to enable gradient-based optimization of vector graphics parameters. In these frameworks, a set of vector primitives—such as Bézier curves, filled paths, or geometric shapes—are rendered into a raster image via a differentiable rasterizer, and the resulting image is evaluated against a target or guided by a pretrained diffusion model. This enables the optimization of vector parameters using image-space losses derived from the diffusion model's learned representations, effectively translating the rich generative knowledge of diffusion models into the structured domain of vector graphics. The approach mirrors the adaptation strategies seen in perception applications, where frozen or lightly fine-tuned diffusion backbones are repurposed for new tasks through carefully designed interfaces rather than end-to-end retraining.

**Score Distillation Sampling for Vector Graphics.** Score Distillation Sampling (SDS), originally proposed in the context of text-to-3D generation, has been adapted for vector graphics synthesis. In this paradigm, the score function of a pretrained text-to-image diffusion model provides gradient signals that guide the optimization of differentiable vector representations toward configurations that match a given text prompt. By treating the rasterized vector image as a differentiable function of the vector parameters and backpropagating SDS gradients through the rasterizer, these methods can synthesize SVG outputs that are semantically consistent with complex textual descriptions while remaining fully scalable and editable. This connection to SDS-based 3D generation highlights a unifying principle: the denoising score of a pretrained 2D diffusion model serves as a versatile optimization signal applicable across a range of non-raster output domains.

**Stylistic Diversity in Vector Synthesis.** Beyond photorealistic or illustrative SVG generation, diffusion-based vector synthesis methods have demonstrated support for diverse artistic styles. Approaches targeting pixel art leverage the discrete, grid-aligned nature of this style by constraining the optimization space accordingly, while sketch-generation methods exploit the sparsity and abstraction inherent in line-based representations. These stylistic capabilities make diffusion-guided vector synthesis particularly valuable for digital artists and designers who require outputs that can be directly integrated into professional design workflows without lossy rasterization artifacts.

**Advantages of Vector Outputs and Broader Applicability.** The advantages of vector outputs extend beyond scalability. Because vector graphics encode images as parameterized geometric primitives, the resulting files are compact and interpretable, facilitating downstream editing operations such as recoloring, reshaping, and layer-based manipulation. This is in contrast to pixel-space diffusion outputs, which require additional post-processing steps for vectorization and often introduce artifacts. The combination of text-conditioned diffusion guidance with differentiable vector representations thus represents a compelling direction for high-quality, editable, and scalable visual content creation, with broad applicability in fields including graphic design, user interface asset generation, and digital illustration. Taken together with the preceding discussions of 3D-aware generation and perception-oriented adaptation, vector graphics synthesis underscores the versatility of pretrained text-to-image diffusion models as general-purpose visual knowledge sources whose utility extends well beyond the pixel-space images on which they were originally trained—a theme that carries forward into their application as discriminative visual perception backbones in the following section.

### 7.4 Visual Perception Tasks

Beyond generative tasks, pretrained text-to-image diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable potential as visual perception backbones for a wide range of downstream discriminative tasks. This repurposing of generative models for perceptual understanding represents a natural extension of the broader trend explored throughout this survey—where the rich priors encoded in large-scale diffusion models are redirected toward fundamentally different objectives—and complements the preceding discussion of vector graphics synthesis by further illustrating how pretrained diffusion knowledge can be systematically unlocked for non-generative applications. The rich semantic representations encoded within these models, acquired through large-scale training on diverse image-text pairs, provide a strong foundation for understanding and interpreting visual scenes in ways that extend well beyond image synthesis.

One of the most compelling observations motivating this line of research is that the internal feature representations of diffusion models, particularly those extracted from intermediate UNet layers and cross-attention maps, capture highly structured semantic and spatial information. Cross-attention maps, for instance, inherently encode correspondences between textual tokens and spatial image regions, offering natural supervisory signals for tasks requiring precise localization and semantic understanding. This has been demonstrated in works such as [92], which reveals that the cross-attention layers of diffusion models intrinsically provide annotation-free attention masks aligned with corresponding text inputs on generated images, enabling downstream tasks such as detection, segmentation, and image-text retrieval without explicit supervision.

For semantic segmentation, diffusion features have proven competitive with or superior to discriminatively trained representations. Researchers have leveraged the hierarchical feature maps produced by the UNet denoising backbone of models such as Stable Diffusion [35] to produce dense per-pixel predictions. The key insight is that the bottleneck and decoder layers of the UNet aggregate multi-scale spatial and semantic context during the denoising process, forming rich representations that are well-suited to dense prediction. Adaptation strategies in this setting commonly involve freezing the pretrained diffusion backbone and training lightweight segmentation heads on top of the extracted features, exploiting the frozen model's semantic richness while minimizing the risk of catastrophic forgetting.

Referring image segmentation, which requires localizing image regions described by free-form natural language expressions, aligns particularly naturally with the architecture of text-conditioned diffusion models. The cross-attention mechanism that couples textual and visual representations during generation can be repurposed to localize specific objects or regions described by referring expressions. By extracting and aggregating attention maps associated with noun phrases or object tokens in the input text, these models can produce segmentation masks without requiring specialized architectures trained from scratch.

Depth estimation represents another active area where diffusion priors have shown strong performance. Pretrained diffusion models encode geometric and structural scene priors that, while learned implicitly during image synthesis, generalize well to monocular depth estimation. Adaptation approaches in this domain include fine-tuning the denoising network to directly predict depth maps as output modalities, or training lightweight adapter modules that redirect the generative prior toward regression targets. The latent diffusion framework [35] is particularly attractive here, as operating in a compact latent space reduces the computational overhead of dense prediction while maintaining high spatial resolution through the pretrained decoder.

Open-vocabulary object segmentation, where models must segment objects described by arbitrary textual queries not seen during training, benefits directly from the vision-language alignment baked into text-to-image diffusion models through contrastive and generative pretraining. The text conditioning mechanism enables seamless handling of novel semantic categories, as the model's cross-attention layers can associate spatial regions with diverse lexical descriptions encountered during pretraining. This is in contrast to closed-vocabulary segmentation approaches that require category-specific supervision.

Pose estimation is a more spatially precise task, yet diffusion model representations have shown promise here as well, particularly when combined with keypoint-specific textual prompts or meta-prompt strategies that query the model with body-part descriptions. The spatial precision of cross-attention maps and the multi-scale feature hierarchy of the UNet backbone provide complementary information for localizing fine-grained keypoints.

A unifying theme across all these perception applications is the importance of careful adaptation strategies. Simply using diffusion model features as drop-in replacements for conventional CNN or ViT encoders is often suboptimal due to the stochastic and timestep-dependent nature of the denoising process. Effective adaptation involves selecting appropriate denoising timesteps at which features are most semantically informative, aggregating features across multiple timesteps or noise levels to capture complementary information, and designing lightweight task-specific heads that align with the conditioning interfaces of the generative backbone. Additionally, text-image alignment techniques, such as crafting task-specific meta-prompts that activate relevant semantic features within the model, have emerged as a powerful and low-cost strategy for eliciting perception capabilities from generative models. These prompt-based steering mechanisms connect directly to the adaptation strategies discussed in the context of semantic correspondence in the following section, where optimizing text embeddings serves as a similarly effective mechanism for eliciting task-relevant internal representations from frozen generative backbones.

Taken together, these developments underscore that large-scale text-to-image diffusion models such as those built on latent diffusion frameworks [35] are not merely generative tools but encode rich, generalizable visual knowledge that can be systematically unlocked for perception tasks. The availability of strong pretrained diffusion backbones opens new directions for data-efficient visual understanding, particularly in settings where labeled data for specific perception tasks is scarce. As will be explored in the following section, this perceptual richness extends to the domain of semantic correspondence and dense matching, where diffusion features have similarly demonstrated strong capacity for capturing the structured semantic relationships needed to establish accurate cross-image correspondences.

### 7.5 Semantic Correspondence and Dense Matching

Semantic correspondence and dense matching represent an important class of computer vision tasks that require establishing pixel- or region-level correspondences between semantically related images, even across significant appearance variations, viewpoint changes, and intra-class differences. Building naturally upon the perception capabilities discussed in the previous section, recent research has demonstrated that the rich internal representations learned by text-to-image diffusion models during large-scale training encode both semantic and spatial information that proves highly effective for these challenging matching tasks, offering a compelling alternative to purely discriminative feature learning approaches.

The key insight motivating this line of work is that diffusion models, trained to synthesize images conditioned on textual descriptions, must implicitly learn to represent semantic concepts, object parts, and their spatial relationships in order to generate coherent and realistic outputs. The intermediate activations of the denoising UNet backbone, particularly the features extracted from attention layers and residual blocks at various spatial resolutions and noise timesteps, capture multi-scale semantic structure that naturally supports correspondence estimation. Unlike features derived from purely discriminative models trained with classification objectives, diffusion features reflect the generative model's internal understanding of how visual concepts relate across diverse instances, making them particularly well-suited for cross-image matching tasks where the correspondence must be grounded in shared semantic content rather than low-level appearance similarity.

A central methodological challenge in exploiting diffusion features for semantic correspondence lies in selecting and combining features from the appropriate timesteps and network layers. Research has shown that features extracted at intermediate noise levels tend to encode the most useful semantic information, while features from very early or late timesteps in the denoising trajectory capture either overly coarse or overly fine-grained signals. Furthermore, since the standard inference process of text-to-image diffusion models requires a text conditioning signal, approaches to semantic correspondence must either fix a generic text prompt or develop methods for optimizing prompt embeddings to elicit the most discriminative internal representations for a given pair of images. Prompt optimization strategies that tune the text embedding to maximize cross-image feature similarity have been explored, allowing the model's internal representations to be steered toward emphasizing the semantic content most relevant to identifying correspondences between specific image pairs. This prompt-based steering connects closely to the broader theme of adaptation strategies discussed in the context of perception tasks, where carefully crafted conditioning inputs serve as a low-cost mechanism for eliciting task-relevant representations from frozen generative backbones.

Another important direction involves fusing diffusion model features with complementary representations derived from self-supervised vision models. Self-supervised methods such as DINOv2 learn strong semantic features through masked image modeling and self-distillation objectives applied to large image collections, resulting in representations that exhibit strong spatial consistency and semantic grouping. Combining the generative semantic richness of diffusion features with the spatially precise and robust representations of DINOv2 through late fusion or learned aggregation strategies has been shown to yield correspondence features that outperform either modality alone on standard evaluation benchmarks. This complementarity arises because diffusion features excel at capturing fine-grained semantic part correspondences, while self-supervised features provide stable spatial structure that anchors the matching process.

Evaluation of these approaches is typically conducted on established dense semantic correspondence benchmarks including SPair-71k, which contains 70,958 image pairs spanning 18 categories with challenging viewpoint and scale variations, and PF-Pascal, which covers 1,351 image pairs across 20 Pascal VOC categories. Performance is measured using the percentage of correctly transferred keypoints (PCK) metric, which assesses whether predicted correspondences fall within a threshold distance of the ground truth keypoint locations. Diffusion-based correspondence methods have demonstrated competitive or state-of-the-art performance on these benchmarks, suggesting that the internal representations of large-scale generative models capture semantic structure that is broadly useful beyond their primary image synthesis objective.

The success of text-to-image diffusion features for semantic correspondence highlights a broader principle that large generative models trained with vision-language objectives develop rich, transferable representations that encode scene understanding at multiple levels of abstraction. This connects both to the preceding discussion of diffusion models as perception backbones and to subsequent challenges such as visual text rendering, where the model's internal representations must capture not only semantic structure but also fine-grained structural and spatial properties. Taken together, these findings suggest that continued scaling of text-to-image diffusion models may yield progressively stronger transferable representations as a beneficial byproduct of improved generative capabilities, with implications extending across the full spectrum of visual understanding tasks.

### 7.6 Text Rendering and Multilingual Visual Text Generation

## 7.6 Visual Text Rendering and Multilingual Generation

Generating accurate and legible textual content within synthesized images remains one of the most persistent challenges in text-to-image diffusion models. While the preceding discussion of semantic correspondence demonstrated that diffusion models develop rich internal representations encoding semantic and spatial structure, visual text rendering exposes a complementary limitation: the same generative pipeline that excels at capturing holistic scene semantics frequently struggles with the precise character-level fidelity that typographic content demands. Unlike natural scene elements where approximate visual plausibility suffices, text requires exact character sequences, correct spatial arrangement, and stylistic consistency with the surrounding image context. Even state-of-the-art diffusion models frequently produce garbled, misspelled, or visually incoherent text when prompted to render words or sentences within generated imagery.

A central difficulty lies in the fact that standard text-to-image pipelines, which rely on semantic embeddings from encoders such as CLIP, are not inherently designed to capture the fine-grained structural properties of individual characters and glyphs. Where CLIP embeddings provide strong semantic grounding for scene content and object relationships, they treat text prompts as holistic semantic units rather than as sequences of visually distinct characters, making them poorly suited for guiding the precise pixel-level rendering that legible typography requires. To address this, researchers have explored auxiliary latent modules that inject explicit glyph and position information into the diffusion process. Such modules typically encode the intended text string at the character or glyph level and condition the denoising network on both the desired content and its spatial location within the image, allowing the model to distinguish between the high-level semantic meaning of a caption and the precise visual form of rendered characters. This form of fine-grained spatial conditioning connects naturally to the layout-guided and position-aware generation strategies discussed elsewhere in this survey, applying the same principle of explicit structural conditioning to the particularly demanding case of typographic content.

OCR-informed strategies represent another important direction. By incorporating optical character recognition signals during training, models can be guided to produce text regions that are not only visually plausible but also machine-readable, closing the feedback loop between generation and recognition. Training objectives specifically designed to penalize character-level errors and encourage structural regularity have also proven beneficial, enabling models to better approximate the appearance of real typographic content across diverse fonts, sizes, and orientations.

The multilingual dimension adds further complexity. Visual text generation must contend with scripts that vary dramatically in character inventory, stroke complexity, and reading direction, from Latin and Cyrillic alphabets to Chinese, Arabic, Devanagari, and beyond. Constructing large-scale multilingual text image datasets has therefore become an important prerequisite for benchmarking progress in this area. Such datasets must capture the diversity of real-world text appearances, including variation in font style, background texture, lighting conditions, and language-specific typographic conventions, providing the supervision signal necessary to train and evaluate models capable of generalizing across scripts and languages.

Recent diffusion-based multilingual visual text generation and editing approaches have begun to combine these ingredients—glyph conditioning, OCR feedback, dedicated training objectives, and multilingual data—into unified frameworks that can render coherent text in multiple languages within a single generative model. The challenge of maintaining visual consistency across such diverse typographic requirements shares important structural similarities with the cross-frame consistency demands of story visualization discussed in the following section, where identity and style must be preserved not across scripts but across narrative panels. While significant progress has been made, faithful and robust visual text generation across arbitrary scripts and complex compositional scenes remains an open research challenge, with implications for applications ranging from automated graphic design and document synthesis to accessibility tools and cross-lingual visual communication.

### 7.7 Story Visualization and Narrative Image Generation

## 7.7 Story Visualization and Multi-Panel Synthesis

Story visualization represents a compelling application of text-to-image diffusion models, requiring the generation of sequences of semantically and visually coherent images that collectively narrate a story. Unlike single-image synthesis, this task demands consistency of characters, scenes, and objects across multiple frames, posing unique challenges related to identity preservation, temporal coherence, and compositional fidelity. In this respect, story visualization occupies a natural bridge between the visual text rendering challenges discussed in the previous section and the full temporal synthesis demands addressed in the context of video generation, sharing concerns about cross-frame consistency while operating at the level of discrete narrative panels rather than continuous motion sequences.

A central challenge in story visualization is maintaining character identity and scene continuity across panels. Naive application of text-to-image models to successive story sentences typically yields visually inconsistent outputs, where characters change appearance, backgrounds shift arbitrarily, and object relationships are disrupted between frames. Methods addressing this challenge often employ reference-guided generation or learned character embeddings that persist across multiple synthesis steps, ensuring that named entities maintain recognizable visual attributes throughout the narrative sequence.

Layout-guided generation plays an important role in storytelling contexts, where spatial placement of characters and objects must reflect narrative logic. By conditioning on bounding boxes or segmentation maps derived from story descriptions, models can arrange subjects according to the spatial relationships implied by the text, producing images where scene composition matches narrative intent. Such spatial grounding is particularly valuable in multi-panel comic-style synthesis, where panel-to-panel transitions must respect both visual continuity and semantic progression.

Large language models have emerged as powerful scene planners for story visualization. By leveraging chain-of-thought reasoning, LLMs can decompose a narrative paragraph into structured scene descriptions, specifying the characters present, their spatial arrangements, and the relevant background elements for each panel. Frameworks that integrate LLM-based layout generation with downstream diffusion synthesis benefit from the rich semantic understanding and compositional reasoning of language models, translating abstract narrative descriptions into grounded visual layouts that guide image generation. This coarse-to-fine planning paradigm, where LLMs produce intermediate structured representations that bridge textual stories and pixel-level synthesis, echoes approaches explored in layout-conditional diffusion generation more broadly.

Compositional multi-panel synthesis additionally requires that the overall aesthetic style, lighting conditions, and color palette remain harmonious across frames. Some methods address this through style consistency constraints or by conditioning all panels on a shared global style embedding extracted from an initial reference image. Others employ attention-based mechanisms to propagate appearance features from previously generated frames into the synthesis of subsequent ones, reducing visual drift over longer narrative sequences.

Evaluation of story visualization systems requires metrics beyond standard single-image quality measures such as FID or CLIP score. Character consistency across frames, background coherence, and narrative faithfulness are dimensions that standard benchmarks do not adequately capture, motivating the development of specialized evaluation protocols. Human evaluation remains an important complement to automated metrics in this domain, assessing whether generated image sequences are perceived as telling a coherent and visually appealing story.

Overall, story visualization sits at the intersection of natural language understanding, compositional generation, and cross-frame consistency, making it one of the most demanding applications of text-to-image diffusion models. Progress in this area increasingly draws on the complementary strengths of large language models for semantic planning and diffusion models for high-quality visual synthesis. The consistency and temporal coherence requirements central to story visualization naturally motivate the broader extension of diffusion models to continuous video generation and temporal synthesis, which we examine in the following section.

### 7.8 Video Generation and Temporal Synthesis

## 7.8 Video Generation and Temporal Synthesis

The extension of text-to-image diffusion models to video generation and temporal synthesis represents one of the most active and challenging frontiers in generative modeling. Building naturally upon the cross-frame consistency and sequential coherence demands explored in the context of story visualization, video generation confronts these challenges in their most demanding form—requiring not merely coherent discrete panels but continuous, temporally smooth motion sequences. While image diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in producing high-quality static visuals, translating these capabilities to the temporal domain requires addressing fundamentally new challenges, including maintaining consistency across frames, modeling motion dynamics, and managing the substantially increased computational demands of processing spatiotemporal data.

A central challenge in video generation is ensuring temporal coherence—generated frames must not only be individually plausible but must also exhibit smooth, realistic transitions over time. Early approaches to video synthesis leveraged pretrained image diffusion models as strong visual priors, extending them to the video domain through the introduction of temporal attention mechanisms that operate across frame sequences. These temporal attention layers complement existing spatial attention modules, allowing the model to reason jointly about appearance and motion. However, naively inflating image diffusion models into video generators often produces flickering artifacts and inconsistent object identities across frames, motivating more principled architectural extensions.

Training-free bridging frameworks offer an attractive paradigm for leveraging the rich visual knowledge encoded in image diffusion models without requiring costly video-specific retraining. By coupling pretrained image diffusion models with dedicated video diffusion components, such frameworks can propagate frame-level edits through self-attention feature injection and cross-frame consistency constraints, enabling temporally coherent generation from text prompts. These approaches are particularly valuable in settings where large-scale paired text-video training data is scarce or expensive to obtain.

Controllable video generation extends the controllability principles developed for image synthesis—such as layout conditioning, structural guidance, and reference-image control—into the temporal domain. Methods in this category allow users to specify not only the content and style of generated videos but also motion trajectories, camera movements, and scene dynamics. Achieving such fine-grained control requires conditioning mechanisms that operate consistently across the temporal dimension, ensuring that spatial control signals are coherently propagated throughout the entire generated sequence.

Video inpainting and outpainting represent important specialized applications of text-conditioned video diffusion models. Video inpainting involves filling in masked or corrupted regions across frames in a temporally consistent manner, while video outpainting extends the spatial or temporal extent of an existing video clip. Both tasks demand that the diffusion model reason simultaneously about the spatial context within individual frames and the temporal context across the sequence, making them significantly more challenging than their image-domain counterparts.

The unique computational demands of video generation—arising from the need to process long sequences of high-resolution frames—have spurred the development of efficient spatiotemporal attention mechanisms, factorized attention designs that decouple spatial and temporal processing, and latent video diffusion frameworks that operate in compressed representation spaces analogous to latent diffusion models for images. These efficiency innovations are critical for making text-to-video generation practically deployable, as the memory and compute requirements of naive full-attention video models scale poorly with sequence length and resolution.

Beyond purely generative tasks, text-to-image diffusion models have been adapted for video editing workflows, where the goal is to modify the content, style, or semantics of an existing video guided by a text prompt while preserving the structural and temporal integrity of the original footage. These video editing pipelines typically involve inverting the source video into the diffusion model's noise space, applying text-conditioned modifications, and reconstructing the edited output with temporal consistency enforced through attention feature reuse or optical flow-based warping.

Overall, the extension of text-to-image diffusion models to video generation and temporal synthesis is a rapidly evolving area that inherits the strengths of image diffusion modeling while confronting a distinct set of challenges rooted in temporal dynamics, computational scalability, and the need for consistent spatiotemporal reasoning. The rich visual and semantic representations developed in service of video generation—particularly the internal attention structures and feature encodings of pretrained diffusion models—also hold significant promise beyond purely generative applications, as explored in the following section, where these representations are repurposed as scalable data factories for downstream perception and recognition tasks. Continued progress in efficient architectures, temporal conditioning mechanisms, and large-scale video-text pretraining is expected to yield increasingly capable and controllable text-to-video generation systems in the near future.

### 7.9 Data Augmentation and Synthetic Dataset Generation

## 7.9 Synthetic Data Generation for Downstream Tasks

Text-to-image diffusion models have emerged as powerful tools for synthetic data generation, offering a compelling alternative to costly real-world data collection for training downstream recognition and perception models. Building upon the rich visual and semantic representations developed across the architectural and application advances discussed in preceding sections, pretrained diffusion models can synthesize diverse training images spanning a wide range of visual concepts, scenes, and conditions that may be difficult or expensive to capture in the real world. This capacity for scalable, controllable data synthesis positions diffusion models not only as generative engines but also as foundational infrastructure for broader computer vision pipelines.

One particularly promising direction involves exploiting the internal representations of diffusion models—specifically their cross-attention maps—to construct annotation-free datasets. These cross-attention maps naturally align with the textual tokens used to condition generation, providing spatial localization information about objects and attributes without requiring manual labeling. [92] demonstrates this approach explicitly, showing that diffusion models' cross-attention layers inherently provide annotation-free attention masks aligned with corresponding text inputs on generated images. This work further investigates unsupervised learning techniques—including contrastive learning, masked modeling, and vision-language pretraining—applied to diffusion-generated images, introducing customized solutions that exploit these free attention masks to consistently improve performance across downstream tasks such as image classification, detection, segmentation, and image-text retrieval.

The potential of synthetic data generated by text-to-image diffusion models for benefiting image recognition has been increasingly recognized. Diffusion-generated images can complement real datasets, particularly in low-data regimes or for long-tail categories where real samples are scarce. By crafting targeted text prompts, practitioners can generate images covering underrepresented classes or challenging conditions, thereby improving the robustness and generalization of trained models. Furthermore, the controllability afforded by conditioning mechanisms such as cross-attention and classifier-free guidance allows fine-grained control over the content, style, and composition of generated training data, enabling systematic coverage of desired visual distributions.

Beyond classification, synthetic datasets generated by diffusion models show promise for detection and segmentation tasks. The availability of cross-attention masks as weak spatial annotations significantly reduces the annotation burden typically associated with dense prediction tasks. This connection between generative conditioning signals and structured spatial supervision further complements the cross-modal representation learning paradigms explored in the following section, where diffusion model internals are similarly repurposed to bridge synthesis and understanding across modalities. As diffusion models continue to improve in fidelity and controllability, their role as scalable data factories for perception tasks is expected to grow, potentially transforming how training datasets are constructed across a wide spectrum of computer vision applications.

### 7.10 Cross-Modal Retrieval and Multimodal Generation Frameworks

## 7.10 Cross-Modal Retrieval and Multimodal Generation

Cross-modal retrieval and multimodal generation represent a rapidly expanding frontier for text-to-image diffusion models, where the rich vision-language representations learned during large-scale pretraining are repurposed to bridge synthesis and understanding across modalities. Building upon the synthetic data generation capabilities discussed in the preceding section—where diffusion model internals such as cross-attention maps were exploited to construct annotation-free datasets—this section examines how similar representational resources can be extended to enable bidirectional vision-language tasks, including image captioning, cross-modal retrieval, and unified multimodal generation.

**Multimodal Generation with Interleaved Inputs.** Modern diffusion-based frameworks increasingly operate on interleaved sequences of textual and visual tokens, enabling unified models that can both generate images from text and reason about visual content in a single coherent architecture. This paradigm draws inspiration from transformer-based designs that treat heterogeneous inputs uniformly, analogous to how vision transformers process image patches and text tokens as equivalent sequence elements. The scalability of transformer backbones, as demonstrated in diffusion transformer studies [74], provides a natural foundation for such unified multimodal pipelines, since the same architectural primitives that scale well for image generation can accommodate mixed-modality token sequences.

**Unified Discrete Diffusion for Vision-Language Generation.** Discrete diffusion formulations offer a compelling pathway for simultaneous vision-language generation, where both image tokens and text tokens undergo a shared diffusion process over discrete codebooks. By operating in a common discrete latent space, such approaches avoid the modality gap inherent in systems that process text and images through entirely separate encoders and decoders. Transformer architectures with long skip connections, as explored in U-ViT style designs [90], are particularly amenable to this setting because they naturally handle heterogeneous token types—time embeddings, text condition tokens, and noisy image patch tokens—within a unified sequence framework, making the extension to interleaved vision-language sequences architecturally straightforward.

**Multilingual and Multi-Modal Image Generation.** Extending text-to-image diffusion systems to multilingual settings requires text encoders capable of encoding prompts from diverse languages into shared semantic representations that align with visual content. Large-scale pretraining of diffusion models on multilingual caption data, combined with language-agnostic text encoders, enables cross-lingual image retrieval and generation within a single model. The transformer backbone's demonstrated scalability [93] is critical here, as multilingual vocabulary expansions and cross-lingual alignment objectives demand models with substantial capacity to disentangle language-specific surface form from language-agnostic semantic content.

**Diffusion-Based Image Captioning.** Beyond synthesis, diffusion model representations can be inverted or probed for image captioning. Internal cross-attention activations within denoising networks encode rich correspondences between visual regions and textual tokens—a property closely related to the annotation-free attention masks leveraged for downstream recognition tasks discussed previously—and these representations can be leveraged to generate descriptive captions by conditioning an autoregressive decoder on diffusion features extracted at intermediate denoising timesteps. This bidirectional use of the diffusion model—as both a generative synthesis engine and a visual understanding backbone—demonstrates the versatility of representations learned during large-scale text-conditioned training. The transferability of diffusion backbones to perception tasks has been explicitly demonstrated [94], providing evidence that the feature hierarchies within pretrained diffusion UNets and transformers encode semantically meaningful information well-suited to captioning and retrieval tasks.

**Cross-Modal Retrieval Enabled by Diffusion Representations.** The joint vision-language representations implicit in trained diffusion models enable cross-modal retrieval applications, where queries in one modality (e.g., text) are used to retrieve semantically relevant items in another (e.g., images), or vice versa. Because diffusion models are trained to denoise images conditioned on text embeddings, their internal representations implicitly align visual and linguistic semantics. Probing studies and feature fusion approaches that combine diffusion features with self-supervised visual representations—analogous to ensemble strategies that merge complementary feature sources—have shown that diffusion-derived embeddings carry distinctive semantic information not captured by discriminatively trained models alone. Hybrid architectures that jointly leverage generative diffusion features and discriminative representations from models such as ViT further enhance retrieval performance by combining complementary inductive strengths [95].

**Scalable Architectures for Unified Multimodal Frameworks.** The architectural trend toward transformer-based diffusion backbones [74; 96] facilitates the construction of unified multimodal generation frameworks, as transformers natively support variable-length, heterogeneous input sequences. GenTron's extension of diffusion transformers from class-conditional to text-conditional generation, and further to video synthesis, exemplifies how a single transformer-based diffusion backbone can be adapted to progressively richer multimodal settings [96]. The scalability and flexibility of such architectures suggest that future unified frameworks will naturally subsume cross-modal retrieval, multilingual generation, and interleaved vision-language synthesis within a single model family, driven by the continued scaling of transformer-based diffusion systems and the accumulation of large-scale multimodal training corpora. Together, the capabilities surveyed across this and preceding sections illustrate a broader trajectory in which text-to-image diffusion models evolve from specialized generative tools into versatile, perception-aware systems capable of supporting a wide spectrum of vision-language applications.

## 8 Evaluation, Safety, and Future Directions

### 8.1 Quantitative Evaluation Metrics and Benchmarks

## 8.1 Metrics and Benchmarks for Text-to-Image Evaluation

Evaluating text-to-image diffusion models rigorously is a foundational challenge, as the quality of generated images and their correspondence to textual prompts must be measured along multiple, often competing dimensions. Over the years, the community has converged on a set of standard quantitative metrics while also developing specialized benchmarks that probe particular model capabilities more precisely. This section surveys the core metrics and benchmark datasets that form the basis of current evaluation practice, setting the stage for a deeper examination of compositional and semantic fidelity evaluation in the following section.

**Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)** is among the most widely adopted metrics for assessing the distributional similarity between generated and real images. FID computes the distance between the statistics of Inception network feature activations extracted from real and generated image sets, with lower scores indicating greater similarity. While FID correlates reasonably well with perceptual image quality, it is sensitive to the choice of reference set and sample size, and does not directly measure semantic alignment between text prompts and generated images. Many landmark text-to-image models, including Imagen [10] and CogView [7], report FID scores on MS-COCO as a standard point of comparison, with Imagen achieving a state-of-the-art zero-shot FID of 7.27 on COCO. Similarly, RAPHAEL [12] reports a competitive FID of 6.61 on MS-COCO, while Kandinsky [11] achieves an FID of 8.03 on the COCO-30K dataset, and Lafite2 [97] reports FID of 6.78 and 8.42 under supervised and zero-shot settings, respectively.

**Inception Score (IS)** is another classical metric that evaluates both image quality and diversity by measuring the sharpness of the conditional label distribution predicted by an Inception classifier alongside the marginal entropy across generated samples. Higher IS values indicate sharper, more diverse images. However, IS does not require a reference dataset of real images, making it susceptible to artifacts specific to the Inception classifier and less reliable for open-domain generation.

**CLIP Score** has emerged as the standard metric for quantifying semantic alignment between generated images and their conditioning text prompts. By computing the cosine similarity between CLIP image and text embeddings, it provides a direct measure of prompt faithfulness. CLIP score has been shown to correlate with human judgments of text-image alignment and is commonly reported alongside FID in model evaluations [98]. However, CLIP score inherits the biases of the CLIP model itself and may reward superficial textual cues rather than deep semantic correspondence.

**Precision and Recall** metrics, adapted from information retrieval to generative modeling, separately quantify fidelity (what fraction of generated samples resemble real images) and diversity (what fraction of real images can be recalled by generated samples). These provide complementary insight beyond FID by decomposing the fidelity-diversity tradeoff, offering a more nuanced view of model behavior that aggregate metrics alone cannot provide.

**Human Evaluation Protocols** remain indispensable, as automated metrics fail to capture all perceptual and semantic nuances. Structured human evaluation studies comparing models across multiple dimensions—image quality, prompt adherence, and aesthetic appeal—have been employed by several model papers. Imagen [10] introduced DrawBench, a comprehensive benchmark consisting of challenging prompts spanning compositionality, counting, spatial relationships, and rare concepts, enabling fine-grained comparison through human side-by-side evaluation. Human raters in that study found Imagen outputs comparable to real COCO images in terms of text alignment. CogView3 [13] similarly relies on human evaluation, demonstrating a 77% preference over SDXL. Additionally, structured multi-task human benchmarks have been proposed that evaluate models across tasks at multiple difficulty levels, revealing systematic gaps in compositional understanding [99].

**MS-COCO** remains the dominant benchmark dataset for zero-shot and supervised text-to-image generation evaluation, providing a large-scale reference distribution of real images paired with captions. The PaintSkills diagnostic benchmark [98] complements COCO by specifically measuring object recognition, counting, and spatial relation understanding, revealing that even state-of-the-art models exhibit significant gaps relative to upper-bound accuracy in compositional tasks.

Despite these advances, a persistent challenge is that aggregate metrics such as FID, IS, and CLIP score capture distributional properties at a holistic level while remaining largely insensitive to fine-grained semantic errors such as attribute misassignment, incorrect object counts, and failed spatial relationships. This insensitivity is not merely a theoretical concern: as models are applied to increasingly complex, multi-object prompts, the gap between strong aggregate scores and poor compositional performance becomes a critical limitation. The design of benchmarks that faithfully reflect real-world generation quality and the full diversity of user prompts remains an open and active research challenge. Studies analyzing large-scale user prompt logs [100] highlight the complexity and variety of prompts encountered in practice, suggesting that controlled benchmark suites may significantly underestimate the difficulty of in-the-wild generation. These limitations motivate the development of specialized evaluation frameworks targeting compositional and semantic fidelity more precisely, which are examined in the following section.

### 8.2 Evaluation of Compositional and Semantic Fidelity

## 8.2 Evaluation of Compositional and Semantic Fidelity

As established in the previous section, standard aggregate metrics such as Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Inception Score (IS), and CLIP score, while widely adopted for assessing overall generation quality and coarse text-image alignment, fall significantly short when it comes to measuring the fine-grained semantic correctness of generated images. These metrics capture distributional properties at a holistic level but are largely insensitive to whether a model has correctly bound attributes to the right objects, placed entities in the correct spatial configurations, or accurately rendered the correct number of objects specified in a prompt. This gap has motivated the development of specialized evaluation frameworks targeting compositional and semantic fidelity more precisely—frameworks that, as will become relevant in the following section, also provide the structured quality signals necessary for reward-based optimization of generative models.

Compositional evaluation benchmarks such as DrawBench and PartiPrompts were designed to probe a broader range of semantic challenges, including spatial relationships, object counting, and multi-concept compositionality. However, even these benchmarks are typically evaluated through human judgments or coarse CLIP-based scores, which do not decompose performance into fine-grained semantic categories. The need for more systematic decomposition of compositional failures has become increasingly apparent as text-to-image models are applied to complex, multi-object prompts where attribute leakage, entity mixing, and incorrect spatial arrangements are common failure modes.

Attribute binding evaluation focuses specifically on whether properties such as color, shape, texture, and size are correctly associated with their intended referent objects rather than being misassigned or blended across entities. Benchmarks targeting attribute binding typically construct prompt sets that systematically vary the number and type of attributes alongside object identities, then use object detectors, classifiers, or visual question answering models to automatically verify whether the correct attribute-object pairings are present in generated images. This approach provides quantitative measurement that goes beyond what aggregate metrics can capture, and the resulting per-dimension scores are precisely the kind of structured signals that reward models discussed in Section 8.3 seek to optimize.

Spatial relationship evaluation examines whether generated images correctly reflect positional and relational descriptions in text prompts, such as "a cat to the left of a dog" or "a cup on top of a book." Evaluating spatial accuracy requires localization capabilities, typically leveraging object detection models to identify bounding boxes of generated entities and then verifying their relative positions against the ground-truth spatial constraints specified in the prompt. Such evaluations have revealed that even high-performing generative models frequently fail to respect spatial constraints, with performance degrading sharply as the number of relational constraints increases.

Object co-occurrence and counting accuracy present additional challenges. Counting evaluation frameworks typically construct prompts specifying precise quantities of objects and then apply detection models to count the number of instances present in generated images, measuring how reliably models produce the correct number. Research has consistently shown that diffusion models struggle with accurate counting, especially for counts beyond two or three, and that standard metrics like FID provide no signal about counting errors whatsoever.

Multi-concept compositionality benchmarks extend these evaluations to scenarios involving multiple simultaneous constraints, combining attribute binding, spatial relationships, and counting within a single prompt. These benchmarks highlight that failures in compositional generation are often compounding: models that fail individual binding tasks fail even more severely when multiple constraints are imposed simultaneously.

A growing consensus in the evaluation community is that reliable assessment of semantic fidelity requires moving beyond single-number aggregate metrics toward structured, decomposed evaluation pipelines that separately quantify performance across semantic dimensions. Vision-language models and visual question answering systems have become important tools in this decomposed evaluation paradigm, enabling automated fine-grained verification of prompt-specific semantic properties in generated images. This shift toward structured semantic evaluation is essential for tracking true progress in compositional text-to-image generation and for identifying remaining failure modes that coarse metrics systematically obscure. Critically, such decomposed evaluation frameworks also provide the foundation for the human preference alignment and reward optimization methods discussed in the following section: by identifying precisely where and how models fall short of semantic correctness, they enable the construction of targeted reward signals that can guide fine-tuning toward genuinely improved compositional and semantic fidelity.

### 8.3 Human Preference Alignment and Reward Optimization

## 8.3 Human Preference Alignment and Reward Optimization

Building on the evaluation frameworks discussed in the previous subsection, a natural next step is to move beyond measurement toward actively optimizing generative models for the qualities that structured evaluation reveals to be important. Aligning text-to-image diffusion models with human aesthetic preferences and quality judgments has thus emerged as a critical research direction, as standard training objectives such as denoising score matching do not directly optimize for perceptual quality or semantic correctness as perceived by human evaluators. The fundamental challenge is that metrics like FID and CLIP score, while correlated with quality, often fail to capture the nuanced preferences that humans apply when judging generated images across dimensions such as aesthetics, prompt faithfulness, photorealism, and compositional coherence—precisely the dimensions that the evaluation methods described in Section 8.2 seek to measure.

**Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).** The most prominent paradigm for human preference alignment involves collecting pairwise or ranked human judgments on generated images and training reward models to predict these preferences. These reward models are then used to fine-tune diffusion model components via reinforcement learning objectives, directly optimizing for human-rated quality rather than reconstruction-based surrogates. This approach mirrors successes in large language model alignment, adapting the RLHF framework to the unique challenges of the continuous, multi-step diffusion generation process. The key difficulty lies in backpropagating reward signals through the stochastic sampling chain, which requires either differentiable reward approximations or policy-gradient-style estimators.

**Reward Model Design and Preference Datasets.** Effective reward models for image generation typically encode multiple quality dimensions simultaneously. Aesthetic quality rewards capture overall visual appeal and artistic merit, while text-image alignment rewards assess how faithfully the generated content matches the input prompt. Safety and fairness rewards have also been explored to penalize harmful or biased outputs. The construction of high-quality preference datasets is equally important; datasets must be large enough to capture diverse human preferences, carefully annotated to avoid annotator bias, and representative of the distribution of prompts and images encountered in deployment. Reward models trained on such datasets can serve as differentiable proxies for human judgment during fine-tuning.

**Fine-Tuning Strategies for Reward Optimization.** Several strategies have been proposed for incorporating reward signals into diffusion model training. Direct reward backpropagation through unrolled sampling chains is theoretically appealing but computationally expensive and prone to instability. Alternative approaches include reward-weighted likelihood objectives that upweight high-reward samples during training, and online reinforcement learning methods that iteratively generate samples, score them with the reward model, and update model parameters accordingly. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA) are commonly employed to reduce the computational overhead of reward-based fine-tuning while preserving the generative capabilities of the pretrained base model.

**Incorporating Reward Signals at Inference Time.** Beyond training-time alignment, reward signals can also guide the generation process at inference time. Reward-guided sampling approaches modify the reverse diffusion trajectory by incorporating gradients from a reward model, analogous to classifier guidance. This allows a fixed pretrained model to be steered toward higher-reward outputs without any parameter updates, providing a flexible and computationally lightweight alternative to full fine-tuning. The trade-off between reward maximization and output diversity must be carefully managed, as aggressive reward optimization can collapse the model toward a narrow set of high-scoring but homogeneous outputs, reducing the creative diversity that makes generative models broadly useful.

**Challenges and Open Problems.** A persistent challenge in human preference alignment is reward hacking, where models learn to exploit idiosyncrasies in the reward model rather than genuinely improving quality as perceived by humans. This necessitates robust reward model evaluation, diverse preference data collection, and regularization strategies that prevent over-optimization. Additionally, human preferences are inherently subjective and culturally variable, making it difficult to train universally applicable reward models. These challenges connect directly to the concerns raised in the following subsection on social bias, as reward models trained on non-representative annotator populations risk encoding and amplifying the same demographic and cultural skews present in the broader training data. Future work must therefore address the scalability of preference data collection, the robustness of reward models to distribution shift, and the development of evaluation protocols that can reliably assess whether reward-optimized models are genuinely better aligned with diverse human populations rather than a narrow annotator demographic.

### 8.4 Social Bias Detection and Mitigation

## 8.4 Social Bias Detection and Mitigation

Building on the human preference alignment frameworks discussed in the previous subsection, it is important to recognize that the preferences encoded in reward models and the outputs optimized through reinforcement learning are not neutral—they reflect the values, perspectives, and demographic compositions of the annotator populations involved. This observation motivates a deeper examination of how social biases are embedded in text-to-image diffusion models, how they can be systematically detected and measured, and what strategies can be employed to mitigate their effects. Text-to-image diffusion models are trained on massive web-scraped datasets that inevitably encode societal stereotypes, historical inequalities, and cultural biases present in human-generated content. These biases manifest in several interrelated ways within generated outputs, raising significant concerns about the fairness and representational equity of deployed systems—concerns that, as we shall see, connect directly to the broader ethical questions addressed in the following subsection.

### Sources of Bias

The primary source of bias in text-to-image diffusion models is the training data itself. Large-scale image-text datasets scraped from the internet reflect existing societal imbalances: certain demographic groups are underrepresented or consistently portrayed in stereotypical roles, while others are depicted with greater diversity and positive framing. When models learn from such data distributions, they absorb and can amplify these patterns. For instance, prompts requesting images of professionals such as "a doctor," "an engineer," or "a CEO" frequently produce outputs skewed toward particular gender and racial presentations, mirroring occupational stereotypes prevalent in the training corpus. Similarly, prompts invoking cultural or geographic references tend to default toward Western-centric aesthetic standards, marginalizing non-Western cultures and visual traditions.

Beyond raw data, the model architecture and training objectives can further entrench biases. Cross-attention mechanisms that align text tokens with spatial image regions may disproportionately associate certain linguistic descriptors with specific demographic attributes. The use of vision-language models such as CLIP as text encoders introduces an additional layer of potential bias, since CLIP's contrastive pretraining on web data encodes associations that reflect the skewed distribution of image-caption pairs on the internet. Language models used as text encoders, such as T5 or BERT, similarly carry biases from their pretraining corpora, which can be propagated into the conditioning signals provided to the diffusion backbone.

### Manifestations of Social Bias

Social biases in text-to-image generation manifest across multiple demographic dimensions:

**Gender bias** is among the most widely documented, with occupational prompts consistently over-representing male-presenting figures in positions of authority and under-representing female-presenting figures. Conversely, prompts referencing domestic or caregiving roles tend to over-generate female-presenting figures.

**Racial and ethnic bias** appears as a tendency to default toward lighter-skinned or Western-European-presenting individuals when race or ethnicity is unspecified in the prompt, as well as the stereotypical association of certain racial groups with specific roles, activities, or aesthetic contexts.

**Cultural bias** manifests as the privileging of Western cultural norms, aesthetics, and settings, such that prompts for generic concepts like "a wedding," "a festival," or "a city street" produce outputs that disproportionately reflect Euro-American cultural contexts rather than the global diversity of human experience.

**Occupational and socioeconomic stereotypes** are reinforced when models consistently associate high-prestige professions with particular demographic combinations while associating low-prestige or manual labor roles with others.

**Intersectional bias** arises when multiple protected attributes interact: the stereotyping can be compounded when gender, race, age, and other dimensions jointly influence the generated output in ways that reflect complex societal prejudices.

### Detection and Measurement Methods

Detecting and quantifying bias in text-to-image models requires carefully designed evaluation protocols that complement the broader evaluation frameworks discussed in Section 8.2. A common approach involves generating large batches of images from carefully controlled prompt sets that vary only in the professional role or social context while leaving demographic descriptors unspecified. The resulting demographic distribution of generated figures can then be assessed through automated classifiers or human annotators to measure representation disparities.

Automated measurement often relies on pretrained face attribute classifiers or body attribute detectors to estimate the perceived gender, race, and age of generated persons. These tools, while enabling scalable evaluation, introduce their own biases and should be interpreted with caution, since they impose binary or limited categorical schemes on complex, continuous human characteristics. Alignment metrics such as CLIP scores can be decomposed to assess whether certain demographic groups receive systematically lower semantic alignment scores for equivalent prompts, indicating disparate quality of generation.

Benchmark datasets specifically designed for bias evaluation have been proposed, featuring prompt sets covering a wide range of occupations, social roles, and cultural contexts. These benchmarks enable standardized comparisons across different model versions and training regimes, facilitating longitudinal tracking of bias as models scale.

Human evaluation studies complement automated metrics by recruiting diverse rater panels to assess representational fairness, stereotyping, and cultural appropriateness of generated images. Such studies are essential for capturing the subjective and contextual nature of bias that automated classifiers may miss, and they underscore the importance of representative annotator demographics—a consideration that connects directly back to the reward model design challenges raised in Section 8.3.

### Mitigation Approaches

Several complementary strategies have been proposed to reduce bias in text-to-image diffusion models:

**Training data curation and rebalancing** involves filtering or re-weighting training datasets to improve demographic representation and reduce the prevalence of stereotypical associations. This approach requires careful auditing of large-scale datasets, which is itself a resource-intensive endeavor.

**Prompt augmentation and counterfactual data augmentation** involve systematically expanding prompts during training to include explicit demographic diversity, encouraging models to generate a broader range of representations in response to underspecified prompts.

**Fine-tuning with fairness objectives** adapts pretrained models using additional training signals that explicitly penalize stereotypical associations or reward equitable demographic representation. The reinforcement learning from human feedback framework discussed in Section 8.3 can be extended to incorporate fairness-aware reward signals that penalize biased outputs, providing a natural bridge between preference alignment and bias mitigation.

**Inference-time intervention** encompasses techniques that modify the generation process without retraining, such as augmenting prompts with demographic diversity cues at inference time or using classifier guidance with fairness-aware classifiers to steer the denoising trajectory toward more equitable outputs.

**Concept erasure and suppression** methods, which selectively remove specific associations from model weights, can in principle be applied to reduce stereotypical concept-demographic linkages. However, these approaches must be carefully validated to ensure that mitigation of one bias dimension does not introduce compensatory biases elsewhere.

### Challenges and Open Questions

Despite progress in bias detection and mitigation, achieving equitable generation across diverse demographic groups remains a fundamentally difficult challenge. Several tensions complicate the problem:

First, there is no universally agreed-upon definition of fairness in image generation; different stakeholders may prioritize demographic parity, individual fairness, cultural authenticity, or other criteria that can conflict with one another.

Second, the very tools used to measure bias, such as demographic classifiers, encode their own assumptions and biases, creating a recursive measurement problem. Relying on binary gender classifiers, for example, erases non-binary identities and can produce misleading fairness assessments.

Third, bias mitigation interventions can degrade overall generation quality or introduce new failure modes, requiring careful calibration of the trade-off between fairness and fidelity.

Fourth, the global diversity of cultural contexts means that what constitutes fair or stereotypical representation is itself culturally contingent and contested, making it difficult to define universal mitigation targets.

Finally, as models scale and are deployed in increasingly diverse real-world contexts, continuous monitoring and updating of bias mitigation measures is necessary, requiring institutional infrastructure that goes beyond one-time technical fixes. These challenges—spanning technical, social, and institutional dimensions—set the stage for the broader ethical concerns examined in the following subsection, where issues of harmful content generation, intellectual property, and disinformation further underscore the need for sustained interdisciplinary engagement with the societal consequences of text-to-image diffusion models. Addressing social bias thus demands collaboration between machine learning researchers, social scientists, ethicists, and affected communities to develop evaluation frameworks and mitigation strategies that are both technically rigorous and socially responsive.

### 8.5 Ethical Concerns and Responsible Deployment

## Ethical Concerns in Text-to-Image Diffusion Models

The rapid advancement and widespread deployment of text-to-image diffusion models have introduced a range of profound ethical concerns that demand careful consideration from researchers, developers, and policymakers alike. These concerns span multiple dimensions, including the generation of harmful or non-consensual content, intellectual property violations, the facilitation of disinformation, and data privacy issues embedded within large-scale training pipelines. While the preceding discussion of social bias detection and mitigation (Section 8.4) addresses one critical facet of responsible AI development, the ethical landscape surrounding text-to-image generation extends considerably further, encompassing questions of legal accountability, personal safety, and the integrity of public information.

### Generation of Harmful and Non-Consensual Content

One of the most pressing ethical challenges surrounding text-to-image diffusion models is their potential to generate harmful, offensive, or non-consensual imagery. Modern systems trained on large, uncurated web-scraped datasets inevitably absorb explicit, violent, and otherwise objectionable content, which may then be reproduced or recombined in novel ways during generation. The risk of generating non-consensual intimate imagery—commonly referred to as deepfake pornography—poses serious threats to individual dignity and safety. Similarly, models may be prompted to generate imagery that sexualizes minors, depicts graphic violence, or produces targeted harassment material. Mitigating these risks requires multifaceted approaches, including training data curation, robust content filtering at inference time, and the implementation of strict usage policies enforced by model providers.

### Intellectual Property and Copyright Concerns

Text-to-image diffusion models are trained on massive corpora of images scraped from the internet, much of which is protected by copyright. This raises serious legal and ethical questions about whether the training process constitutes infringement, whether generated outputs can themselves be copyrighted, and what obligations developers and users bear toward original creators whose works contributed to model training. Artists and illustrators have raised significant objections to their distinctive styles being replicated by diffusion models without attribution, compensation, or consent. The emerging landscape of concept erasure and style suppression—discussed in the context of concept ablation methods in Section 6.9—represents one technical response to these concerns, enabling the targeted removal of specific artistic styles or copyrighted visual elements from model capabilities. However, legal frameworks governing generative AI and copyright remain unsettled across jurisdictions, underscoring the need for ongoing dialogue between technical communities, legal scholars, and creative industries.

### Deepfakes and Disinformation

The photorealistic image generation capabilities of modern diffusion models present significant risks for the creation and dissemination of disinformation. Fabricated images of public figures, staged political events, or falsified documentary evidence can be produced at scale with minimal technical expertise. Such synthetic media threatens to erode public trust in visual information and can be weaponized for political manipulation, reputational damage, and electoral interference. The challenge of detecting AI-generated imagery has spurred research into passive forensic detection methods as well as proactive provenance mechanisms. Watermarking techniques that embed imperceptible signals into generated images—such as the protective perturbation approaches discussed in relation to content suppression strategies like EditShield in Section 6.9—offer one avenue for maintaining traceability of synthetic content. Provenance tracking frameworks, such as the Content Authenticity Initiative (CAI) and C2PA standards, aim to attach cryptographically signed metadata to images, enabling downstream verification of their origin and modification history.

### Data Privacy and Training Data Concerns

Large-scale diffusion models trained on web-crawled datasets may inadvertently memorize and reproduce sensitive personal information, including identifiable photographs of private individuals who never consented to their images being used for model training. Studies have demonstrated that diffusion models can reproduce near-verbatim copies of training images under certain prompting conditions, raising significant privacy concerns. This phenomenon is particularly acute for individuals whose images appear frequently in training data, such as public figures, but also extends to private individuals whose photographs appear on social media or other publicly accessible platforms. Differential privacy, data minimization strategies, and mechanisms for individuals to request the removal of their data from training pipelines represent important technical and policy responses to these challenges.

### Frameworks for Responsible Deployment

Responsible deployment of text-to-image diffusion models requires a layered approach combining technical safeguards, organizational policies, and regulatory oversight. Content moderation systems—whether implemented as pre-generation prompt filtering, post-generation image classifiers, or human review pipelines—represent a first line of defense against misuse. Major model providers have adopted usage policies that explicitly prohibit the generation of certain categories of harmful content, though the enforcement of such policies at scale remains an ongoing challenge. Watermarking and digital provenance mechanisms are increasingly recognized as essential infrastructure for maintaining accountability in AI-generated media ecosystems. Additionally, red-teaming exercises and adversarial prompt testing, as discussed in Section 8.7, play a critical role in identifying and patching vulnerabilities in deployed systems before they can be exploited at scale.

### Societal Representation and Cultural Harm

Beyond direct harms, text-to-image diffusion models also raise concerns about their role in perpetuating and amplifying societal biases and cultural stereotypes, as discussed in depth in Section 8.4. The power to generate compelling visual representations of people, places, and events at scale confers significant influence over collective visual culture. Ensuring that this influence is exercised responsibly—with attention to diversity, inclusion, and cultural sensitivity—requires ongoing engagement with affected communities, investment in evaluation frameworks that capture representational harms, and commitment to iterative model improvement in response to identified deficiencies. These representational concerns connect naturally to the broader interpretability challenges discussed in the following section, where understanding how models internally encode concepts—including demographic attributes and cultural associations—is essential for diagnosing and correcting harmful generative behaviors.

In summary, the ethical dimensions of text-to-image diffusion models are broad and deeply consequential. Addressing them demands sustained interdisciplinary collaboration spanning machine learning research, law, policy, ethics, and the creative industries, with a commitment to placing human welfare and dignity at the center of technological development. The technical solutions discussed throughout this survey—from bias mitigation and concept erasure to watermarking and interpretability—collectively constitute the building blocks of a more responsible generative AI ecosystem, though their effective deployment ultimately depends as much on institutional commitment and regulatory frameworks as on algorithmic innovation.

### 8.6 Interpretability and Internal Representation Analysis

Understanding the internal representations and decision-making processes of text-to-image diffusion models is essential for improving transparency, trustworthiness, and controllability. As the preceding discussion of ethical concerns highlights, many of the most pressing challenges in responsible deployment—from diagnosing biased outputs to enabling targeted concept erasure—ultimately depend on understanding what models have learned and how that knowledge is organized internally. Interpretability research in this domain spans a broad range of methodologies, from analyzing cross-attention maps to probing latent spaces for semantic content, and has yielded important insights into how textual concepts are encoded, propagated, and decoded throughout the generative process. These insights, in turn, directly inform the robustness evaluation and adversarial testing strategies discussed in the following section, where understanding internal representations is prerequisite to understanding how those representations behave under stress.

**Cross-Attention Map Analysis**

One of the most accessible windows into the internal workings of text-to-image diffusion models is the cross-attention mechanism, which mediates the interaction between textual conditioning signals and spatial image features. Cross-attention maps reveal which spatial regions of the image are most strongly associated with specific tokens in the input text prompt. Studies have demonstrated that these maps carry strong semantic meaning, reflecting object layouts, attribute associations, and compositional structure. For instance, work on training-free structured diffusion guidance [79] explicitly investigates the properties of cross-attention layers, finding that keys and values in these layers encode semantically meaningful information about object layouts and content. By analyzing and manipulating these representations, researchers have been able to improve attribute binding and compositional accuracy without any additional training, effectively validating that cross-attention maps are functionally interpretable structural representations rather than opaque computational artifacts.

The interpretability of cross-attention maps has further motivated a range of downstream applications. Visualization of attention weights across denoising timesteps reveals that early stages of generation rely heavily on text conditioning to establish global structure and content, while later stages increasingly focus on fine-grained texture and detail refinement. This temporal behavior has been systematically studied and exploited architecturally in models such as eDiff-I [44], which observes that text conditioning is most active in early denoising stages and becomes less influential as generation progresses. This insight not only informs the design of ensemble architectures but also deepens the interpretive understanding of how semantic concepts are progressively instantiated in image space throughout the diffusion trajectory.

**Latent Space Semantic Encoding**

Beyond attention mechanisms, the latent spaces of diffusion models encode rich semantic information about concepts, styles, and scene properties. Probing studies have examined whether specific directions or subspaces in the latent representation correspond to interpretable semantic attributes such as object identity, spatial position, color, texture, and style. Such analyses draw inspiration from earlier probing work in natural language processing [47], which demonstrated that different layers of transformer models encode distinct types of linguistic information. Analogously, in text-to-image diffusion models, different layers and timesteps of the denoising network have been found to capture different levels of semantic abstraction, with shallower layers encoding coarse global structure and deeper layers encoding finer-grained appearance details.

The structure of the latent space is also influenced by the choice of text encoder. Models that use CLIP-based text encoders, which are trained with contrastive image-language objectives, encode visual concepts in ways that are qualitatively different from those produced by language-model-based encoders such as T5 [10]. CLIP text representations have been shown to carry a distinctive cross-modal associative property, sometimes described as "synesthesia," that enables stronger visual-semantic grounding [48]. In contrast, T5-based encoders provide richer linguistic structure and better handling of complex compositional descriptions, as exploited in Imagen [10]. Comparing the internal representations induced by these different encoders illuminates the trade-offs between visual grounding and linguistic expressivity, and provides actionable guidance for architecture design.

**Concept Representation and Disentanglement**

A closely related interpretability challenge concerns the degree to which individual concepts are represented in a disentangled and localized fashion within diffusion model representations. Ideal interpretability would imply that each distinct concept mentioned in a text prompt activates a separable and well-localized region of the model's representation space, enabling precise attribution and intervention. However, empirical studies reveal that concept representations in diffusion models often exhibit entanglement, whereby attributes, objects, and relationships interact in complex and nonlinear ways. This entanglement is a primary cause of failure modes such as attribute leakage, where adjectives intended to modify one object inappropriately influence the rendering of another. Crucially, this same entanglement underlies many of the adversarial vulnerabilities examined in the following section, where distributed concept representations create unpredictable failure modes under adversarial prompting and out-of-distribution inputs.

Research on CLIP's compositional understanding [101] has directly probed whether vision-language models can correctly bind compositional concepts such as "red cube behind blue sphere," finding that while CLIP can compose concepts in single-object settings, performance degrades substantially when variable binding and structural relationships are required. These findings carry direct implications for text-to-image diffusion models conditioned on CLIP embeddings, suggesting that the conditioning signal itself may lack the representational structure necessary for faithful compositional rendering. Such interpretability analyses thus not only diagnose model limitations but also point toward the need for richer, more structured conditioning representations.

**Probing Studies and Visualization Techniques**

Probing classifiers applied to intermediate activations of diffusion denoising networks have been used to assess what types of information are encoded at different network depths and timesteps. These studies follow the tradition of probing analyses in NLP [47], where lightweight classifiers are trained on frozen representations to predict specific properties, thereby testing whether those properties are linearly decodable from the representations. In diffusion models, probing has been applied to assess the presence of semantic categories, spatial relationships, and object attributes in U-Net activations, revealing that higher-level semantic information tends to be concentrated in the bottleneck and decoder layers of the U-Net architecture.

Visualization techniques including feature map saliency, principal component analysis of latent activations, and t-SNE embeddings of prompt representations have complemented probing studies by providing intuitive visual summaries of the structure within learned representations. These tools have been particularly valuable for understanding how different prompts cluster in the conditioning space and how changes in prompt phrasing affect the distribution of generated images. For instance, analyzing the embeddings of semantically related prompts using models like ELLA [49] reveals how LLM-derived conditioning representations organize complex multi-attribute descriptions in ways that enable finer-grained semantic control compared to CLIP-based counterparts.

**Temporal Dynamics of Representation**

An important dimension of interpretability specific to diffusion models, as opposed to single-pass generative models, is the temporal evolution of representations across denoising timesteps. Because diffusion models iteratively refine their outputs over many steps, the internal representations at each timestep can be examined as a sequence, revealing how the model's "understanding" of the target image evolves from coarse semantic layout to fine perceptual detail. Research such as eDiff-I [44] has empirically characterized this trajectory, showing that the synthesis behavior qualitatively changes: early timesteps strongly depend on the text prompt for content placement, while later timesteps effectively ignore text conditioning and focus on refinement. This temporal specialization has practical consequences for interpretability, suggesting that explanations of model behavior must account for which stage of generation is being analyzed. It also has direct implications for robustness, since adversarial perturbations introduced at different stages of the denoising trajectory may have qualitatively different effects on the final output—a connection explored more deeply in the following section.

**Implications for Trustworthiness and Model Improvement**

The insights gained from interpretability analyses have direct practical implications that extend across the ethical, robustness, and safety dimensions discussed throughout this survey. By understanding which components of the model are responsible for specific failure modes—such as incorrect attribute binding, missing objects, or spatial misalignment—researchers and practitioners can design more targeted interventions. Interpretability also supports auditing for biases encoded in model representations, by examining whether protected attributes such as gender or race are systematically encoded in ways that produce stereotyped outputs, directly complementing the bias detection and mitigation strategies discussed in the context of ethical concerns. Furthermore, transparent representations facilitate user trust, enable debugging of unexpected behaviors, and support regulatory compliance in high-stakes deployment contexts.

Overall, interpretability research for text-to-image diffusion models remains an active and growing area, with cross-attention analysis, latent space probing, concept disentanglement studies, and visualization techniques collectively building a richer mechanistic understanding of how these powerful generative systems translate textual descriptions into visual content. This mechanistic understanding provides the foundation upon which the robustness and adversarial evaluation methodologies of the following section are built: knowing how models encode and process information internally is a prerequisite for understanding how and why they fail under adversarial or out-of-distribution conditions. Continued investment in interpretability methods is therefore essential for ensuring that advances in generative capability are matched by corresponding advances in model transparency, accountability, and resilience.

### 8.7 Robustness Verification and Adversarial Evaluation

Robustness verification and adversarial evaluation represent critical dimensions in assessing the reliability and safety of text-to-image diffusion models prior to real-world deployment. Just as the previous discussion of interpretability methods illuminates how these models encode and propagate semantic information internally, robustness research examines how that internal machinery responds under pressure—whether from deliberately crafted adversarial inputs, out-of-distribution prompts, or attempts to circumvent safety constraints. As these models become increasingly integrated into consumer-facing products and creative pipelines, understanding their behavior under adversarial conditions has become a pressing research priority that directly informs the concept ablation and content suppression strategies discussed in the following section.

**Adversarial Prompts and Prompt Injection Attacks**

Text-to-image diffusion models are inherently vulnerable to adversarial prompts—carefully crafted textual inputs designed to elicit unsafe, biased, or unintended outputs. Prompt injection attacks exploit the model's text-conditioning mechanism to override content filtering safeguards or redirect the generative process toward harmful content. Unlike adversarial examples in discriminative models, which typically require pixel-level perturbations, adversarial prompts in generative settings can be constructed entirely in natural language, making them more accessible to malicious actors. Jailbreaking attempts—where users craft prompts that bypass built-in safety constraints—further highlight the fragility of surface-level filtering mechanisms that rely solely on keyword matching or CLIP-based similarity thresholds.

The conditioning and guidance mechanisms that underpin these models introduce specific attack surfaces. Classifier-free guidance [57], for instance, combines conditional and unconditional score estimates, and adversarial manipulation of the conditioning signal can systematically steer generation in unintended directions. Similarly, classifier guidance [51] uses gradients from an external classifier, which can themselves be exploited through adversarial gradient perturbations. Research on training-free guidance has further exposed vulnerabilities: theoretical analysis demonstrates that training-free methods are more susceptible to adversarial gradients and exhibit slower convergence compared to classifier-based guidance approaches [56], suggesting that the flexibility offered by plug-and-play guidance comes at a measurable robustness cost.

**Out-of-Distribution Inputs and Generalization Failures**

Beyond deliberate adversarial attacks, text-to-image models frequently encounter out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts—descriptions that fall outside the semantic coverage of their training data—leading to degraded output quality, semantic misalignment, or the generation of artifacts. The challenge is compounded by the long-tail distribution of natural language: rare concept combinations, unusual spatial configurations, and domain-specific terminology can all trigger generalization failures. These failure modes are closely related to the concept entanglement phenomena identified in interpretability research, where the distributed representation of concepts across shared attention mechanisms makes it difficult to isolate and handle individual concepts reliably. Guidance techniques that rely on pretrained classifiers or feature extractors are particularly susceptible, as these auxiliary networks may themselves fail on noisy or OOD inputs. The practice of fine-tuning guidance models on noise-corrupted labeled data attempts to address this limitation, but such approaches face scalability challenges when the space of possible conditions is large and diverse [53].

**Evaluation Frameworks for Stress-Testing**

Systematic evaluation of robustness requires dedicated benchmarking frameworks that go beyond standard metrics such as FID or CLIP score. Stress-testing protocols should include adversarial prompt suites targeting known failure modes—attribute binding errors, object hallucination, spatial relationship violations, and unsafe content generation—alongside out-of-distribution prompts that probe the boundaries of the model's generalization. Universal guidance frameworks [55] that condition generation on arbitrary modalities such as face recognition, object detection, and segmentation provide a natural test bed for evaluating cross-modal robustness, as errors in any upstream guidance signal propagate into the generated output.

Human evaluation remains indispensable for assessing robustness dimensions that automated metrics fail to capture, including subtle semantic inconsistencies, culturally inappropriate outputs, and the fidelity of generated content under ambiguous or underspecified prompts. Combining automated adversarial probing with structured human evaluation protocols offers a more comprehensive picture of model reliability under challenging real-world conditions.

**Improving Robustness Through Training and Inference Interventions**

Several strategies have been proposed to improve the robustness of diffusion models at both training and inference time. At the training level, incorporating diverse and adversarially augmented conditioning signals can reduce sensitivity to prompt variations. The introduction of perceptual loss objectives [102] has been shown to improve sample quality and reduce artifacts, indirectly contributing to more stable generation under varied input conditions. Addressing the discrepancy between training objectives and sampling behavior through refined loss formulations [61] can further reduce failure modes at inference time, particularly at high guidance scales where out-of-distribution samples and mode collapse are most pronounced.

At inference time, guidance refinement strategies such as Perturbed-Attention Guidance [67] improve structural coherence by steering generation away from degraded intermediate samples, offering a complementary mechanism to standard classifier-free guidance that is applicable even in unconditional settings. Semantic-aware guidance adjustments [60] that adapt guidance scales across different semantic regions further reduce spatial inconsistencies that can manifest under adversarial or unusual prompting conditions. Consensus guidance mechanisms designed to filter out noisy and adversarial gradients—such as those employed in latent diffusion counterfactual generation pipelines [103]—offer another avenue for improving robustness against gradient-based attacks by aligning guidance signals with the model's implicit classifier.

Condition-annealed sampling approaches [68] that introduce scheduled noise into the conditioning vector during inference improve output diversity and reduce sensitivity to the specific conditioning signal at high guidance scales, which may also contribute to more graceful degradation under adversarial prompting. End-to-end optimization methods that operate on true generated pixels rather than one-step approximations [71] provide more accurate gradient signals and reduce the risk of misaligned guidance that adversarial inputs might exploit.

**Implications for Safe Deployment**

The findings across robustness research have significant implications for the safe deployment of text-to-image diffusion models in real-world applications. First, no single intervention—whether prompt filtering, guidance modification, or adversarial training—provides comprehensive protection against the full spectrum of adversarial threats. Layered defense strategies combining input-level filtering, inference-time robustness interventions, and output-level safety classifiers are therefore recommended for production systems. Second, the reliance on external guidance networks introduces supply-chain vulnerabilities, as the robustness of the overall system is bounded by the weakest component in the guidance pipeline [53]. Third, the gap between automated evaluation metrics and human judgments of safety and appropriateness underscores the continued need for human oversight in high-stakes deployment contexts.

These robustness limitations also motivate the concept ablation and machine unlearning approaches discussed in the following section. When adversarial prompts and jailbreaking attacks can circumvent inference-time safety filters, modifying model weights directly to excise harmful concepts from the model's generative repertoire becomes an attractive complementary strategy. Looking forward, establishing standardized adversarial evaluation benchmarks tailored to text-to-image generation—analogous to established robustness benchmarks in the discriminative domain—represents an important open challenge. Such benchmarks should cover adversarial prompt robustness, OOD generalization, fairness under demographic perturbations [104], and resistance to prompt injection, providing a unified framework for comparing the robustness of different model families and guidance strategies across diverse deployment scenarios.

### 8.8 Concept Ablation and Content Suppression Evaluation

As text-to-image diffusion models become increasingly capable of generating photorealistic content, the ability to selectively remove or suppress specific concepts, artistic styles, or sensitive material from pretrained models has emerged as a critical research challenge. Building directly on the robustness limitations identified in the previous section—where adversarial prompts and jailbreaking attacks were shown to circumvent inference-time safety filters—concept ablation and machine unlearning methods offer a complementary and more fundamental defense: surgically excising targeted knowledge from model weights themselves, rather than relying on surface-level filtering that can be bypassed. Unlike full retraining, which is computationally prohibitive, these approaches aim to remove specific capabilities while preserving the overall generative quality of the model.

**Concept Ablation and Machine Unlearning**

Concept ablation refers to the targeted removal of a specific visual concept, artistic style, or object category from a pretrained diffusion model's generative repertoire without degrading its ability to generate unrelated content. This is fundamentally different from simple prompt filtering or content moderation at inference time, as it modifies the model weights themselves to prevent the generation of certain content even when explicitly prompted. Machine unlearning approaches, originally studied in the context of classification models for data privacy compliance, have been adapted to generative settings where the goal is to make the model behave as if certain training data or concepts were never seen.

A central challenge in this domain is the entanglement of concepts within the learned parameter space of large diffusion models. Because concepts are distributed across millions of parameters through shared attention mechanisms and cross-attention conditioning, removing one concept without affecting semantically related or co-occurring concepts is non-trivial. This entanglement is directly connected to the concept entanglement phenomena discussed in the interpretability and robustness sections, where distributed representations across shared attention mechanisms were identified as a source of both generalization failures and vulnerability to adversarial inputs. Methods must therefore balance the competing objectives of completeness—ensuring the target concept is fully suppressed—and specificity—ensuring that unrelated generation capabilities remain intact.

**Evaluation Protocols for Concept Removal**

Evaluating the success of concept ablation requires dedicated protocols that go beyond standard generation quality metrics such as Fréchet Inception Distance or CLIP scores. A comprehensive evaluation framework for concept removal typically involves three axes. First, suppression completeness assesses whether the model can still generate the target concept when explicitly prompted, often measured using classifier-based detection rates or human evaluation of generated samples under targeted prompts. Second, semantic specificity measures whether ablation has inadvertently degraded the generation of related but distinct concepts, evaluated through generation quality on a held-out set of non-target prompts. Third, overall generation fidelity verifies that the model's general image quality and text-image alignment are preserved across diverse prompts unrelated to the removed concept.

Robustness of concept removal is another critical evaluation dimension, one that directly connects back to the adversarial robustness concerns raised in the previous section. A model may fail to generate a concept under direct prompting but remain vulnerable to paraphrased, indirect, or adversarially constructed prompts that invoke the concept through semantic proximity—precisely the kind of prompt injection and jailbreaking attack that robustness research has documented. Evaluation protocols must therefore stress-test removal methods against a diverse set of prompt variations, including synonyms, stylistic descriptions, and multi-hop references to the target concept, to verify that suppression is not merely surface-level.

**Content Suppression for Artistic Styles and Sensitive Material**

A particularly important application of concept ablation is the removal of specific artistic styles, especially those associated with living or recently deceased artists whose work may have been included in training data without consent. Style-targeted ablation methods must contend with the highly distributed and implicit nature of stylistic information, which manifests through color palettes, brushstroke patterns, compositional tendencies, and textural signatures rather than discrete semantic tokens. Evaluation in this setting often involves both automated perceptual similarity metrics and expert human evaluation by art professionals who can assess whether the characteristic visual fingerprint of a targeted style has been effectively removed.

For sensitive content suppression—including explicit material, violent imagery, and depictions of real individuals in compromising contexts—evaluation must additionally account for the adversarial landscape of potential misuse. Methods such as protective perturbations and content suppression strategies aim to prevent unauthorized generation or editing of protected imagery. Evaluation of these methods requires testing not only direct generation but also image-guided generation, inpainting, and style transfer pipelines that might circumvent concept-level suppression by leveraging visual conditioning rather than textual prompting.

**Balancing Removal and Utility**

A fundamental tension in concept ablation is the trade-off between the thoroughness of removal and the preservation of model utility. Overly aggressive ablation, particularly when target concepts are semantically close to frequently generated content, can produce models with substantially degraded generation capabilities. Conversely, insufficiently targeted ablation leaves residual traces of the suppressed concept that can be elicited through creative prompting—a vulnerability that is especially acute given the adversarial prompt techniques surveyed in the preceding section. Current evaluation methodologies increasingly adopt a Pareto-style analysis, plotting suppression completeness against generation fidelity degradation across a range of intervention strengths, enabling practitioners and auditors to understand the practical operating points of different ablation methods.

The field is moving toward standardized evaluation suites that include curated sets of target concepts spanning artistic styles, celebrity identities, object categories, and sensitive content classes, paired with comprehensive prompt libraries designed to probe both direct and indirect generation of each target. Such standardized benchmarks are essential for enabling reproducible comparisons across the growing landscape of concept ablation and machine unlearning methods, and for providing regulators and deployers with credible assurances about the content safety of deployed diffusion systems. Importantly, these benchmarks must also address the open challenges outlined in the following section—including long-tail concept coverage, compositional evaluation, and cultural inclusivity—to ensure that concept removal does not disproportionately degrade performance on underrepresented or culturally specific content that the broader field is simultaneously striving to improve.

### 8.9 Open Challenges and Future Research Directions

Despite the remarkable progress in text-to-image diffusion models, numerous open challenges remain that will shape the trajectory of future research across technical, ethical, and societal dimensions. The preceding discussion of concept ablation and machine unlearning has already highlighted one such frontier—the surgical removal of unwanted content from pretrained models—but this represents only one facet of a broader landscape of unsolved problems. What follows outlines the most pressing directions that the field must address to realize the full transformative potential of generative diffusion systems.

**Improving Long-Tail Concept Generation.** Current large-scale diffusion models excel at generating common, frequently seen concepts but struggle with rare, long-tail, or highly specialized visual content. Training data distributions are inherently imbalanced, and models tend to memorize and reproduce dominant patterns while failing to generalize to underrepresented concepts. Future research must address this by developing better data curation strategies, few-shot generalization techniques, and compositional reasoning capabilities that allow models to synthesize novel combinations of concepts rather than relying on memorized templates.

**Achieving Consistent Multi-Turn Interactive Generation.** Practical deployment of text-to-image systems increasingly demands iterative, conversational workflows where users refine and extend generated content over multiple turns. Existing models largely treat each generation as an independent task, lacking persistent memory of prior context, user preferences, or established scene elements. Methods that support coherent multi-turn editing—maintaining identity, style, and scene consistency across interactions—remain an important open frontier. Progress in this area will benefit from advances in instruction-based editing [81] and iterative refinement paradigms.

**Scaling to Higher Resolutions.** While significant progress has been made in generating high-fidelity images at moderate resolutions, synthesizing very high-resolution images (e.g., 4K or beyond) remains computationally prohibitive for most architectures. Latent diffusion approaches [35] and cascaded frameworks have partially addressed this challenge, but further innovations in efficient attention mechanisms [105], hierarchical generation, and resolution-adaptive architectures are needed. Transformer-based backbones with scalable attention [90; 106] offer promising directions for resolution-agnostic generation without retraining.

**Reducing Computational Costs.** The training and inference costs of state-of-the-art text-to-image diffusion models remain substantial barriers to democratization and environmental sustainability. Training systems like Stable Diffusion require hundreds of thousands of GPU hours, while inference demands multiple sequential denoising steps. Advances in knowledge distillation, quantization [107], architectural pruning, caching strategies [108; 109], and efficient compact latent representations [36] have begun to address inference efficiency, but training efficiency remains a largely open problem. Resource-efficient training strategies such as time-decoupled training [83] represent early steps toward more accessible model development. Mobile and edge deployment [110] also requires sustained research investment to bring generative capabilities to resource-constrained environments.

**Advancing Culturally Inclusive and Globally Representative Generation.** Text-to-image models trained predominantly on English-language, Western-centric datasets tend to reflect the cultural biases and aesthetic norms of dominant groups, marginalizing non-Western visual traditions, languages, and cultural contexts. Future work must prioritize the collection and curation of diverse, multilingual, and multicultural training datasets, alongside evaluation frameworks that assess cultural inclusivity and representational diversity beyond narrow demographic axes. Multilingual visual text generation and cross-cultural scene synthesis represent important concrete research directions in this space.

**Developing More Reliable Evaluation Frameworks.** Standard metrics such as FID and CLIP score provide coarse assessments of generation quality and text-image alignment but fail to capture fine-grained semantic correctness, compositional fidelity, attribute binding accuracy, and cultural appropriateness. Existing benchmarks like DrawBench and PartiPrompts are valuable but insufficient for the full spectrum of real-world generation requirements [10]. This limitation is particularly evident in the context of concept ablation evaluation, where specialized protocols must assess suppression completeness, semantic specificity, and robustness against adversarial prompting—dimensions that generic benchmarks do not address. Future evaluation frameworks must be compositional, multi-dimensional, and capable of assessing nuanced properties such as spatial relationships, object co-occurrence, counting accuracy, and stylistic consistency, potentially leveraging vision-language models and human preference studies to complement automated metrics.

**Addressing the Co-Evolution of Generative Capabilities and Safety.** As text-to-image diffusion models grow more capable, the risks of misuse—including deepfake generation, non-consensual imagery, disinformation, and intellectual property violations—escalate correspondingly. Achieving the right balance between generative expressiveness and safety requires robust content filtering, watermarking, concept erasure [111], and model-level suppression mechanisms that are resistant to adversarial circumvention. The challenge of concept ablation and machine unlearning discussed previously illustrates precisely this tension: overly aggressive removal degrades model utility, while insufficiently targeted removal leaves residual vulnerabilities. Moreover, reinforcement learning from human feedback [73] and reward-based alignment techniques must be extended to incorporate not only aesthetic quality but also fairness, safety, and ethical constraints. The co-evolution of evaluation, safety mechanisms, and generative capability is perhaps the most complex long-term challenge facing the field, requiring interdisciplinary collaboration across machine learning, ethics, law, and social science to ensure that these powerful tools are developed and deployed responsibly.

**Improving Compositional and Semantic Understanding.** Despite notable advances in cross-attention manipulation [79; 112; 113] and layout-guided generation, accurately rendering complex compositional scenes with multiple interacting objects, correct attribute bindings, and precise spatial relationships remains a persistent challenge. Future work should integrate richer structural reasoning—drawing on scene graphs, structured language representations, and planning-based approaches—to bridge the gap between what users describe and what models reliably produce.

In summary, the future of text-to-image diffusion research lies not only in scaling model capacity and data, but in developing architectures and training paradigms that are efficient, controllable, compositionally faithful, culturally inclusive, and aligned with human values and safety requirements. The challenges outlined above—spanning generation quality, computational accessibility, cultural representation, evaluation rigor, safety, and compositional understanding—are deeply interconnected, and progress on any one front will often depend on simultaneous advances across the others. Addressing these challenges collectively will determine whether these models can fulfill their transformative potential across creative, scientific, and societal applications.
