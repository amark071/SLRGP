# Vision-Language Pretraining

# Vision-Language Pretraining: A Survey

## 1. Pretraining Methods and Objectives

Early vision-language pretraining (VLP) established contrastive learning between image and text representations as a dominant paradigm, yet several limitations of this approach have motivated subsequent refinements. A central challenge concerns the noisy, many-to-many correspondences inherent in web-harvested image-caption datasets. As [arxiv_2204.04588] argue, the standard contrastive objective employed by models such as CLIP does not adequately account for such noise, contributing to data and compute inefficiency; their proposed remedy introduces progressive self-distillation with soft image-caption similarity targets to produce more robust cross-modal representations. A related asymmetry exists in how augmentation is applied during training: [arxiv_2305.20088] observes that standard CLIP training applies data augmentations exclusively to images while leaving textual inputs unmodified, thereby limiting the diversity of supervision signals; systematic text rewrites on the language side yield meaningful improvements in downstream transfer performance.

A related but distinct problem arises in weakly supervised settings, where cross-modal supervision is deliberately limited to reduce data collection costs. [arxiv_2305.15483] observe that existing weakly supervised VLP methods rely solely on local image descriptors such as object tags as cross-modal anchors, constraining the richness of learned alignments; they instead propose relative representations to capture broader structural correspondences between modalities while preserving the efficiency advantages of weak supervision. Beyond noise and supervision quality, the difficulty of aligning multiple caption concepts to multiple image regions simultaneously has prompted curriculum-based approaches. Drawing inspiration from developmental learning in children, [arxiv_2305.17540] introduce a curriculum strategy for image-caption pretraining that progressively increases conceptual complexity, improving alignment of noun-level semantics to visual objects and yielding gains on downstream tasks such as zero-shot image classification and object detection.

While contrastive methods have dominated large-scale VLP, generative pretraining via image captioning has often been regarded as an inferior alternative. [arxiv_2306.07915] challenges this assumption through a careful empirical comparison, finding that image captioners can serve as scalable vision learners competitive with contrastive counterparts, thereby reopening the question of which pretraining objective is preferable for vision backbone learning. This finding is complemented by large-scale generative models such as PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794], which jointly scales vision and language components within a unified text-generation interface, supporting a broad range of vision, language, and multimodal tasks across many languages.

Beyond purely contrastive or purely generative objectives, several works pursue hybrid architectures that support both understanding and generation. [arxiv_2101.11562] highlights the fundamental tension between these two disciplines—understanding benefits from unrestricted cross-modal message passing, whereas generation requires controlled autoregressive decoding—and proposes a decoupled encoder-decoder network with scheduled sampling to reconcile them. Similarly, [arxiv_2206.07643] addresses the limitation of methods that target either high-level semantic tasks or fine-grained region-level understanding in isolation, proposing a coarse-to-fine pretraining strategy with fusion in the backbone to bridge both levels of visual comprehension within a single framework. A further axis of variation concerns parameter efficiency: [arxiv_2303.11866] demonstrate that effective vision-language alignment can be achieved through a small number of targeted parameter updates to pretrained vision and language models, substantially reducing the computational overhead typically associated with full contrastive pretraining. Together, these works reveal that pretraining objectives, supervision strategies, learning schedules, and parameter budgets are all consequential design dimensions in VLP.

## 2. Prompt Learning and Parameter-Efficient Transfer

### 2.1 Visual and Multimodal Prompt Learning

Visual and multimodal prompt learning has emerged as a productive extension of the prompt paradigm from NLP into vision and vision-language settings, though the transfer is not straightforward. A central challenge is that most visual prompt learning methods are designed around discriminative objectives, creating a mismatch with the generative pretraining consistency that makes prompt learning effective in language models. To address this, [arxiv_2303.04998] proposes rethinking visual prompt learning through the lens of masked visual token modeling, aligning the downstream adaptation mechanism more closely with the pretraining objective. This stands in contrast to additive input-space approaches such as convolutional visual prompts, which focus on robustness to out-of-distribution samples: [arxiv_2303.00198] introduces convolutional visual prompts specifically to overcome the overfitting that arises when high-dimensional additive prompt vectors are used in self-supervised, test-time adaptation settings without labeled data, highlighting a practical limitation of standard visual prompting under label scarcity.

In the joint vision-language domain, soft prompt learning has attracted considerable attention as a parameter-efficient strategy for adapting large pretrained models with few examples. Building on contrastive pretraining frameworks such as CLIP, Context Optimization (CoOp) introduced learnable context tokens into the textual branch, yet its static prompts generalize poorly across domains. Conditional Prompt Learning addressed this limitation by generating instance-conditioned prompts, enabling more flexible adaptation [arxiv_2203.05557]. However, a persistent overfitting problem remains in existing soft prompt methods, where accuracy on unseen classes degrades substantially; LASP responds with a text-to-text optimization framework that improves generalization across classes [arxiv_2210.01115]. A further scalability bottleneck arises because existing prompt tuning frameworks must parallelize learnable inputs across all categories, incurring prohibitive GPU memory costs; Compound Text-Guided Prompt Tuning mitigates this by incorporating image-adaptive cues to decouple category enumeration from prompt optimization [arxiv_2312.06401]. When labeled target data are unavailable entirely, [arxiv_2305.00350] proposes an unsupervised fine-tuning regime that directly optimizes prompts by aligning model predictions with unlabeled distributions, removing the dependency on annotations. A complementary direction leverages natural-language domain descriptions rather than target images: [arxiv_2212.03241] demonstrates that a textual prompt alone can drive zero-shot domain adaptation by steering source-trained feature statistics toward a described target domain, underscoring the versatility of prompt-driven approaches across diverse multimodal transfer scenarios.

Prompt-based adaptation has also been extended to more specialized multimodal tasks: [arxiv_2205.13125] leverages prompt-based learning for unpaired image captioning, using pretrained vision-language knowledge to bridge the gap between unaligned visual and linguistic domains.

### 2.2 Parameter-Efficient Adaptation of VLP Models

As VLP model sizes grow rapidly, full fine-tuning becomes computationally expensive for both training and storage, motivating techniques that tune only a small subset of parameters [arxiv_2308.09804]. VL-PET addresses this challenge by integrating modular modifications such as Adapter and LoRA into encoder-decoder architectures with granularity control, demonstrating that competitive performance can be achieved while updating far fewer parameters than full fine-tuning [arxiv_2308.09804]. A related line of work extends parameter-efficient adaptation to multimodal and cross-modal settings beyond static image-text pairs. In video-language contexts, fixing the language model and training only the vision module limits cross-modal information exchange; parameter-efficient multimodal transformers address this by enabling joint adaptation with reduced memory overhead [arxiv_2012.04124]. Similarly, the LAVISH adapter demonstrates that frozen vision transformers pretrained solely on visual data can be efficiently adapted to audio-visual tasks by introducing lightweight latent adapters without modifying any original parameters [arxiv_2212.07983], suggesting that the representational capacity of large pretrained ViTs generalizes across modalities when appropriately prompted through adapter modules.

Lightweight architectural adaptation has also gained traction in language-grounded settings. I-Tuning freezes a pretrained language model and inserts a small set of trainable parameters to bridge visual inputs, substantially reducing training cost for image captioning [arxiv_2202.06574]. Similarly, PILL introduces adapter experts and attention gating mechanisms to plug visual information into large language models, enabling diverse instruction following with minimal parameter overhead [arxiv_2311.02126]. Soft prompt tuning conditions frozen models on downstream tasks through learned prompt tokens, yet under few-shot settings it is highly sensitive to initialization and prone to overfitting [arxiv_2303.12314]; meta-gradient regularization has been proposed to stabilize generalization [arxiv_2303.12314], while self-training on unlabeled data, as in LiST, offers a complementary strategy for improving few-shot prompt-based fine-tuning [arxiv_2110.06274]. Comprehensive benchmarks such as LVLM-eHub provide systematic evaluation of the resulting multimodal capabilities [arxiv_2306.09265]. Taken together, these approaches illustrate a convergence toward methods that preserve pretrained representations while enabling flexible, low-cost adaptation across diverse downstream tasks.

## 3. Visual Grounding and Localization

### 3.1 Referring Expression Comprehension and Visual Grounding

Early work on referring expression comprehension (REC) established the need for models capable of jointly reasoning over visual and linguistic inputs to localize specific objects described by natural language phrases. [arxiv_1901.00850] highlighted a critical limitation in this space, demonstrating that existing benchmark datasets suffer from systematic bias and that state-of-the-art models cannot be reliably evaluated on their intermediate reasoning steps, motivating the development of more diagnostically rigorous benchmarks. Complementing this concern for evaluation quality, [arxiv_1711.08389] proposed an end-to-end approach for phrase grounding that jointly learns multiple text-conditioned embeddings within a single model, introducing a concept weight branch to automatically assign phrases to semantically distinct subspaces rather than relying on predefined assignments, thereby simplifying the grounding pipeline while improving flexibility.

As the field matured, researchers began extending REC beyond static, third-person images. [arxiv_2308.12035] introduced a first-person perspective dataset derived from Ego4D, arguing that grounding referring expressions in egocentric video frames represents a substantially more demanding capability necessary for embodied agents such as robotic systems and wearable devices, and that conventional image-based benchmarks fail to capture the complexity of real-world localization under such conditions.

Fine-grained alignment between vision and language has emerged as a key challenge for VLP models applied to grounding tasks. [arxiv_2210.04183] observed that most existing multimodal pretraining methods excel at global-level image-text alignment while lacking effective fine-grained interaction, proposing jointly masked multimodal modeling to address this gap. Similarly, [arxiv_2303.02995] noted that contrastive models like CLIP do not explicitly encode hierarchical structure in visual or linguistic representations, limiting their capacity for localized understanding. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2209.07046] specifically examined the visual interpretability of CLIP's feature maps, an aspect that had been largely overlooked despite its direct relevance to grounding. Together, these works underscore a shared consensus that coarse global alignment is insufficient for precise visual grounding and that richer, spatially aware representations are essential for advancing REC within the VLP paradigm.

Early work on grounding natural language in visual scenes emphasized the importance of combining individual word meanings to interpret complex referring expressions [arxiv_1107.0031], and subsequent research demonstrated that integrating perceptual context is essential for resolving linguistic ambiguities in multimodal settings [arxiv_1603.08079]. These foundational insights motivated more sophisticated cross-modal pretraining frameworks. [arxiv_2312.11967] addresses the challenge of distinguishing a target object from semantically similar distractors through context disentangling and prototype inheriting, arguing that discriminative contextual information is systematically underutilized by prior methods, particularly when multiple objects share the same category. Complementarily, [arxiv_2112.15324] identifies a confounding bias between language and location in grounding pipelines, showing that models often rely on spurious language-location associations rather than genuine visual reasoning. In low-annotation regimes, [arxiv_2201.06686] proposes bidirectional cross-modal matching for unpaired referring expression grounding, extending the applicability of grounding methods beyond fully supervised settings. [arxiv_2207.02334] further demonstrates that grounding can be learned weakly within vision-language transformers without relying on pre-trained object detectors, broadening the scope of fine-grained alignment. Probing studies reinforce these concerns: [arxiv_2005.00619] shows that text-only contextual representations achieve only partial alignment with visual representations of concrete nouns, suggesting that pretraining on language alone is insufficient for robust visual grounding.

### 3.2 Referring Image Segmentation and Region-Level Alignment

Referring image segmentation represents a particularly demanding instantiation of visual grounding, requiring models to produce pixel-level masks for targets specified through free-form natural language rather than predefined category labels [arxiv_2203.16768]. Early approaches drew on referring expression comprehension methods that modeled visual context by comparing candidate objects within a scene, recognizing that relational descriptions demand awareness of surrounding entities [arxiv_1608.00272]. However, the predominance of convolutional architectures introduced a structural limitation: CNNs struggle to capture long-range dependencies between linguistic entities and image regions, and lack the flexibility needed for fine-grained cross-modal interaction [arxiv_2203.16768]. Transformer-based alternatives have therefore gained traction, with one line of work proposing convolution-free architectures that attend jointly over visual and linguistic tokens to overcome these shortcomings [arxiv_2203.16768].

At the level of instance-specific reasoning, a complementary distinction exists between one-stage methods that directly segment from fused features and two-stage pipelines that first generate instance proposals before selecting among them via language matching [arxiv_2204.12109]. The latter approach affords richer instance-level representations but introduces dependency on an upstream detector, whereas one-stage methods benefit from end-to-end optimization. Region-level alignment in VLP provides a natural foundation for both paradigms: VL-BERT encodes region-of-interest features alongside word tokens within a unified Transformer, enabling the model to associate linguistic phrases with specific image regions during pretraining [arxiv_1908.08530]. More recent work has identified relation-level alignment as a further gap, noting that models may correctly localize individual entities while failing to capture directional spatial relations between them [arxiv_2212.10549]. Extending grounding to panoptic settings, where both things and stuff regions must be jointly localized from narrative descriptions, pushes region-level alignment toward a spatially fine and semantically general formulation [arxiv_2109.04988], highlighting that pixel-level and region-level objectives are increasingly converging within the VLP paradigm.

### 3.3 Instance-Level Tasks and Weakly Supervised Localization

Instance-level vision-language tasks demand that models move beyond coarse image-text matching toward fine-grained spatial understanding, including object detection, phrase grounding, and referring expression comprehension. Early work in weakly supervised phrase grounding demonstrated that image-caption alignment could serve as a training signal for localizing textual phrases in images without explicit region-level annotations. [arxiv_1903.11649] proposed an end-to-end model that uses caption-to-image retrieval as a downstream task to guide phrase localization, inferring latent correspondences between regions-of-interest and phrases. Similarly, [arxiv_1803.03879] addressed the same weakly supervised setting by incorporating knowledge-aided consistency to improve region-language mapping when explicit supervision is unavailable. Both approaches highlight the challenge of bridging visual regions and linguistic descriptions without dense annotation.

VLP frameworks have increasingly incorporated instance-level objectives to support such tasks. LXMERT [arxiv_1908.07490] introduced a large-scale Transformer architecture with dedicated cross-modality encoders and object-level visual features, enabling strong performance on tasks requiring spatial reasoning and object-level alignment. Complementing this, [arxiv_2008.08012] argued that existing attention mechanisms lack sufficient linguistic understanding, proposing linguistically-aware attention to reduce the semantic gap between modalities in tasks such as visual grounding. Beyond grounding, instance-level pretraining has been extended to more specialized domains: [arxiv_2203.03911] demonstrated that VLP techniques can be adapted for scene text detection and spotting, a task requiring precise instance-level localization of textual elements within images. Meanwhile, [arxiv_2206.07669] proposed a unified sequence interface that reformulates diverse vision tasks—including detection and segmentation—as token generation problems, offering a generalizable architecture that subsumes instance-level tasks within a single framework.

## 4. Image Captioning with VLP

### 4.1 Pretraining Objectives and Cross-Modal Alignment for Captioning

VLP has increasingly relied on cross-modal alignment objectives to bridge the semantic gap between visual and linguistic modalities. A dominant paradigm is cross-modal masked language modeling (CMLM), which encourages models to recover masked tokens by attending to paired visual inputs [arxiv_2211.13437]. However, this objective has been shown to suffer from notable limitations: [arxiv_2211.15398] identifies two critical failure modes—a modality bias whereby a substantial proportion of masked tokens can be recovered using linguistic context alone without consulting visual features, and an underutilization of fine-grained image-token correspondences. These findings suggest that CMLM, while foundational, is insufficient on its own to enforce robust cross-modal grounding.

To address such shortcomings, several refined objectives have been proposed. [arxiv_2211.13437] introduces semantic completion learning, which targets the visual and linguistic information that standard masked modeling tasks tend to overlook, thereby promoting richer cross-modal interactions. Similarly, [arxiv_2211.15398] proposes leveraging per image-token consistency to ensure that visual inputs are more actively consulted during pretraining. Beyond masking-based strategies, [arxiv_2210.05335] highlights the underexplored problem of multimodal uncertainty, arguing that both inter- and intra-modal ambiguity complicate cross-modal alignment, and proposes uncertainty-aware pretraining to handle cases where visual or linguistic signals may correspond to multiple plausible targets. Collectively, these works reveal that effective cross-modal alignment requires objectives that go beyond simple token recovery, accounting for modality bias, semantic completeness, and uncertainty.

### 4.2 Zero-Shot and Training-Efficient Image Captioning

Early work on zero-shot and training-efficient image captioning emerged as a response to the data-hungry nature of supervised captioning pipelines. Traditional approaches, exemplified by sequence-to-sequence architectures such as Show and Tell [arxiv_1609.06647], required large volumes of paired image-sentence data and offered little flexibility when confronted with unseen object categories or new domains. This limitation motivated the zero-shot novel object captioning paradigm, wherein models must generate descriptions of novel objects without additional training samples [ss_480423].

VLP has substantially advanced training efficiency by learning transferable representations from large-scale web-harvested image-text pairs. However, noisy many-to-many correspondences in web data undermine data and compute efficiency [arxiv_2204.04588]; addressing caption quality in such datasets has therefore become a priority, with [arxiv_2307.10350] demonstrating that replacing nondescriptive alt-text with generated captions improves the utility of web-scraped data for downstream vision-language models. A complementary line of work explores lightweight VLP architectures to reduce computational overhead: MiniVLM [arxiv_2012.06946] shows that smaller pretrained models can retain competitive performance on captioning benchmarks while being more practical for deployment, and eP-ALM [arxiv_2303.11403] proposes augmenting frozen large language models with perceptual adapters, achieving strong captioning performance with minimal additional training. [arxiv_2306.07915] further challenges the assumption that contrastive pretraining is superior, demonstrating that captioning-based pretraining is a scalable and competitive alternative for learning vision representations. Collectively, these contributions illustrate a shift toward pretraining strategies and architectural designs that reduce reliance on labeled data while preserving or enhancing generalization.

### 4.3 Generative Pretraining for Captioning

Generative pretraining has become a central paradigm for advancing image captioning, with researchers increasingly integrating large-scale language model pretraining into vision-language architectures. A foundational motivation is the recognition that purely encoder-based pretraining leaves the decoder undertrained; as demonstrated in the context of sequence-to-sequence pretraining for neural machine translation, jointly pretraining the decoder alongside the encoder yields substantially different representational properties compared to encoder-only approaches, with the decoder's pretraining shown to have significant downstream impact on generation quality [arxiv_2203.08442]. This insight carries directly into captioning, where the generative component must be carefully initialized.

Building on this principle, vision-guided generative pretraining has been explored to leverage large-scale pretrained language models for multimodal generation tasks. [arxiv_2109.02401] demonstrates that generative pretrained language models can be effectively guided by visual signals for multimodal abstractive summarization, proposing an architecture that injects vision modality information into an otherwise text-centric generative pretraining framework. This approach highlights a broader trend of adapting frozen or lightly fine-tuned language models to visual inputs rather than training multimodal generators from scratch. The tension between understanding and generation objectives in encoder-decoder pretraining, noted by [arxiv_2101.11562], is directly relevant here: scheduled sampling provides one mechanism for reconciling these competing demands within a unified architecture.

## 5. Multilingual and Cross-Modal Representation Learning

### 5.1 Multilingual Vision-Language Pretraining and Cross-Lingual Transfer

Multilingual vision-language pretraining has emerged as a productive research direction aimed at learning universal representations that span both modalities and languages. A recurring challenge is the reliance on strictly aligned multilingual image-text pairs, which are scarce and difficult to scale. Early work demonstrated that a single unified model could generate captions in multiple languages by introducing artificial control tokens, offering a lightweight but effective approach to multilingual image captioning without requiring separate language-specific models. More recently, [arxiv_2305.15483] demonstrates that weakly supervised VLP, which relies on object tags as cross-modal anchors rather than full captions, can reduce data costs while preserving downstream performance, though such approaches are constrained by their reliance on local image descriptions.

Evaluation has matured alongside these modeling advances. [arxiv_2205.13125] approaches the related problem of unpaired image captioning through prompt-based learning, arguing that adversarial and reinforcement-learning methods used in prior unpaired captioning work capture only limited cross-domain information. The multilingual dimension remains underexplored in evaluation infrastructure, with most existing VLP benchmarks remaining English-centric—a gap that motivates the construction of more culturally and linguistically diverse evaluation resources. These benchmarks underscore that cross-lingual transfer in vision-language models remains an open problem requiring both stronger pretraining methods and more representative evaluation resources.

### 5.2 Cross-Modal Representation Alignment and Fusion

Cross-modal representation alignment and fusion constitute a foundational challenge in VLP, as images and text naturally reside in distinct regions of the feature space, making direct instance-level alignment difficult, particularly when representations are still evolving during training. Early transformer-based approaches addressed this by extending the BERT architecture to jointly encode both modalities: VL-BERT treats words and region-of-interest visual features as a unified input sequence, allowing self-attention mechanisms to model cross-modal dependencies within a single backbone [arxiv_1908.08530]. While this joint encoding strategy proved effective, it relies on a relatively unconstrained fusion process that does not explicitly guide semantic correspondence between modalities.

To impose more structured alignment, subsequent work proposes using a representation codebook to mediate alignment, addressing the instability of direct instance-level matching by mapping both modalities to discrete shared codes before fusion. These approaches collectively reflect a progression from implicit to increasingly explicit alignment mechanisms. A parallel line of research highlights that global-level alignment, while necessary, is insufficient for tasks requiring fine-grained understanding: [arxiv_2210.04183] addresses this gap through jointly masked multimodal modeling, encouraging the model to learn detailed image-text interactions beyond coarse semantic correspondence. Similarly, contrastive learning has been applied to visual dialog to sharpen cross-modal understanding by distinguishing relevant from irrelevant visual-textual pairings during training. Together, these works illustrate a broader trend toward combining explicit alignment objectives with fine-grained interaction modeling to produce richer cross-modal representations.

### 5.3 Grounding Language Models with Visual Knowledge

Contrastive approaches such as CLIP demonstrated the power of aligning multi-modal representations at scale, yet their reliance on billions of web-crawled samples and hundreds of GPUs poses significant barriers to reproducibility. Subsequent work has sought to address these constraints through knowledge distillation and architectural efficiency. At the representational level, the InfoNCE objective has been shown to implicitly induce a prototype grouping effect, motivating explicit prototypical contrastive objectives to strengthen semantic clustering. Beyond dual-encoder paradigms, multi-grained alignment has emerged as a productive direction, overcoming the limitation of purely object-centric features that struggle to capture inter-object relations.

Grounding language models with visual knowledge represents a complementary strand of research. [arxiv_2005.00619] shows that text-only contextual representations achieve only partial alignment with visual representations of concrete nouns, confirming that pretraining on language alone is insufficient for robust visual grounding. Rather than modifying model weights, parameter-efficient visual prompting adapts the visual encoder of vision-language models in a black-box-compatible manner, as discussed in Section 2.2. [arxiv_2109.02401] demonstrates that generative pretrained language models can be effectively guided by visual signals, while [arxiv_2311.02126] introduces adapter experts and attention gating mechanisms to plug visual information into large language models with minimal parameter overhead. Evaluating and aligning such models with human preferences has also received attention, with few-shot fine-tuning approaches demonstrating that targeted adaptation can substantially improve instruction-following capabilities.

### 5.4 Unified and Generative Vision-Language Modeling

Early efforts in VLP established that learning aligned cross-modal representations could yield strong performance across a wide range of downstream tasks. However, many such methods rely on global image-text similarity or coarse cross-modal attention, failing to capture fine-grained semantic correspondences between visual regions and textual phrases. These findings collectively suggest that alignment granularity is a central axis of progress in the field.

On the generative and unified modeling front, a key question is whether discriminatively pretrained vision-language transformers can be extended to generative tasks such as image captioning and visual question answering; architectural modifications have been shown to enable such transfer [arxiv_2101.11562]. More principled approaches pretrain universal encoder-decoder networks designed from the outset to support both perception and generation tasks, with PaLI [arxiv_2209.06794] representing a prominent example of jointly scaling both components. The tension between understanding and generation objectives identified by [arxiv_2101.11562] motivates scheduled sampling as a reconciliation mechanism, while [arxiv_2206.07643]'s coarse-to-fine pretraining strategy offers an alternative path toward bridging both levels of visual comprehension within a single framework. [arxiv_2206.07669]'s unified sequence interface, which reformulates diverse vision tasks as token generation problems, represents a further step toward fully generative unified architectures. Together, these works trace a trajectory from task-specific discriminative pretraining toward flexible, generative, and unified architectures capable of handling the full breadth of vision-language tasks.

## 6. Evaluation and Benchmarking

Rigorous evaluation is essential for measuring progress in VLP, yet existing benchmarks exhibit several systematic limitations that complicate fair comparison. A recurring concern is dataset bias: [arxiv_1901.00850] demonstrated that state-of-the-art grounding models cannot be reliably evaluated on their intermediate reasoning steps due to systematic bias in benchmark datasets, motivating the development of more diagnostically rigorous evaluation protocols. Similarly, [arxiv_2308.12035] argues that conventional image-based benchmarks fail to capture the complexity of real-world localization under egocentric conditions, motivating first-person perspective evaluation datasets.

Beyond individual task benchmarks, comprehensive multi-task evaluation frameworks have emerged to provide holistic assessment of multimodal capabilities. LVLM-eHub [arxiv_2306.09265] provides systematic evaluation of large vision-language models across diverse multimodal capabilities, offering a more complete picture of model strengths and weaknesses than single-task benchmarks. Probing studies provide a complementary diagnostic lens: [arxiv_2005.00619] shows that text-only contextual representations achieve only partial alignment with visual representations of concrete nouns, revealing a fundamental gap between linguistic and visual grounding that standard benchmarks may not surface.

The cultural and linguistic representativeness of evaluation resources has also received increasing attention. Most existing VLP evaluation infrastructure is English-centric, with non-English and non-Western visual content underrepresented. This gap motivates the construction of more culturally and linguistically diverse benchmarks, as well as evaluation protocols that explicitly measure cross-lingual transfer rather than treating English performance as a proxy for general capability. Addressing caption quality in web-scraped datasets—for instance, by replacing nondescriptive alt-text with generated captions [arxiv_2307.10350]—is a related concern that affects both training data quality and the validity of downstream evaluation. Collectively, these evaluation challenges underscore that progress in VLP requires not only stronger models but also more rigorous, representative, and diagnostically informative benchmarks.

## 7. Discussion and Outlook

The preceding sections reveal several converging trends in vision-language pretraining. First, the field has moved from simple contrastive objectives toward richer pretraining paradigms that account for noise [arxiv_2204.04588], modality asymmetry [arxiv_2305.20088], semantic completeness [arxiv_2211.13437], and multimodal uncertainty [arxiv_2210.05335]. Second, parameter efficiency has emerged as a central concern as model sizes grow, with prompt learning [arxiv_2203.05557, arxiv_2210.01115, arxiv_2312.06401], lightweight adapters [arxiv_2308.09804, arxiv_2212.07983, arxiv_2311.02126], and meta-learning stabilization [arxiv_2303.12314] all offering complementary strategies for low-cost adaptation. Third, the granularity of alignment has progressively increased, from global image-text matching toward region-level [arxiv_1908.08530], pixel-level [arxiv_2203.16768], and relation-level [arxiv_2212.10549] objectives, driven by the demands of grounding and localization tasks.

Several open challenges remain. The tension between understanding and generation objectives [arxiv_2101.11562] has not been fully resolved, and unified architectures that handle both remain an active research frontier [arxiv_2206.07669, arxiv_2209.06794]. The multilingual dimension of VLP is comparatively underdeveloped, with most pretraining and evaluation infrastructure remaining English-centric. Weakly supervised and zero-shot settings [arxiv_2305.15483, arxiv_2305.00350, arxiv_2212.03241] offer promising directions for reducing annotation costs, but their limitations—particularly the reliance on local image descriptors and sensitivity to domain shift—require further investigation. Finally, evaluation methodology itself requires continued attention: benchmark bias [arxiv_1901.00850], cultural representativeness, and the gap between global and fine-grained alignment metrics [arxiv_2210.04183] all complicate the reliable measurement of progress. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated advances in pretraining objectives, architectural design, parameter-efficient adaptation, and evaluation methodology.
