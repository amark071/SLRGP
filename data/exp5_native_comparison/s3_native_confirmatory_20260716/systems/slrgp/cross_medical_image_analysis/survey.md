# Deep Learning for Medical Image Analysis

# Deep Learning for Medical Image Analysis: A Survey

## 1. Broad Surveys and Overviews

The rapid adoption of deep learning in medical image analysis has prompted a substantial body of survey literature aimed at consolidating methodological advances and cataloguing empirical applications. The most widely cited of these overviews, Litjens et al. [arxiv_1702.05747], provides a comprehensive examination of deep learning concepts pertinent to medical imaging, reviewing over 300 contributions and covering tasks ranging from image classification and object detection to segmentation across multiple organ systems and imaging modalities. This breadth distinguishes it from more focused treatments such as Lundervold and Lundervold [arxiv_1811.10052], whose overview concentrates specifically on MRI, situating deep learning within the broader trajectory of machine learning and arguing that deep neural networks became the dominant paradigm following their breakthrough performance on benchmark tasks around 2009.

Complementing these earlier surveys, Chen et al. [arxiv_2105.13381] offer a more recent synthesis that acknowledges the remarkable success of deep learning across disease detection and diagnosis tasks while foregrounding a persistent bottleneck: the scarcity of large, well-annotated medical datasets. This concern with practical limitations is echoed in Ul Haq [arxiv_2202.08546], which traces the field's momentum to the 2012 ImageNet competition and surveys how convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have since been adapted to diverse medical imaging problems. From a somewhat earlier vantage point, Razzak et al. [arxiv_1704.06825] situate deep learning within the particular demands of the healthcare sector, emphasising that human expert interpretation of medical images is inherently limited in scale and consistency, thereby motivating automated approaches. Anwar et al. [arxiv_1709.02250] narrow the scope further by focusing specifically on CNN architectures, reviewing their application to clinical image analysis tasks and highlighting advances in biomedical engineering that have made the field a leading area of research. Altaf et al. [arxiv_1902.05655] take a broader conceptual stance, surveying recent developments while offering a critical appraisal of challenges and future directions, and noting that community interest had grown sufficiently by 2018 to warrant a dedicated Medical Imaging with Deep Learning conference.

The meta-survey by Egger et al. [arxiv_2011.08184] situates deep learning within the broader landscape of artificial intelligence across scientific disciplines, drawing on the analogy between artificial neural networks and biological brain structure to aid conceptual understanding—a framing particularly useful for newcomers. Maier et al. [arxiv_1810.05401] adopt an explicitly pedagogical orientation, proceeding systematically from theoretical foundations—including the perceptron and basic neural network theory—through to practical applications in medical image processing. This structured, bottom-up approach contrasts with broader surveys that assume greater prior familiarity with the field.

Taken together, these surveys chart both the rapid methodological progress and the enduring challenges—data scarcity, interpretability, and clinical translation—that continue to define the field, tracing an evolving discourse from foundational methodological mapping [arxiv_1702.05747] and domain-specific pedagogy [arxiv_1811.10052], through critical conceptual synthesis [arxiv_1902.05655], toward an increasing concern with real-world clinical deployment [arxiv_2105.13381].

## 2. CNN-Based Approaches and Transfer Learning

### 2.1 Methodological Foundations

Convolutional neural networks have emerged as the dominant architectural paradigm in medical image analysis. Several works adopt a more pedagogically oriented approach to reviewing CNN applicability across diverse imaging contexts. Anwar et al. [arxiv_1709.02250] survey CNN-based approaches across multiple clinical problem types, while Bernal et al. [arxiv_1712.03747] narrow their scope to brain MRI, reviewing CNN techniques for lesion segmentation, anatomical segmentation, and classification. The question of training strategy is addressed directly by Tajbakhsh et al. [arxiv_1706.00712], who empirically compare full training against fine-tuning of pre-trained CNNs across four medical imaging applications, finding that fine-tuning generally outperforms training from scratch when labeled medical data are limited. Shin et al. [arxiv_1602.03409] examine how dataset characteristics and transfer learning interact in computer-aided detection tasks, demonstrating that the benefit of pre-training depends critically on the degree of domain mismatch between source and target datasets.

### 2.2 Transfer Learning, Fine-Tuning, and Self-Supervised Pre-training

Transfer learning has emerged as a foundational strategy for applying deep learning to medical image analysis, particularly when labeled data are scarce. Targeted transfer learning approaches have been proposed with comparative evaluations demonstrating that careful selection of source domains and fine-tuning protocols substantially improves performance on small medical datasets [arxiv_1912.06761]. Multi-source transfer learning using CNNs has been applied to lung pattern analysis in CT imaging, where leveraging features pre-trained across multiple source datasets yielded more robust representations than single-source transfer [arxiv_1612.02589]. In low-resource settings such as gastrointestinal landmark classification, systematic evaluation of CNN representations confirmed that the choice of pre-trained backbone and the extent of fine-tuning critically determine downstream performance [arxiv_1903.11176].

Beyond supervised pre-training, self-supervised strategies have gained considerable traction as a means of exploiting large quantities of unlabeled medical images. A self-supervised framework built around Swin Transformers demonstrated that tailored proxy tasks enable effective learning of both global and local 3D representations transferable to segmentation and detection tasks [arxiv_2111.14791]. For 2D segmentation, self-supervised pretraining has similarly been shown to reduce dependence on manually annotated data [arxiv_2209.00314]. Addressing computational barriers inherent to many self-supervised methods, cross-architectural self-supervision has been proposed to deliver efficient representation learning under the constrained batch sizes and limited data typical of healthcare applications [arxiv_2308.10064]. Benchmarking studies have systematically compared transfer learning strategies across medical imaging tasks, providing practitioners with empirical guidance on which pre-training regimes transfer most effectively [arxiv_2108.05930]. Collectively, these findings indicate a clear trajectory toward pre-training strategies that decouple representation learning from annotation availability.

### 2.3 Semi-Supervised and Label-Efficient Learning

The scarcity of expert-annotated data remains one of the most persistent challenges in medical image analysis, motivating a broad family of semi-supervised and label-efficient learning strategies. A foundational line of work exploits the smoothness assumption, encouraging model outputs to remain consistent under perturbations of similar inputs. Building on this principle, cross-adversarial local distribution regularisation enforces output consistency across augmented views in semi-supervised segmentation [arxiv_2310.01176], while extreme consistency training simultaneously addresses annotation scarcity and domain shift [arxiv_2004.11966]. Complementing these approaches, a risk-minimisation reformulation of semi-supervised segmentation on unlabeled data offers a theoretically grounded alternative to heuristic smoothness constraints [ss_478321]. Beyond consistency regularisation, adversarial and generative frameworks have been explored: a feature-enhanced adversarial semi-supervised network has been developed for pulmonary embolism segmentation in CTPA images [arxiv_2204.04217], and early work established a precedent for learning segmentation from weak annotations such as bounding boxes and image-level labels using deep CNNs [arxiv_1502.02734]. More recently, diffusion models have been applied to semi-supervised segmentation, with timestep ensembling of denoising diffusion probabilistic models yielding robust predictions under limited annotation budgets [arxiv_2311.07421].

For classification tasks, global latent mixing provides stronger regularisation than local perturbation alone [arxiv_2005.11217], while mutual label correction addresses the related problem of noisy labels [arxiv_2109.05159]. Label efficiency is further pursued through cross-task reuse, as classification labels can guide segmentation learning [arxiv_1912.12533], and through denoising-based supervision [ss_482123]. Self-supervised pretraining offers another route: multimodal puzzle tasks enable annotation-efficient representation learning [arxiv_1912.05396]. Domain adaptation under label constraints is addressed by jointly tackling domain shift and annotation scarcity [arxiv_2212.02078], while FocalMix extends semi-supervised learning to 3D detection [arxiv_2003.09108]. Most recently, combining selective labeling with prompt tuning has been shown to mitigate overfitting in label-limited segmentation scenarios [arxiv_2308.04911].

## 3. Unsupervised and Reinforcement Learning Paradigms

### 3.1 Unsupervised Representation Learning

Unsupervised learning has emerged as a principled response to annotation scarcity. Raza and Singh [arxiv_1812.07715] surveyed unsupervised deep learning methods for medical image analysis, arguing that, unlike supervised approaches, unsupervised techniques are not biased by labelling choices and can exploit the full breadth of available imaging data. Building on this foundation, contrastive and self-supervised methods have attracted growing attention. Zhang et al. [arxiv_2211.08557] demonstrated that augmenting instance-discrimination contrastive learning with unsupervised feature clustering improves representation quality for segmentation tasks, addressing a known limitation of purely instance-based objectives. Han et al. [arxiv_2101.04269] combined radiomic features with contrastive learning for chest X-ray pneumonia detection, illustrating how self-supervised pretraining can reduce dependence on manual labels in high-throughput clinical settings.

### 3.2 Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning represents a distinct paradigm that has also gained traction in medical imaging. Zhou et al. [arxiv_2103.05115] provided a structured tutorial and literature review of deep reinforcement learning, demonstrating its potential for sequential decision-making tasks such as landmark detection and image acquisition optimisation. Hu et al. [arxiv_2206.14302] extended this analysis by cataloguing specific applications while acknowledging persistent challenges including sample inefficiency and the difficulty of reward design in clinical contexts. Together, these unsupervised and reinforcement learning directions reflect a broader disciplinary shift toward learning frameworks capable of operating effectively under the label-scarce conditions characteristic of medical imaging practice.

## 4. 3D and Volumetric Deep Learning

### 4.1 Surveys of Volumetric Approaches

Volumetric medical image segmentation presents unique challenges compared to natural image segmentation, primarily because modalities such as MRI and CT produce three-dimensional data whose spatial context must be preserved across all axes. Singh et al. [arxiv_2004.00218] review 3D deep learning architectures applied to medical images, highlighting how advances in GPU hardware and the growing availability of volumetric datasets have enabled the adoption of 3D CNN architectures for disease diagnosis and clinical decision support. Niyas et al. [arxiv_2108.08467] provide a dedicated survey of 3D CNN-based medical image segmentation, underscoring the importance of volumetric representations for accurate delineation of anatomical structures. Lundervold and Lundervold [arxiv_1811.10052] offer a modality-specific perspective, focusing on MRI and tracing how deep learning has transformed the analysis of this inherently three-dimensional imaging modality.

### 4.2 2D vs. 3D and Hybrid (2.5D) Approaches

Pure 2D CNNs process individual slices independently, forfeiting the rich inter-slice contextual information present in volumetric data. Conversely, full 3D CNNs can exploit spatial dependencies across all three axes but incur substantially higher computational costs and GPU memory demands, hindering clinical deployment [arxiv_1906.02817]. This tension has motivated the development of intermediate strategies. The so-called 2.5D or pseudo-3D approach provides a stack of adjacent slices as input to a 2D network, enabling the model to incorporate local volumetric context without the full overhead of 3D convolutions [arxiv_1912.09287]. Zhang et al. [arxiv_2010.06163] conducted an empirical study of 2.5D solutions, demonstrating that carefully designed hybrid architectures can achieve computation-efficient volumetric segmentation competitive with full 3D models. Vu et al. [arxiv_1912.09287] similarly found that incorporating neighbouring slices consistently improved over single-slice 2D baselines while remaining more tractable than volumetric 3D inference.

Beyond these conventional trade-offs, Sobirov et al. proposed "super images," a 2D perspective on 3D data that reorganises volumetric slices into a single 2D image, enabling standard 2D architectures to process volumetric information while avoiding the computational burden of 3D operations [arxiv_2205.02847]. To automate the selection among 2D, 3D, and hybrid operators, Zhu et al. introduced V-NAS, a neural architecture search framework that learns which convolution dimensionality to apply at each network layer [arxiv_1906.02817]. Together, these works illustrate a research trajectory moving from manual design heuristics toward principled, data-driven selection of spatial processing strategies.

### 4.3 Novel 3D Architectures

The proliferation of deep learning for volumetric segmentation has driven substantial architectural innovation. One prominent direction involves encoder–decoder frameworks derived from U-Net. RA-UNet integrates hybrid attention mechanisms within a 3D encoder–decoder to extract liver and tumour regions from CT volumes, explicitly addressing the high computational cost of fully 3D networks [arxiv_1811.01328]. Cascaded convolutional and adversarial networks have been proposed for abdominal multi-organ segmentation, extending conditional generative adversarial networks to enforce anatomical plausibility [arxiv_2001.09521], while atrous residual interconnected encoders coupled with attention decoders have demonstrated strong performance for vertebrae segmentation in CT [arxiv_2104.03715].

A second direction pursues efficiency. TernaryNet replaces standard convolutions with sparse and binary operations to enable GPU-free inference [arxiv_1801.09449], and a general lightweight system has been proposed that avoids task-specific tuning while maintaining competitive accuracy across diverse segmentation benchmarks [arxiv_1911.01764]. The Implicit U-Net addresses 3D computational challenges by combining a convolutional feature extractor with an implicit localisation network, reducing memory overhead without sacrificing spatial modelling [arxiv_2206.15217].

More recently, large-kernel designs have emerged as a means of capturing long-range dependencies without the full cost of transformer self-attention. Large-kernel attention modules have been incorporated into 3D segmentation networks to model extensive anatomical variation in organs and tumours [arxiv_2207.11225], and 3D UX-Net modernises hierarchical transformer priors using large-kernel volumetric convolutions, demonstrating competitive performance against Swin-based architectures such as SwinUNETR [arxiv_2209.15076]. Neural architecture search has also been applied to automate the selection between 2D, 3D, and pseudo-3D convolution strategies across network layers [arxiv_1906.02817], complementing empirical studies that systematically evaluate 2.5D solutions as a practical compromise [arxiv_2010.06163].

### 4.4 Generalizable and Lightweight Segmentation Systems

The development of generalizable, lightweight, and efficient segmentation systems represents a critical frontier in volumetric medical image analysis, as clinical deployment demands models that are both computationally tractable and robust across diverse settings. Reviews of the field have consistently highlighted the tension between representational power and practical efficiency [arxiv_2004.00218, arxiv_2108.08467]. Taking efficiency further, Heinrich et al. introduce TernaryNet, which employs sparse and binary convolutions to enable deep model inference without GPUs, making 3D segmentation feasible in resource-constrained, image-guided intervention settings [arxiv_1801.09449]. Perslev et al. directly address generalizability by proposing a single, lightweight network designed to accurately segment a wide variety of 3D medical image types without task-specific pipeline engineering, challenging the prevailing norm of highly specialised systems [arxiv_1911.01764].

Beyond computational efficiency, domain generalization methods tackle the performance degradation that arises when models are deployed across different clinical centres or imaging modalities, with contrastive single-domain approaches offering privacy-preserving alternatives to multi-source adaptation [arxiv_2306.05254]. Cross-modality generalization has also been pursued through semi-supervised transformer-based training strategies that leverage image-level supervision to reduce annotation dependency [arxiv_2309.09246]. Collectively, these efforts reflect a broader recognition that scalable medical image segmentation systems must simultaneously optimise for efficiency, generalizability, and reduced reliance on large annotated datasets [arxiv_2012.05440, arxiv_1808.05205].

## 5. Platforms, Datasets, and Infrastructure

### 5.1 Software Platforms and Frameworks

The growing complexity of volumetric medical image analysis has motivated the development of dedicated software platforms that abstract away low-level implementation details and provide domain-specific functionality. General-purpose deep learning libraries, while flexible, do not natively support the particular requirements of medical imaging pipelines, and adapting them demands considerable engineering effort, leading to widespread duplication of work across research groups [arxiv_1709.03485]. To address this gap, NiftyNet was introduced as one of the earliest dedicated platforms, offering modular components for common medical image analysis tasks including segmentation, regression, and image generation, with built-in support for volumetric data formats and preprocessing routines [arxiv_1709.03485]. MIScnn was proposed as a framework specifically targeting medical image segmentation with CNNs, enabling researchers to configure end-to-end pipelines without requiring standalone, dataset-specific implementations [arxiv_1910.09308]. Whereas NiftyNet emphasised broad task coverage and integration with established neuroimaging toolkits, MIScnn focused more narrowly on segmentation flexibility and ease of pipeline assembly, reflecting a complementary rather than competing design philosophy.

More recently, OpenMedIA extended this ecosystem by providing an open-source toolbox supporting a rich set of deep learning methods—including 2D and 3D classification, segmentation, localisation, and detection—across heterogeneous AI computing platforms, incorporating both PyTorch and MindSpore backends to accommodate diverse hardware environments [arxiv_2208.05616]. This heterogeneous computing support represents a meaningful advance over earlier frameworks, which were predominantly tied to single hardware ecosystems. Collectively, these platforms reflect a broader recognition, documented across multiple surveys [arxiv_1702.05747, arxiv_2004.00218], that reproducible and accessible tooling is essential for translating methodological advances into reliable clinical workflows.

### 5.2 Datasets and Data Curation

The availability of large, well-curated datasets is widely recognised as a foundational prerequisite for effective deep learning in medical image analysis. Li et al. [arxiv_2106.12864] conducted a systematic collection of medical image datasets specifically to address the gap between the volume of data required by deep learning algorithms and what is practically available in clinical settings, while Chen et al. [arxiv_2105.13381] similarly concluded that further improvement of deep learning models is majorly constrained by the absence of large labelled corpora. This concern is echoed in earlier work by Anwar et al. [arxiv_1709.02250] and Lundervold and Lundervold [arxiv_1811.10052], both of which situate data availability as central to progress in the field.

A compounding challenge is the cost and subjectivity of expert annotation. Acquiring ground-truth labels demands substantial specialist time, and multiple annotators frequently produce divergent labels, introducing human bias that must be explicitly modelled during training. To reduce reliance on exhaustive manual labelling, active annotation strategies have been proposed that prioritise the most informative samples for annotation, thereby improving label efficiency. Complementary to this, semi-supervised and task-driven data augmentation strategies expand effective training set size without proportional increases in annotation effort. Where centralised dataset construction is infeasible due to privacy and legal constraints, federated learning offers an alternative paradigm enabling collaborative training across institutions without sharing raw images. Collectively, these strategies reflect a growing recognition that dataset curation for medical imaging must encompass not only volume but also annotation quality, privacy-preserving collection mechanisms, and cross-domain consistency.

## 6. Unsupervised and Generative Deep Learning Methods

### 6.1 Self-Supervised and Contrastive Representation Learning

The scarcity of large annotated datasets in medical imaging has driven substantial interest in self-supervised and contrastive representation learning as alternatives to fully supervised paradigms. Early surveys highlighted that unsupervised deep learning could leverage abundant unlabeled data to circumvent annotation bottlenecks [arxiv_1812.07715], and subsequent work confirmed that limited labeled data remains one of the principal constraints on deep learning progress [arxiv_2105.13381].

Self-supervised methods have emerged as particularly competitive approaches. Zhang et al. [arxiv_2211.08557] demonstrated that augmenting instance-discrimination contrastive learning with unsupervised feature clustering improves representation quality for segmentation tasks, addressing a known limitation of purely instance-based objectives. Han et al. [arxiv_2101.04269] combined radiomic features with contrastive learning for chest X-ray pneumonia detection, illustrating how self-supervised pretraining can reduce dependence on manual labels in high-throughput clinical settings. A self-supervised framework built around Swin Transformers demonstrated that tailored proxy tasks enable effective learning of both global and local 3D representations transferable to segmentation and detection tasks [arxiv_2111.14791]. For 2D segmentation, self-supervised pretraining has similarly been shown to reduce dependence on manually annotated data by initialising models with domain-relevant features learned from unlabeled images [arxiv_2209.00314]. Addressing computational barriers inherent to many self-supervised methods, cross-architectural self-supervision has been proposed to deliver efficient representation learning under the constrained batch sizes and limited data typical of healthcare applications [arxiv_2308.10064]. Multimodal puzzle tasks have further been leveraged for annotation-efficient representation learning [arxiv_1912.05396], and benchmarking studies have systematically compared transfer learning strategies across medical imaging tasks [arxiv_2108.05930].

### 6.2 Unsupervised Domain Adaptation

Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) has emerged as a critical research direction in medical image analysis, addressing the pervasive domain shift problem that arises when models trained on one data distribution are applied to another. Because annotating medical images is costly and expert-intensive, UDA methods seek to transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain, making them particularly attractive for clinical deployment.

Adversarial training and generative adversarial networks have dominated this space. Adversarial loss has been used to align cross-modality feature distributions for biomedical segmentation without requiring target labels, while semantic-aware GANs have been proposed to preserve anatomical structure during image-level translation for chest X-ray segmentation. Vendor-induced distribution shifts have been addressed by coupling U-Net with GAN-based translation. Beyond purely adversarial approaches, variational approximation has been employed to map source and target domains into a shared latent space for cardiac segmentation, and self-attentive spatial adaptive normalisation has been proposed to handle large cross-modality discrepancies. Label-efficient UDA that jointly addresses annotation scarcity and domain shift [arxiv_2212.02078] and contrastive single-domain generalisation approaches [arxiv_2306.05254] offer complementary strategies. Cross-modality generalisation has also been pursued through semi-supervised transformer-based training strategies that leverage image-level supervision [arxiv_2309.09246]. Collectively, these contributions reflect a maturing field moving toward more robust, annotation-efficient, and clinically practical adaptation solutions.

## 7. Disease-Specific and Modality-Specific Applications

Deep learning has been applied across a wide spectrum of disease areas and imaging modalities, with performance varying considerably depending on the clinical context and data availability. Broad surveys have catalogued these applications systematically: Litjens et al. [arxiv_1702.05747] reviewed over 300 contributions spanning classification, detection, and segmentation across modalities including radiology, pathology, and ophthalmology, while Anwar et al. [arxiv_1709.02250] focused specifically on CNN-based approaches across diverse imaging types. Together, these reviews establish that no single modality or disease domain has monopolised deep learning research, though certain areas have attracted disproportionate attention.

MRI represents one of the most extensively studied modalities. Lundervold and Lundervold [arxiv_1811.10052] provided a dedicated overview of deep learning for MRI, noting its particular relevance for neuroimaging and cardiac applications. Bernal et al. [arxiv_1712.03747] further narrowed this focus to brain MRI, reviewing how deep convolutional networks have been applied to lesion segmentation, anatomical parcellation, and classification tasks. The three-dimensional nature of many medical imaging datasets has motivated the development of 3D-specific architectures, as reviewed by Singh et al. [arxiv_2004.00218] and Niyas et al. [arxiv_2108.08467], the latter focusing on 3D CNN-based segmentation methods applicable to volumetric data from CT and MRI alike. Large-kernel attention modules tailored to 3D MRI and CT segmentation have been proposed, arguing that enlarged receptive fields better capture the extensive anatomical variation and low contrast characteristic of organ and tumour boundaries [arxiv_2207.11225].

Annotation scarcity represents a fundamental obstacle across disease-specific applications. A feature-enhanced adversarial semi-supervised network has been developed for pulmonary embolism segmentation in CTPA images [arxiv_2204.04217], and semi-supervised approaches exploiting unlabeled data through adversarial multi-view learning have been applied to diverse segmentation tasks [arxiv_2012.05440]. Domain generalisation methods address performance degradation when models are deployed across different clinical centres or imaging modalities [arxiv_2306.05254], while cross-modality generalisation has been pursued through semi-supervised transformer-based training strategies [arxiv_2309.09246]. Chen et al. [arxiv_2105.13381] contextualise these advances within the broader landscape of deep learning for disease detection and diagnosis, noting that data limitations remain a persistent bottleneck across disease-specific applications regardless of modality.

## 8. Robustness, Interpretability, and Evaluation

Robustness and interpretability present pressing concerns that cut across all application domains. Models trained on noisy labels exhibit sensitivity to annotation errors and inter-observer variability, a problem that has motivated robust training strategies including mutual label correction [arxiv_2109.05159] and denoising-based supervision [ss_482123]. Shortcut learning—where models exploit spurious correlations rather than clinically meaningful features—poses a particular threat in medical contexts where data are limited, and eye-gaze-guided approaches have been proposed to rectify such biases by incorporating clinical attention signals. Transparency and interpretability are not merely desirable but essential for trustworthy clinical deployment, reinforcing that robustness and explainability must be treated as first-class design objectives alongside predictive accuracy.

Evaluation methodology itself warrants careful attention. The choice of benchmark dataset, evaluation metric, and experimental protocol substantially influences reported conclusions, and comparisons across studies are complicated by heterogeneous data splits and preprocessing pipelines. Empirical studies comparing 2D, 2.5D, and 3D segmentation strategies [arxiv_2010.06163, arxiv_1912.09287] illustrate how carefully controlled experimental designs can yield actionable guidance for practitioners, while benchmarking studies of transfer learning strategies [arxiv_2108.05930] provide systematic evidence on which pre-training regimes transfer most effectively across medical imaging tasks. Dedicated platforms such as NiftyNet [arxiv_1709.03485] and MIScnn [arxiv_1910.09308] contribute to reproducibility by standardising pipeline components, and systematic dataset catalogues [arxiv_2106.12864] facilitate fair comparison across methods. Collectively, these infrastructure investments underscore that progress in the field depends as much on rigorous evaluation practices and accessible shared resources as on algorithmic innovation.

## 9. Conclusions

This survey has traced the development of deep learning for medical image analysis across its principal dimensions: broad methodological foundations, CNN-based and transfer learning approaches, unsupervised and reinforcement learning paradigms, 3D and volumetric architectures, software infrastructure, and disease-specific applications. Several cross-cutting themes emerge consistently.

First, data scarcity remains the central bottleneck. Across surveys [arxiv_1702.05747, arxiv_2105.13381, arxiv_1811.10052, arxiv_1709.02250], the limited availability of large, well-annotated medical datasets is identified as the primary constraint on further progress, motivating the entire ecosystem of semi-supervised [arxiv_2310.01176, arxiv_2004.11966, ss_478321], self-supervised [arxiv_2111.14791, arxiv_2209.00314, arxiv_1912.05396], and transfer learning [arxiv_1706.00712, arxiv_1612.02589, arxiv_2108.05930] strategies reviewed here.

Second, the tension between representational power and computational efficiency is a persistent design challenge, particularly for volumetric data. The spectrum from 2D slice-wise processing through hybrid 2.5D approaches [arxiv_1912.09287, arxiv_2010.06163] to full 3D architectures [arxiv_1811.01328, arxiv_2001.09521, arxiv_2104.03715], and the emergence of large-kernel designs [arxiv_2207.11225, arxiv_2209.15076] and neural architecture search [arxiv_1906.02817], reflects sustained effort to navigate this trade-off. Lightweight and generalizable systems [arxiv_1911.01764, arxiv_1801.09449] represent one practical resolution, prioritising deployability without sacrificing competitive accuracy.

Third, the field is maturing from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward systematic infrastructure. Dedicated platforms [arxiv_1709.03485, arxiv_1910.09308, arxiv_2208.05616], curated dataset repositories [arxiv_2106.12864], and domain adaptation frameworks [arxiv_2212.02078, arxiv_2306.05254, arxiv_2309.09246] collectively lower the barrier to reproducible, clinically relevant research. Reinforcement learning [arxiv_2103.05115, arxiv_2206.14302] and unsupervised representation learning [arxiv_1812.07715, arxiv_2211.08557, arxiv_2101.04269] further expand the methodological toolkit available to practitioners operating under annotation constraints.

Finally, robustness, interpretability, and rigorous evaluation must be treated as first-class objectives. As deep learning models approach clinical deployment, concerns about shortcut learning, inter-observer label variability [arxiv_2109.05159, ss_482123], and domain shift [arxiv_2212.02078, arxiv_2306.05254] become paramount. Standardised benchmarking [arxiv_2108.05930, arxiv_2010.06163] and transparent tooling [arxiv_1709.03485, arxiv_1910.09308] are essential complements to algorithmic advances, ensuring that reported gains translate reliably into clinical benefit.
