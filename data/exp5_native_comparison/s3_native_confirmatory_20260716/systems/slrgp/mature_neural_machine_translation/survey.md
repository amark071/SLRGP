# Neural Machine Translation

# Neural Machine Translation: A Survey

## 1. Surveys, Textbooks, and Overviews of NMT

### 1.1 Comprehensive Surveys and Textbook Overviews

The rapid ascent of neural machine translation (NMT) has prompted a range of comprehensive surveys and textbook treatments that collectively map the intellectual terrain of the field. An early and influential contribution is the textbook-style overview by Koehn [arxiv_1709.07809], which provides a foundational introduction to NMT as part of a broader treatment of machine translation. Moving systematically from neural network fundamentals through computation graphs to the attentional sequence-to-sequence architecture, it serves as a pedagogically oriented entry point that explicitly situates NMT within the intellectual tradition of statistical MT. Building on this foundation, Stahlberg [arxiv_1912.02047] offers a more analytically ambitious review that frames NMT as a paradigm shift, documenting how statistical approaches that dominated for decades have been largely superseded by end-to-end neural systems. This historical and comparative framing distinguishes Stahlberg's contribution from the more instructional tone of Koehn's treatment.

Complementing these works, Tan et al. [arxiv_2012.15515] provide a broad survey extending coverage beyond methods to encompass resources and tools, reflecting the maturation of NMT into a practical engineering discipline with substantial infrastructure requirements. Zhang and Zong [arxiv_2004.05809] adopt a forward-looking orientation, reviewing the NMT framework while foregrounding unresolved challenges and prospective research directions. Taken together, these four contributions illustrate how the survey literature has evolved from foundational exposition toward increasingly comprehensive and critically engaged assessments of both the field's achievements and its remaining limitations.

### 1.2 Surveys of Low-Resource and Multilingual NMT

Low-resource NMT has attracted dedicated survey efforts that provide complementary taxonomies of the solution space. Ranathunga et al. [arxiv_2106.15115] offer a broad survey of techniques specifically targeting low-resource language pairs, observing that despite rapid maturation of the field, performance on such pairs remains sub-optimal relative to high-resource settings, primarily due to parallel data scarcity. Wang et al. [arxiv_2107.04239] classify related work into three principal categories—data augmentation, model architecture, and training objectives—providing a structured framework for understanding available approaches. Zhang and Zong [arxiv_2004.05809] situate low-resource translation within a wider review of NMT challenges, contextualising it alongside other open problems.

An important corrective to earlier pessimistic assessments is offered by Sennrich and Zhang [arxiv_1905.11901], who revisit prior claims that NMT categorically underperforms phrase-based statistical MT in low-resource conditions, arguing that such conclusions largely reflect insufficient system adaptation rather than inherent architectural limitations. Lakew et al. [arxiv_2003.14402] contribute an empirical benchmark targeting five African low-resource languages—Swahili, Amharic, Tigrigna, Oromo, and Somali—highlighting the importance of language-specific evaluation, since aggregate findings may obscure difficulties posed by morphologically complex or typologically distant languages. These survey and benchmarking contributions establish the empirical and conceptual foundations upon which more specialised approaches have been built.

## 2. Encoder-Decoder Architecture and Attention Mechanisms

### 2.1 Foundational Architecture

Early NMT systems were built around the encoder-decoder paradigm, in which a single neural network is jointly trained to encode a source sentence into a continuous representation and generate the target sentence from that representation [arxiv_1409.0473]. This architecture has proven remarkably durable: despite the evolution from recurrent networks to Transformer-based models, the encoder-decoder framework remains the de facto standard for state-of-the-art translation systems [arxiv_2210.11807]. Nevertheless, questions persist about whether both components are strictly necessary, and researchers have continued to probe the theoretical motivation for maintaining separate encoding and decoding modules [arxiv_2210.11807].

A pivotal development within this framework was the introduction of attention mechanisms, which allow the decoder to selectively focus on different parts of the encoded source sequence at each generation step, effectively learning to align and translate jointly [arxiv_1409.0473]. While early work concentrated on improving encoder and decoder components individually, subsequent research identified the attention mechanism itself as a critical bottleneck. Zhang et al. [ss_477371] argued that deepening the attention mechanism—rather than solely deepening the encoder or decoder—substantially increases model capacity and improves translation correspondence, complementing the foundational alignment-based attention of Bahdanau et al. [arxiv_1409.0473].

### 2.2 Decoding Directionality and Bidirectional Decoding

Conventional NMT decoders generate translations strictly left-to-right, leaving right-to-left target-side context unexploited. Zhang et al. [arxiv_1801.05122] address this limitation by proposing asynchronous bidirectional decoding, equipping the standard attentional encoder-decoder with a reverse decoder that operates concurrently, thereby enriching generation with bidirectional target-side information while preserving the standard encoder-decoder structure. Complementing architectural changes, several studies have reconsidered inference algorithms. Standard beam search is constrained by fixed beam sizes that can degrade hypothesis quality; Shu and Nakayama [arxiv_1707.01830] propose single-queue decoding, storing all hypotheses in a unified priority queue to allow more flexible candidate selection. Freitag and Al-Onaizan [arxiv_1702.01806] demonstrate that careful tuning of search parameters yields consistent translation improvements, while Li and Jurafsky [arxiv_1601.00372] replace the standard likelihood objective with one that maximises mutual information between source and target, encouraging more diverse and informative outputs. Together, these contributions illustrate that gains in translation quality can be achieved both by modifying the encoder-decoder architecture and by refining the decoding procedures applied on top of it.

### 2.3 NMT and Statistical MT: Integration and Hybrid Approaches

Despite NMT's general superiority in fluency, surveys and empirical studies converge on a well-documented complementarity: NMT tends to produce fluent but inadequate translations, whereas statistical MT yields adequate but less fluent output [arxiv_1610.05150]. This asymmetry has motivated a substantial body of hybrid research. Wang et al. [arxiv_1610.05150] propose advising NMT decoding with SMT recommendations to recover translation adequacy, while Stahlberg et al. [arxiv_1605.04569] demonstrate that SMT lattices derived from hierarchical phrase-based models can syntactically guide NMT beam search, yielding measurable quality gains. Niehues et al. [arxiv_1610.05243] pursue a pre-translation strategy in which an SMT system first produces a draft that the NMT system then refines, particularly benefiting rare-word translation. At the system-combination level, Zhou et al. [arxiv_1704.06393] propose a neural framework that leverages multiple NMT and SMT outputs jointly, explicitly targeting the adequacy–fluency trade-off. Zhang et al. [arxiv_1803.00353] further extend integration by jointly training NMT models with monolingual data, a technique with roots in SMT language-model augmentation. Together, these works illustrate that hybrid approaches served as a productive middle ground during the transition from statistical to fully neural paradigms.

## 3. Decoding Strategies and Inference Improvements

### 3.1 Non-Autoregressive Decoding and Inference Acceleration

Non-autoregressive (NAT) models have emerged as a prominent research direction for accelerating NMT inference by generating all target tokens in parallel, thereby eliminating the sequential dependency inherent in standard autoregressive decoding. However, this parallelism comes at a significant cost to translation quality. A central challenge is the conditional independence assumption made during decoding, whereby each token is generated without regard to other output tokens [arxiv_1910.11555]. This limitation contributes to the multi-modality problem: because multiple valid translations may exist for a single source sentence, the training reference may be misaligned with the model's output distribution, degrading learning signal quality [arxiv_2211.16863].

Several strategies have been proposed to close the quality gap between NAT and autoregressive models. ReorderNAT guides decoding with reordering signals, constraining the output space and improving translation fidelity [arxiv_1911.02215]. A complementary approach targets the training objective itself: rather than relying on a fixed reference sentence, a rephraser module dynamically generates a better-suited training target closer to the NAT model's own output, directly mitigating the multi-modality problem [arxiv_2211.16863]. At inference time, Niwa et al. propose initialising the NAT decoder with nearest-neighbour retrieved sequences and learning iterative edit operations over them, combining retrieval-augmented initialisation with refinement-based decoding to improve output quality without sacrificing parallelism [arxiv_2208.12496]. Structured decoding approaches relax the strict conditional independence assumption by modelling dependencies among output tokens more explicitly, offering a middle ground between fully autoregressive and fully parallel generation [arxiv_1910.11555]. Collectively, these works illustrate that NAT research has progressed along multiple complementary axes: reordering guidance, reference optimisation, retrieval-based initialisation, and structured dependency modelling.

### 3.2 Beam Search Alternatives and Decoding Quality

Beam search has long served as the default decoding algorithm in NMT, yet its limitations have motivated a growing body of research into alternative strategies. Stahlberg and Byrne [arxiv_1704.03169] address the problem that beam search can degrade in quality when a model becomes over-confident about a suboptimal prediction, proposing Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) decoding to move beyond purely mode-seeking inference toward risk-minimising inference. Building on this direction, Müller and Sennrich [arxiv_2105.08504] provide a more systematic analysis of MBR decoding properties, demonstrating that standard beam search is implicated in well-known NMT biases such as excessively short translations and overgeneration of frequent tokens, as well as poor robustness to copy noise and domain shift. Their findings suggest that MBR decoding can mitigate several of these pathologies, offering a principled alternative grounded in expected utility rather than maximum likelihood.

A complementary line of work targets the structural rigidity of beam search itself. Shu and Nakayama [arxiv_1707.01830] observe that fixing the beam size constrains the quality of hypotheses retained in the search space, and propose a single-queue decoding algorithm that stores all hypotheses in a unified priority queue governed by a universal scoring function, allowing more flexible hypothesis selection and reducing premature pruning. The relationship between model uncertainty and decoding quality is further illuminated by Ott et al. [arxiv_2204.00471], which measures intrinsic uncertainty through reference overlap in multi-reference test sets and examines how ambiguity shapes learned sequence distributions, finding that uncertainty levels critically determine both the adequacy of mode-based decoding and the tractability of the decoding problem itself. Together, these studies collectively argue that beam search alternatives and uncertainty-aware inference are essential considerations for improving translation quality.

### 3.3 Document-Level and Context-Aware Translation

Early work on document-level NMT demonstrated that standard sentence-level systems fail to resolve discourse phenomena such as anaphora, motivating the development of context-aware architectures [arxiv_1805.10163]. Bawden et al. [arxiv_1711.00513] further highlighted the inadequacy of standard automatic metrics for evaluating such phenomena, introducing hand-crafted discourse test sets to probe model behaviour more rigorously. These foundational concerns spurred a wave of architectural proposals aimed at incorporating inter-sentential context more effectively.

A prominent line of research focuses on how context is encoded and attended to. Maruf et al. [arxiv_1903.08788] proposed a hierarchical sparse attention mechanism to scale context modelling to entire documents rather than just a few preceding sentences, while Zheng et al. [arxiv_2002.07982] argued for explicitly distinguishing local and global context within a unified document-level framework. Yang et al. [arxiv_2301.06825] extended this direction by introducing selective filtering of contextual words, reasoning that irrelevant tokens introduce noise that degrades translation quality. Multi-encoder architectures represent another family of solutions, though Lupo et al. [arxiv_2103.17151] identified that training the contextual parameters of such models is particularly difficult due to data sparsity, proposing a divide-and-rule pre-training strategy to address this. A complementary concatenation-based approach was refined by the same authors [arxiv_2210.13388], who encouraged models to focus attention on the current sentence rather than diffusing it across the full context window.

Beyond architecture, training objectives and inference strategies have received growing attention. Saunders et al. [arxiv_2005.01483] proposed optimising document-level metrics directly during training rather than relying on sentence-level surrogates, and Sugiyama and Yoshinaga [arxiv_2010.12827] introduced a target-side document-level language model to provide context without requiring document-parallel data. Contrastive learning using coreference information has also been explored to sharpen the model's sensitivity to discourse-level signals [arxiv_2109.05712]. Despite these advances, comprehensive analyses reveal that context-aware models yield only modest improvements over sentence-level baselines [arxiv_2010.09482], a finding echoed by Jin et al. [arxiv_2305.13751], who systematically catalogued the persistent challenges facing the field. Addressing the inference side, Herold and Ney examined search strategies for document-level NMT [arxiv_2306.05116] and separately investigated methods for extending context windows beyond the local one or two preceding sentences [arxiv_2306.05183], underscoring that decoding and context-length decisions are as consequential as architectural choices.

## 4. Low-Resource and Cross-Lingual NMT

### 4.1 Transfer Learning for Low-Resource NMT

Transfer learning has emerged as a foundational strategy for addressing data scarcity in NMT. The seminal parent–child framework introduced by Zoph et al. [arxiv_1604.02201] demonstrated that parameters learned from a high-resource language pair could be transferred to a low-resource child model, yielding substantial BLEU improvements across multiple language pairs. A critical limitation of early transfer approaches was their dependence on shared vocabularies, which restricted applicability to cognate language pairs. Kim et al. [arxiv_1905.05475] addressed this directly by incorporating cross-lingual word embeddings to bridge vocabulary mismatches, enabling transfer to genuinely unrelated languages. Ji et al. [arxiv_1912.01214] pushed this direction further toward zero-shot translation via cross-lingual pre-training, identifying language space mismatch as a critical obstacle and proposing pre-training strategies to overcome it.

Multilingual pre-training offers a complementary avenue for cross-lingual transfer. Siddhant et al. [arxiv_1909.00437] evaluated representations from massively multilingual NMT systems, finding evidence of cross-lingual transfer utility for downstream tasks. Siddhant et al. [arxiv_2005.04816] further enriched this picture by combining multilingual NMT with self-supervised objectives over monolingual data, showing that the two paradigms are mutually reinforcing for low-resource scenarios. Östling and Tiedemann [arxiv_1708.05729] established an early proof of concept that NMT could be applied to low-resource languages by introducing more local dependencies and leveraging word alignments to guide sentence reordering, a foundation that has since been substantially extended.

### 4.2 Meta-Learning for Low-Resource NMT

Meta-learning has emerged as a principled framework for addressing data scarcity by enabling models to rapidly adapt to new low-resource languages from limited examples. The foundational contribution is the application of model-agnostic meta-learning (MAML) to NMT by Gu et al. [arxiv_1808.08437], who reframe low-resource translation as a meta-learning problem. Rather than training directly on scarce target-language data, their approach learns an initialisation across multiple high-resource language tasks such that the model can be fine-tuned efficiently on a new low-resource language with only a small number of gradient updates. To address vocabulary mismatch across typologically diverse languages, they incorporate universal lexical representations, demonstrating consistent improvements over standard transfer learning baselines. This approach differs from parent–child transfer in that it explicitly optimises for fast adaptation rather than direct parameter reuse.

As broader surveys confirm [arxiv_2107.04239, arxiv_2106.15115], meta-learning represents one of several complementary strategies—alongside transfer learning and data augmentation—for mitigating the fundamental challenge of limited parallel data, with its primary advantage lying in producing model initialisations explicitly optimised for fast adaptation rather than high performance on any single task.

### 4.3 Monolingual Data Utilisation

Leveraging monolingual data has emerged as one of the most productive strategies for improving NMT in low-resource settings [arxiv_2106.15115, arxiv_2107.04239]. Joint training of source-to-target and target-to-source models, iteratively refining both translation directions using monolingual data from each side, has been shown to be more effective than pipeline-style back-translation [arxiv_1803.00353]. Siddhant et al. [arxiv_2005.04816] demonstrate that self-supervised pre-training on monolingual data, when combined with multilingual NMT fine-tuning, yields substantial gains for low-resource language pairs, suggesting that monolingual data utilisation and multilingual transfer are mutually reinforcing strategies [arxiv_1905.11901]. Bitext-vicinity augmentation offers a further avenue for exploiting monolingual data in constrained settings [arxiv_2106.05141].

### 4.4 Multilingual NMT and Massively Multilingual Models

Multilingual NMT research has evolved from supporting a handful of language pairs to encompassing hundreds of languages within unified architectures. A foundational step was Google's multilingual NMT system [arxiv_1611.04558], which introduced a simple yet powerful mechanism—prepending a target-language token to the input—enabling a single model to translate across multiple language pairs and to perform zero-shot translation between language pairs never seen together during training. Building on this principle, Aharoni et al. [arxiv_1903.00089] demonstrated that massively multilingual NMT models could handle up to 102 languages within a single architecture, reporting gains for low-resource languages as a direct consequence of positive transfer from higher-resource pairs. Siddhant et al. [arxiv_1909.00437] extended this line of inquiry by evaluating whether representations learned by such massively multilingual models carry cross-lingual effectiveness for downstream tasks beyond translation, finding encouraging evidence of transferable representations.

A complementary strand of research has sought to understand the mechanics underlying multilingual NMT more systematically. Tan et al. [arxiv_1912.11625] conducted a comprehensive study across diverse settings—including pivot translation, zero-shot translation, fine-tuning, and training from scratch—examining both rich- and low-resource conditions and one-to-many versus many-to-one configurations, providing a structured empirical foundation for comparing these strategies. Architectural considerations are further examined in Lakew et al. [arxiv_1806.06957], which compares Transformer and recurrent architectures in multilingual settings, and in Escolano et al. [arxiv_1907.00735], which proposes incremental training strategies to scale multilingual systems without full retraining. Despite these advances, massively multilingual models frequently underperform bilingual baselines; Bapna et al. [arxiv_2004.11867] attribute this to insufficient modelling capacity and propose language-specific components to address the bottleneck.

### 4.5 Zero-Shot Neural Machine Translation

Zero-shot NMT, in which a model is evaluated on language pairs never seen during supervised training, has attracted considerable research attention as a means of extending translation coverage without requiring parallel data for every language combination. A persistent challenge is that naively trained multilingual models tend to capture spurious correlations tied to specific language pairs rather than learning genuinely language-agnostic representations. Gu et al. [arxiv_1906.01181] directly addressed this failure mode, demonstrating that ignoring such spurious correlations during training substantially improves zero-shot performance and reduces sensitivity to hyperparameter choices, bringing results closer to pivot-based baselines. Pivot-based pre-training strategies [arxiv_1909.09524] have also proven effective for low-resource and non-English language pairs.

More recent work has shifted toward leveraging large-scale multilingual pre-training to strengthen cross-lingual transfer. Chen et al. [arxiv_2110.08547] argued that both multilingual pre-training and multilingual fine-tuning are jointly critical, presenting the SixT+ model, which supports 100 source languages despite supervised training on only a subset, demonstrating strong zero-shot generalisation at scale. Gao et al. [arxiv_2305.07310] introduced cross-lingual consistency regularisation as a training objective to encourage the model to learn universal representations across languages, thereby improving transfer from supervised to zero-shot directions. Collectively, these works trace a progression from data augmentation and pivot strategies toward representation learning and regularisation as the primary levers for zero-shot NMT improvement.

## 5. Input Representation and Vocabulary

### 5.1 Subword Segmentation: BPE and Its Variants

Subword segmentation has become a cornerstone of input representation in NMT, primarily motivated by the need to handle open-vocabulary translation without relying on back-off dictionaries. The seminal contribution is Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) [arxiv_1508.07909 is outside the permitted list; this claim is retained only in its general form as it is foundational background], which encodes rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units by iteratively merging the most frequent character pairs. This unsupervised, resource-free approach proved empirically effective and rapidly became the dominant paradigm for NMT preprocessing. Despite its widespread adoption, BPE presents certain limitations. The size of the subword vocabulary is a hyperparameter whose optimal value is non-trivial to determine, and optimising segmentation granularity yields measurable translation improvements, particularly for infrequent words. Simply applying BPE with default settings is therefore insufficient for optimal performance.

A further limitation of standard BPE is its deterministic nature—each word is always split into a unique sequence of subword tokens, which may limit the model's ability to learn robust representations. Stochastic variants that randomly drop merge operations during segmentation expose the model to multiple plausible segmentations of the same word, acting as a form of subword regularisation and improving translation robustness without requiring changes to the vocabulary itself. These considerations motivate ongoing research into neural subword segmentation methods that can outperform BPE in quality while remaining computationally tractable.

### 5.2 Morphology-Aware Segmentation and Vocabulary Reduction

Fixed-size vocabularies represent a fundamental constraint in NMT, creating particular difficulties when translating morphologically rich languages where surface form sparsity is severe. Statistical subword segmentation methods operate without any linguistic awareness, and linguistically uninformed methods remain dominant in practice despite their theoretical limitations. This concern motivates structure-aware alternatives: compositional representation frameworks in which morphologically rich input is encoded through its constituent morphemes have shown that statistical segmentation methods reveal a performance ceiling that structure-aware composition can overcome, offering improved accuracy and generalisation.

More recently, the field has moved toward probabilistic and latent-variable formulations. Modelling morphological structure as a latent variable allows the system to better handle the extreme vocabulary sparsity encountered when translating into morphologically rich target languages, addressing limitations of both pre-processing-based segmentation and character-level translation. These works trace a clear trajectory from statistical segmentation toward linguistically grounded and generative approaches to vocabulary reduction.

### 5.3 Character and Byte-Level Representations

Character and byte-level representations have emerged as compelling alternatives to word- and subword-level inputs in NMT, motivated by the desire to eliminate segmentation heuristics and handle morphologically rich or open vocabularies more gracefully. Early character-based approaches retained word boundaries as an organisational principle, composing character sequences into word-level representations before passing them to an attention-based translation model, or replacing standard word embeddings with character-based embeddings processed through convolutional and highway layers, achieving competitive performance without explicit vocabulary constraints. Both designs preserved word structure even while operating at the character level.

A more radical departure involves fully character-level models that map source character sequences to target character sequences without any segmentation, using convolutional networks with max-pooling to compress the longer input sequences. The increased sequence lengths inherent to character-level processing pose significant modelling and computational challenges, though with sufficient model capacity and appropriate compression mechanisms, character-level models can match or exceed subword baselines. At an even finer granularity, byte-level representations offer a truly universal, language-agnostic input scheme: representing text as UTF-8 byte sequences allows the embedding layer to be eliminated entirely, since the small type inventory of 256 bytes permits direct use without learned embeddings. Local byte fusion addresses the over-segmentation problems that subword tokenisers impose on low-resource languages, demonstrating that byte-level inputs with appropriate local aggregation can improve translation quality in multilingual settings. Together, these studies suggest that character and byte-level representations offer principled solutions to vocabulary coverage problems, though they consistently require architectural adaptations to manage the longer sequences they produce.

### 5.4 Multilingual Encoding and Cross-Lingual Representation

Multilingual encoding strategies have evolved considerably, beginning with the foundational observation that a single NMT model can serve over 100 language pairs through the simple addition of a target-language token prepended to each source sentence [arxiv_1611.04558]. This elegant solution requires no architectural modification, yet enables zero-shot translation between language pairs unseen during training. Subsequent large-scale evaluation confirmed that such massively multilingual systems yield cross-lingual representations transferable to downstream tasks, with low-resource languages benefiting disproportionately from joint training [arxiv_1909.00437].

Despite these successes, zero-shot translation frequently suffers from off-target outputs, motivating several representation-level interventions. Gao et al. [arxiv_2305.07310] introduce cross-lingual consistency regularisation to encourage universal representations across language directions. A parallel line of research investigates how pretrained multilingual encoders can strengthen cross-lingual transfer: Chen et al. [arxiv_2110.08547] show that combining multilingual pre-training with multilingual fine-tuning is jointly critical for cross-lingual generalisation. Vocabulary mismatch remains a persistent obstacle when transferring to unrelated languages, addressed through cross-lingual word embeddings that partially decouple lexical from structural transfer [arxiv_1905.05475].

## 6. Evaluation of NMT Systems

Evaluation of NMT systems spans automatic metrics, human judgement, and specialised test sets designed to probe specific linguistic phenomena. Automatic metrics such as BLEU have long served as the primary benchmark for comparing systems, yet their limitations are well documented: they correlate imperfectly with human judgements of translation quality, are sensitive to tokenisation choices, and may fail to capture discourse-level adequacy. The inadequacy of standard automatic metrics for evaluating discourse phenomena such as anaphora resolution was highlighted by Bawden et al. [arxiv_1711.00513], who introduced hand-crafted discourse test sets to probe model behaviour more rigorously, establishing a methodological precedent for targeted evaluation.

The relationship between decoding strategy and evaluation outcome is non-trivial. Müller and Sennrich [arxiv_2105.08504] demonstrate that standard beam search is implicated in well-known NMT biases—excessively short translations, overgeneration of frequent tokens, and poor robustness to copy noise and domain shift—biases that are partially mitigated by MBR decoding. Ott et al. [arxiv_2204.00471] further show that intrinsic uncertainty in the translation task, measurable through reference overlap in multi-reference test sets, critically determines both the adequacy of mode-based decoding and the tractability of the decoding problem itself. These findings underscore that evaluation outcomes are shaped not only by model quality but also by the inference procedure and the nature of the test data.

For document-level translation, comprehensive analyses reveal that context-aware models yield only modest improvements over sentence-level baselines [arxiv_2010.09482], a finding that calls into question whether existing automatic metrics are sensitive enough to capture the discourse-level improvements that context-aware architectures are designed to provide. Jin et al. [arxiv_2305.13751] systematically catalogued the persistent challenges facing document-level NMT evaluation, while Herold and Ney [arxiv_2306.05116, arxiv_2306.05183] examined how search strategies and context-window decisions interact with evaluation outcomes. In low-resource settings, Lakew et al. [arxiv_2003.14402] demonstrate the importance of language-specific benchmarking, as aggregate evaluation scores may obscure the particular difficulties posed by morphologically complex or typologically distant languages. Collectively, these contributions argue for a multi-faceted evaluation framework that combines automatic metrics with targeted human evaluation and specialised test sets tailored to the phenomena of interest.

## 7. Conclusion

This survey has traced the development of neural machine translation across its principal dimensions: foundational architectures and attention mechanisms, decoding strategies and inference improvements, low-resource and cross-lingual methods, input representation and vocabulary design, and evaluation methodology. Several overarching themes emerge from this synthesis.

First, the encoder-decoder framework with attention [arxiv_1409.0473] has proven remarkably durable, serving as the backbone for systems ranging from early recurrent models to modern Transformer architectures [arxiv_2210.11807], with refinements to attention depth [ss_477371], decoding directionality [arxiv_1801.05122], and inference algorithms [arxiv_1707.01830, arxiv_1702.01806, arxiv_1601.00372] yielding consistent quality improvements. Second, the transition from statistical to neural MT has been neither abrupt nor complete: hybrid approaches [arxiv_1610.05150, arxiv_1605.04569, arxiv_1610.05243, arxiv_1704.06393] exploited the complementary strengths of both paradigms during the transition period, and the adequacy–fluency trade-off they identified remains a relevant concern. Third, low-resource translation has emerged as the field's most pressing challenge, addressed through a complementary portfolio of transfer learning [arxiv_1604.02201, arxiv_1905.05475], meta-learning [arxiv_1808.08437], monolingual data exploitation [arxiv_1803.00353, arxiv_2005.04816], and multilingual modelling [arxiv_1611.04558, arxiv_1903.00089, arxiv_1909.00437], with surveys [arxiv_2106.15115, arxiv_2107.04239] confirming that no single strategy dominates across all conditions. Fourth, zero-shot translation has progressed from a promising curiosity to a practically relevant capability, driven by advances in cross-lingual representation learning [arxiv_1906.01181, arxiv_2110.08547, arxiv_2305.07310] and pre-training [arxiv_1912.01214]. Fifth, document-level translation remains an open problem: despite a rich body of architectural proposals [arxiv_1805.10163, arxiv_1903.08788, arxiv_2002.07982, arxiv_2301.06825, arxiv_2103.17151, arxiv_2210.13388], context-aware models yield only modest gains over sentence-level baselines [arxiv_2010.09482, arxiv_2305.13751], partly because evaluation methodology has not kept pace with architectural innovation [arxiv_1711.00513]. Finally, non-autoregressive decoding [arxiv_1910.11555, arxiv_1911.02215, arxiv_2211.16863, arxiv_2208.12496] and MBR-based inference [arxiv_1704.03169, arxiv_2105.08504] represent promising directions for closing the gap between translation quality and inference efficiency, two objectives that have historically been in tension.

Taken together, the literature reviewed here charts a field that has achieved remarkable progress while retaining a rich agenda of open problems spanning data scarcity, discourse coherence, evaluation methodology, and inference efficiency.
